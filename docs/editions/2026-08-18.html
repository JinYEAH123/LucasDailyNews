U.S. and Iran Talks Fail, Oil Gets a Little More Expensive

The United States and Iran spent 60 days trying to talk instead of fight. That time ran out on Monday, and they still haven't agreed on anything. Since then, oil -- the fuel that powers cars, trucks, and airplanes -- has gotten a little more expensive, and leaders on both sides are saying tougher things.

Why this mattersWhen countries argue over the Strait of Hormuz, it can affect prices all over the world, because so much oil travels through that one narrow passage. If oil costs more, it can make gas for your family's car, and even things shipped by truck or boat, cost a bit more too. It also matters because a fight between two countries can sometimes pull in other countries nearby, making a small argument into a much bigger problem.
Read the whole story

The United States and Iran have been arguing for a long time, and there's already fighting between them. They tried a 60-day plan to talk things out instead. That plan ended Monday with no deal.

U.S. President Donald Trump says his country talks directly with Iran's military leaders. He said Iran wants a deal but won't agree to his conditions. He also said he might declare the Strait of Hormuz -- a narrow strip of ocean where huge numbers of oil ships pass through every day -- to be U.S. territory. He warned another country, Oman, not to get in the way of U.S. ships blocking Iranian oil ships.

An Iranian official told reporters that Iran will now act 'fully offensive,' meaning more ready to attack, since the talks didn't work. By Tuesday, Trump said no talks were happening at all, and a nearby country called the UAE said it had spotted a possible missile threat.

So right now, nobody is talking, both sides sound angrier, and oil already costs a bit more because of the tension.

A Deadline Passes, and the U.S. and Iran Edge Closer to War

A 60-day deadline for the U.S. and Iran to reach a deal ended this Monday with nothing signed. Oil prices ticked up, President Trump talked about declaring a key shipping route U.S. territory, and an Iranian official said the country would go 'fully offensive.' A day later, nobody could even agree on whether talks were still happening.

Why this mattersIf the Strait of Hormuz gets blocked or fought over, gas prices could rise for everyone, everywhere — including in your family's car and in the cost of things shipped by sea. It's also a reminder that wars don't always start with a clear declaration; sometimes they creep closer through threats, confusion, and moves that are hard to take back.
Read the whole story

For 60 days, American and Iranian officials worked under a memorandum — a written agreement to keep talking — meant to end a war between the two countries without a deal being reached. It expired this past Monday with no lasting agreement in place. Almost immediately, oil markets reacted: Brent crude, the price used to set oil costs across Europe and much of the world, rose to $88.85 a barrel, and WTI, the main U.S. benchmark, climbed to $82.42.

The reason oil prices move at all is a narrow strip of water called the Strait of Hormuz, which sits between Iran and Oman. About a fifth of the world's oil passes through it on tankers every single day. If that strait were ever closed or blocked, gas prices could jump almost everywhere, because so much of the world's fuel supply squeezes through this one channel, the way traffic backs up for miles when a single bridge on a busy highway shuts down.

President Trump said Iran wants a deal but won't accept the terms he's offering, and he raised the idea of declaring the Strait of Hormuz U.S. territory — an extraordinary claim, since it borders Iran and Oman, not the United States. He also confirmed something unusual: his administration has a direct backchannel, a private, informal line of communication, with Iran's Revolutionary Guard, the elite military force that answers to Iran's top religious leader rather than its regular government. 'They're good poker players, but they're dying,' Trump said, adding that he has 'no time schedule.' In the same stretch of days, he threatened to bomb Oman — a country that isn't even part of the dispute — if it interfered with the U.S. blockade, a military effort to stop ships from moving in or out of Iranian waters.

Iran did not back down quietly. A senior Iranian official told the news agency Reuters that after talks stalled, the country would shift to a 'fully offensive' military posture and carry out a 'timely and precise' attack. That kind of language, from a government official rather than a random commentator, is taken seriously by intelligence agencies and markets alike, because it suggests a decision has already been made somewhere inside the government.

By the next day, the picture got even murkier. CNN reported Trump saying no talks were actually underway, contradicting the sense of ongoing negotiation from just a day earlier. Meanwhile, officials in the United Arab Emirates said they had detected a 'missile threat.' When world leaders can't even agree publicly on whether diplomacy is happening, it becomes harder for anyone — allies, enemies, or ordinary people trying to plan their lives — to know what happens next.

  • Backchannel — A private, unofficial way for two governments or groups to communicate outside of public negotiations.
  • Blockade — A military action where one side uses ships or force to stop people or goods from moving in or out of an area.

  1. When two countries can't agree, is it better to keep talking patiently or to make tough threats?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Keep talking

    • Talking gives everyone time to calm down and find a solution without anyone getting hurt.
    • Threats can make the other side angrier and less willing to agree to anything.
    • Most people affected by a war never wanted it and would prefer a peaceful answer.

    Show strength

    • Sometimes a country only takes talks seriously if it believes the other side is willing to act.
    • Waiting too long to act can let a dangerous situation get worse.
    • A country has to protect its own ships and people, even if that means sounding tough.

    • A country enforcing a blockade needs every country nearby to respect it, or the whole plan fails.
    • Warning a country ahead of time can actually prevent a bigger conflict later.

    It's unfair

    • Oman isn't fighting in this war, so threatening it punishes a country that just wants to stay out of it.
    • Big threats against a smaller, peaceful country can feel like bullying rather than fair warning.
    • Innocent people in Oman could suffer from a conflict they had no part in starting.

  1. Is making big, unpredictable threats — like claiming a foreign strait as your own territory or threatening to bomb a country not even involved — a smart way to win a negotiation?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes, unpredictability can work

    • Keeping the other side unsure of what happens next can push them toward compromise faster than patient, predictable diplomacy.
    • Bold threats can signal real resolve, making the other side believe you're serious enough to act.
    • Throughout history, leaders who took dramatic, surprising stances sometimes reached deals faster than those who negotiated cautiously.

    No, it's too dangerous

    • Unpredictable threats can be misread as genuine intentions, causing an accidental escalation into real conflict.
    • Threatening an uninvolved country like Oman can destroy the trust needed for that country to help mediate later.

  2. When a country's shipping is being blocked by a much stronger power, should it respond with force to defend its rights, or keep negotiating even though talks have stalled?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Respond with force

    • Allowing your shipping and economy to be strangled without any cost invites even more pressure in the future.
    • Showing a willingness to act might actually bring the stronger side back to the negotiating table with more respect.
    • International law generally treats freedom of navigation as a shared right, so resisting a blockade can be framed as self-defense, not aggression.

    Keep negotiating, avoid force

    • Taking military action against a far stronger power could lead to devastating losses that outweigh any gain.
    • Escalation risks dragging other countries into a wider regional war that would hurt far more people than the original dispute.
    • Rising oil prices and open conflict would damage the weaker country's own economy and civilians more than continued patience would.

Trump Tells the Military: Practice Less With South Korea

President Trump said he told the U.S. military to do fewer training exercises with South Korea. He said it would save money and that he gets along well with North Korea's leader, Kim Jong Un. He announced this just before a big 11-day drill with 18,000 South Korean soldiers began — but that drill happened anyway, because it was too late to stop it.

Why this mattersImagine your school's soccer team suddenly practices less, right when a rival school's team starts inviting your team to hang out more. You might wonder if the friendship is changing. That's a bit like what's happening between the U.S., South Korea, North Korea, and China right now — big countries figuring out who their closest friends really are.
Read the whole story

South Korea and North Korea used to be one country. They split apart after a war ended in 1953, more than 70 years ago. The United States has kept soldiers in South Korea ever since, to help protect it. Every year, American and South Korean soldiers practice fighting together, like a fire drill, but for war. This is called a joint military exercise.

On Sunday, President Trump wrote on his social media site, Truth Social, that he wants fewer of these practices. He said it costs too much money. He also said he has a 'very good relationship' with Kim Jong Un, the leader of North Korea. Just hours later, a big practice drill began anyway — 18,000 South Korean soldiers, more people than fit in a large sports stadium, took part for 11 days.

At the same time, China's top diplomat, Wang Yi, is getting ready to visit South Korea. This will be his first solo visit there in four years. China has long supported North Korea and is one of the countries that signed the deal ending the 1953 war. Some people think China is stepping forward now because the U.S. seems to be stepping back.

So two things are happening at once: the United States is doing less with an old friend, and China is doing more with that same friend. People are watching closely to see what happens next.

Words worth knowing
  • joint military exercise — When soldiers from two countries practice fighting together, like a rehearsal, so they're ready if a real war ever happens.
  • ally — A country that promises to help and protect another country, like a very close friend.

Trump Pulls Back Military Drills With South Korea — And China Steps In

President Trump says he's ordered the Pentagon to shrink joint military exercises with South Korea, pointing to cost savings and his friendly relationship with North Korea's Kim Jong Un. He announced it on his own social media platform just hours before a huge scheduled drill began anyway. Days later, China's top diplomat is set to visit Seoul for the first time in nearly four years.

Why this mattersAlliances like the one between the U.S. and South Korea shape which countries feel safe, which feel threatened, and who gets influence in a region full of major U.S. trading partners and technology suppliers. Decisions made in the White House about far-off military commitments can ripple into things you don't usually connect to foreign policy — from the price of electronics made in South Korea to how stable that part of the world stays for the next decade.
Read the whole story

For more than 70 years, the United States and South Korea have practiced fighting together, in case North Korea ever attacked. On Sunday, Trump announced on Truth Social — the social media platform he owns — that he had told the Pentagon, the massive building outside Washington, D.C. that runs the U.S. military, to significantly cut back these joint drills. He said the exercises cost too much money and that his personal rapport with North Korean leader Kim Jong Un made them less necessary.

The timing was awkward. Just hours after his announcement, the U.S. and South Korea kicked off Ulchi Freedom Shield, an eleven-day exercise involving 18,000 South Korean troops — one of the year's biggest drills. Trump said it went ahead only because it was 'too late to cancel.' That left a strange picture: a president publicly downgrading an alliance commitment while the very exercise he wanted smaller carried on almost exactly as planned.

These drills matter because they're not just practice — they're a signal. Running large-scale exercises every year tells North Korea, and anyone else watching, that the U.S. and South Korea can fight effectively together if they ever need to. Scaling them back, even for budget reasons, can be read as the U.S. easing up on that signal, whether or not that's what Trump intends.

Three days after Trump's announcement, another development landed: South Korea's foreign ministry confirmed that Wang Yi, China's top diplomat, will make an official two-day visit to Seoul starting Wednesday — his first visit there focused solely on South Korea since September 2021. China isn't a neutral bystander in Korean affairs. It remains officially a party to the armistice, the 1953 agreement that stopped the fighting in the Korean War without ever becoming a formal peace treaty, and it's still North Korea's most important economic backer, propping up its economy through trade and aid.

Put the two stories together and a pattern appears: just as Washington signals it might be pulling back part of its commitment to South Korea, Beijing steps forward with a high-profile diplomatic visit. Some see this as China simply filling a gap the U.S. is creating. Others caution against reading too much into the timing, since so far this amounts to one reduced drill and one scheduled visit, not a dramatic shift in the regional order.

Words worth knowing
  • Pentagon — The five-sided building near Washington, D.C. that serves as headquarters for the U.S. Department of Defense, and is often used as shorthand for U.S. military leadership itself.
  • joint military exercise — A practice event where two or more countries' armed forces train together, both to prepare for real conflict and to show potential enemies they can cooperate effectively.
  • armistice — A formal agreement to stop fighting in a war, which is not the same as a peace treaty — North and South Korea are technically still at war under the 1953 armistice.

A TV Network Is Suing the Government

ABC is a big television network that many American families watch. It just did something unusual: it sued a part of the U.S. government. ABC says the government broke an important rule about free speech, after a disagreement over one of its late-night shows.

Why this mattersEven if you don't watch late-night TV, this story is really about a big question: who gets to decide what can be said on television, a TV channel or the government? That question could affect what shows, jokes, and news you get to see someday.
Read the whole story

ABC has a late-night show hosted by a comedian named Jimmy Kimmel. Every night, he tells jokes and talks about the news.

Something happened between ABC and a government group called the FCC. The FCC is the part of the U.S. government that watches over TV and radio stations across the country.

ABC believes the FCC broke a rule called the First Amendment. That rule is part of America's founding rulebook, and it protects people's right to say what they think, even on television. Now ABC wants a judge to decide who is right.

Nobody knows yet exactly what will happen next. But people who care about free speech say this case matters a lot, no matter who wins.

A TV Network Sues the U.S. Government

ABC, one of America's biggest television networks, has sued the FCC — the federal agency that regulates broadcasting — claiming it violated ABC's free-speech rights. The fight grew out of a clash over late-night host Jimmy Kimmel's show. It's rare for a broadcaster to take a federal regulator to court over what can air on television.

Why this mattersYou might not watch much broadcast TV, but the principle here touches everything you do watch: whether a government agency can quietly shape what shows up on your screen just by controlling who gets to broadcast. If a regulator can pressure a huge network like ABC, the same logic could someday reach streaming platforms, news sites, or apps you use every day.
Read the whole story

Here's the concrete part: ABC filed a lawsuit against the FCC. The FCC is the government agency that decides who gets to broadcast on TV and radio, and it enforces rules about what stations can air. ABC says the FCC crossed a line by pressuring the network over Jimmy Kimmel's show, and that this pressure violates the First Amendment — the part of the U.S. Constitution that protects free speech and a free press.

To understand why the FCC has this kind of leverage, picture this: TV networks don't own the airwaves they broadcast on. Airwaves are actually a shared public resource, and networks get a 'broadcast license' — official permission — from the FCC to use a slice of them. That license can be renewed, questioned, or in theory taken away. So if a network worries about angering the agency that controls its license, it might think twice before airing something risky, even if no one ever says 'do this or else' out loud.

The exact spark that set off this particular fight — what the FCC said or did about Kimmel's show — hasn't been fully laid out in public reporting yet. What is clear is that it comes during a broader clash between the Trump administration and major TV networks, several of which have faced complaints, investigations, or pressure over their news and entertainment programming.

Press-freedom advocates, including a guest on the news program Democracy Now, have described this kind of government pressure on broadcasters as part of an 'authoritarian takeover' of the media — meaning they see it as the government trying to control what people can watch and hear. Others would push back hard on that framing, arguing the FCC has always had some role in overseeing broadcast content and that enforcing existing rules isn't the same as censorship. Reasonable people disagree sharply about which reading is closer to the truth here.

Whichever way this lawsuit turns out, lawyers and journalists say it will matter beyond ABC and Kimmel. Courts rarely get asked to decide exactly how much power a government regulator can hold over what a private broadcaster puts on air. However this case ends, it will likely shape how much room future administrations have to lean on TV networks — a precedent that outlasts any one host or one show.

Words worth knowing
  • FCC — The U.S. government agency that regulates television and radio broadcasting, including who gets permission to use the airwaves.
  • First Amendment — The part of the U.S. Constitution that protects freedoms like speech and the press from government interference.
  • broadcast license — Official government permission that allows a TV or radio station to use a specific slice of the public airwaves.
