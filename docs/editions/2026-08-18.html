The features the states are pointing at are ones you have used today. Infinite scroll — the feed that never reaches a bottom, so there is no natural moment to stop. Notifications that arrive just when your attention drifts. Likes, which turn other people's approval into a number you can watch go up. The states' argument is not that these things are strange or new. It is that Meta studied how they affected young users, understood that they were unusually powerful on developing brains, and kept them anyway because engagement is what makes the company money. NPR reported the states summing it up this way: profits won.

The case has a second half that is more technical but easier to prove or disprove. It involves a federal law called COPPA, the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act, which says a company cannot collect personal data from a child under 13 without a parent's permission. Instagram's rules say you must be 13 to have an account. The states allege that Meta knew perfectly well that plenty of younger kids were on the apps anyway, and collected their data regardless. Meta says its apps include safety tools built for teenagers and that it has never designed products to harm anyone.

It matters who is bringing this. An attorney general is a state's top lawyer — an elected official whose job includes suing companies on behalf of the public. Individual families have sued social media companies before, usually one at a time, and those cases are slow and hard to win. Four states arriving together, with subpoena power that lets them demand internal company documents, is a different scale of pressure. Presiding is Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers, who has handled other big technology trials, including Epic Games' fight with Apple.

Here is the part worth keeping in mind through every headline you read for the next two months: at this stage, everything the states have said is an allegation, not an established fact. Opening arguments are each side's best story about the evidence, told before the evidence has been tested. Internal emails can look damning stripped of context and ordinary in it. Meta will get its turn, and its lawyers will argue that correlation between phone use and teenage unhappiness is not the same as Instagram causing it — a point on which genuine researchers genuinely disagree.

What the states want is two things. Money damages, which for a company Meta's size may sting less than it sounds. And court-ordered changes to how the apps are actually built — which, if granted, would be the bigger deal by far, because it would mean a judge, not a product team in Menlo Park, deciding what an app for teenagers is allowed to do. That is why this trial is being watched well beyond the four states involved.

The case is not really about what teenagers post. It is about how the apps are built. The states argue that specific engineering choices — a feed with no bottom, notifications timed to pull you back, a public counter of how many people approved of your photo — were selected and refined because they increased the time young people spent inside the product, and that Meta understood this was a problem for some of those users and shipped the features anyway. In the states' framing, reported by NPR, profits won. That is a claim about intent, and intent is the hardest thing to prove in a courtroom.

There are two separate legal theories running in parallel, and they carry different burdens. One is a consumer-protection argument: that Meta made public statements about safety that did not match what its own researchers and internal documents allegedly showed, which is a form of deception. The other is narrower and more mechanical — an alleged violation of COPPA, the federal Children's Online Privacy Protection Act, which forbids collecting personal data from children under 13 without a parent's consent. Instagram's stated minimum age is 13, but the states say Meta knew younger children were on the platform in large numbers and profited from their data regardless.

Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers is presiding in the Northern District of California. She is not new to this kind of defendant; she handled the Epic Games case against Apple, another fight over whether a platform's design choices were business strategy or something the law should override. Here the states are asking for two things: money damages, and injunctive relief — a court order changing how the products are actually engineered. The second is by far the bigger deal. Damages are a cost a company that size can absorb. A judge telling Meta what its feed may and may not do would reset the rules for an industry.

Meta's defence, in outline, is that the underlying science is contested and the harms are not its doing. Researchers genuinely disagree about how much social media causes teenage anxiety and depression as opposed to correlating with it; the same years brought smartphones, the pandemic, and a great deal else. Meta also argues it has spent years building teen accounts with default privacy settings, time limits and parental controls, and that a company cannot be liable for every bad outcome that happens to occur on a service used by billions of people.

Two things will decide a lot of this: internal documents and jury instinct. Discovery — the pre-trial process in which each side must hand over its records — has already produced years of Meta's own research, emails and slide decks, and the states will read the most damaging lines aloud. Meta will argue those lines are fragments of an honest internal debate rather than a confession. Juries tend to respond to documents in which employees appear to say one thing privately and another publicly, which is why 'what did the company know, and when' matters more here than any single statistic about screen time.

This trial is also one node in something much larger. Dozens of other states and hundreds of school districts have sued Meta and other platforms on similar grounds, and legislatures in the US, the EU and Australia have been writing age-verification and design rules of their own. A verdict in Oakland will not settle whether Instagram is bad for you. It will settle something narrower and more consequential: whether the way an app is designed can be treated by the law as a defective product, in the same way a badly designed car part can be.

China's statistics bureau reported that factory output grew 4.5% in July compared with a year earlier — slower than June's 5.3% and below what economists had forecast. Retail sales barely moved, up around 0.6%, and urban unemployment rose to 5.2%. Why this matters为什么重要China is the world's second-largest economy and the biggest buyer of raw materials, so what its households do — or do not — spend shows up in commodity prices, factory orders and job markets far from Beijing. The numbers themselves came out on Monday afternoon Beijing time, and they were unusually consistent with one another. Industrial output — how much the country's factories actually produced — grew 4.5% year-on-year, against the 4.8% a Reuters poll of economists had expected. Fixed-asset investment, which covers spending on things like factories, machinery, roads and apartment blocks, fell further. The manufacturing purchasing managers' index stayed below 50, the dividing line between an expanding and a shrinking sector. When output, investment, consumption and employment all move the wrong way in the same month, economists stop calling it noise.

Bloomberg's framing captured what made this release different from a routine soft patch: the weakness is 'across the board,' and it is happening while China builds one of the most aggressive artificial-intelligence and advanced-manufacturing capacities in the world. Enormous sums are flowing into chips, data centres, electric vehicles and batteries. But those industries employ relatively few people for the capital they absorb, and a data centre does not shop, eat out or renovate a home. The result is what analysts increasingly describe as a two-track economy: a technological frontier moving fast, and a household economy that has not recovered its confidence since the property market turned.

Retail sales are the clearest symptom. Growth of 0.6% means Chinese households, in aggregate, spent almost exactly what they spent a year ago. Behind that is a simple mechanism: for most Chinese families, the apartment is the savings account. Property prices have fallen for years, so people feel poorer even if their salary has not changed, and they respond by saving rather than spending. Weak spending means weak revenue for shops and services, which means cautious hiring, which pushes unemployment up — and rising unemployment makes households save more still. Breaking that loop is the central policy problem, and it is not one that faster chip production solves.

The official growth target for the year is 4.5–5.0%. The first quarter came in at 4.7%, comfortably inside the range, but a summer this weak makes the upper half of the band look out of reach and the lower edge look like work. Targets in China are not merely forecasts; provincial officials are evaluated against them, which is exactly why economists watch the gap between the target and the data so closely. The obvious response is stimulus aimed at consumers — trade-in subsidies, childcare support, stronger pensions and health insurance so families feel safe spending. Beijing has resisted large direct handouts, partly on ideological grounds and partly out of concern about debt.

One small detail drew disproportionate attention. The bureau published the figures at 3 p.m. Beijing time rather than the usual 10 a.m. Nothing in the release explained the change, and it may be entirely administrative. But statistical agencies everywhere guard predictable release schedules precisely because predictability is what makes the numbers credible; markets notice when a routine breaks. Some outside economists already treat Chinese official statistics with caution, cross-checking them against electricity use, freight volumes, satellite imagery and corporate earnings. Others point out that those alternative measures have their own flaws, and that the July figures were bad — which is not what a government massaging its books would publish.

How much this matters beyond China's borders is a genuine argument. A slower China buys less Australian iron ore, fewer German machine tools and less Brazilian soy, and it exports deflation — cheap goods that hold prices down in other countries, welcome for shoppers and painful for competing manufacturers. Some analysts read the data as a temporary trough before stimulus arrives in the autumn. Others read it as the visible shape of a longer transition: an economy deliberately shifting from property and construction toward technology, paying the cost of that shift now, in jobs and confidence, in the hope of the payoff later.

    Content must stay off-limits内容不该被审

    • Once officials can weigh programming, the licence becomes a lever that whoever holds power can pull against critics.
    • Stations will start guessing what the current government wants and quietly avoid it, and nobody outside will ever see the shows that were never made.
    • The First Amendment exists precisely because governments are worst at judging speech aimed at themselves.
    • 一旦官员可以评判节目内容，执照就变成了掌权者对付批评者的一根杠杆。
    • 电视台会开始猜测现任政府想要什么并悄悄避开，而外人永远看不到那些从未被做出来的节目。
    • 《第一修正案》之所以存在，正是因为政府最不擅长评判针对自己的言论。

    Programming is part of the deal节目本就是条件之一

    • The airwaves are a free public resource, and the law that grants them has demanded service 'in the public interest' from the start.
    • A regulator who may never look at what is broadcast cannot enforce any obligation at all — the requirement becomes decoration.
    • Refusing to examine content would also protect a station that broadcast deliberate lies or shut whole communities out of coverage.
    • 电波是免费使用的公共资源，而授予它的法律从一开始就要求「为公共利益服务」。
    • 一个永远不能看播出内容的监管者，根本无法执行任何义务，这项要求就成了装饰。
    • 完全不看内容，同样会保护那些故意播假消息、或把整个群体排除在报道之外的电视台。

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it想不出来？两方各有说法

    Look for the real motive应当追查真实动机

    • Any official smart enough to retaliate is smart enough to write down a respectable reason, so stated reasons alone are easy to fake.
    • Courts already examine intent in other areas of law, such as discrimination cases, using evidence like emails and public statements.
    • If motive can never be questioned, the constitutional protection against retaliation exists on paper and nowhere else.
    • 聪明到会搞报复的官员，也聪明到会写下一个体面的理由，所以只看写出来的理由太容易伪装。
    • 法院在其他法律领域（比如歧视案件）本来就会审查意图，依据是邮件、公开表态等证据。
    • 如果动机永远不能被质疑，那么宪法对报复的保护就只存在于纸面上。

    • Rules that turn on provable conduct rather than inferred intent are ones a regulator and a company can both understand in advance.
    • 法官凭时间点和语气去猜隐藏动机，有时会猜错，而错判很难挽回。

    • 以可证明的行为而非推测的意图为标准，监管者和企业事先都能明白规则是什么。
