Imagine two people in an argument who agree to a rule: "We have 60 days to work this out." That is roughly what the United States and Iran did in June, when they signed a memorandum of understanding — a written promise to try for a deal that would end their conflict.

Today, August 17, that 60-day clock ran out. There is no deal. President Trump said he is not interested in extending the deadline, which means the fighting is likely to continue.

Then things got tenser. Trump warned that the U.S. might strike Oman — a small country on the Arabian Peninsula that is normally a friend of the United States — if it "gets in the way" in the Strait of Hormuz.

Here is why that strait matters so much. Look at a map of the Persian Gulf: the only way out to the open ocean is a narrow gap of water between Iran and Oman. It is about 33 km wide at its tightest point — roughly the distance from downtown Vancouver to Abbotsford. Around a fifth of all the oil the world uses squeezes through that gap on giant tankers.

So when countries start threatening each other there, traders around the world get nervous, and the price of oil goes up. Today Brent crude — one of the main types of oil people buy and sell — climbed to about $89 a barrel. The Dow Jones and the S&P 500 (two big scoreboards for American company stocks) each dropped about half a percent.

今天，8月17日，这60天到期了。协议没谈成。特朗普总统表示他不打算延长期限，这意味着战事很可能继续下去。

A memorandum of understanding is not a treaty. It commits the signatories to a process, not to an outcome — which is precisely why deadlines attached to one tend to lapse quietly. June's document gave both governments 60 days to turn a pause in the fighting into something durable.

That deadline expired today with no agreement. Trump said he has no interest in extending it, removing the one procedural device that had been keeping negotiators in the room at all.

The sharper development was aimed at Oman. Muscat has spent decades as the region's designated intermediary — the quiet channel through which Washington and Tehran have talked when direct contact was impossible. Threatening it is not merely aggressive; it degrades the infrastructure any future negotiation would need.

The Strait of Hormuz is the lever everyone is reaching for. At its narrowest it is about 33 kilometres across, with the shipping lanes narrower still, and roughly a fifth of global oil consumption transits it. Iran almost certainly cannot close it for long — it lacks the capacity, and a closure would strangle its own exports — but it can make insurance underwriters reprice the risk, and that alone moves markets.

It did today. Brent crude reached about $89 a barrel, while the Dow and the S&P 500 each fell around half a percent. That is a measured response rather than a panic, which tells you traders are pricing elevated risk rather than an imminent closure.

The strategic question is what leverage remains. Sanctions are largely exhausted, the ceasefire framework has lapsed, and the intermediary has been publicly threatened. What is left is either direct escalation or an eventual return to talks on worse terms than were available in June.

霍尔木兹海峡是所有人都在伸手去够的那根杠杆。最窄处约33公里，航道更窄，全球石油消费量的约五分之一从这里通过。伊朗几乎肯定无法长期封锁它——它没有这个能力，而且封锁会先勒死自己的出口——但它能让保险承保人重新为风险定价，仅此一项就足以撬动市场。

今天就撬动了。布伦特原油触及约每桶89美元，道琼斯指数和标普500各跌约0.5%。这是有分寸的反应，而不是恐慌，说明交易员定价的是风险抬升，而不是封锁在即。
