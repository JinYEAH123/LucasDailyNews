Three Scientists Win Nobel Prize for Turning Brain Cells On and Off With Light

Three scientists just won the world's biggest science prize for figuring out how to control tiny brain cells using beams of light. They borrowed a trick from pond algae to do it. Their work now helps doctors understand diseases like Parkinson's and epilepsy.

Why this mattersYour brain has billions of tiny cells that control everything you do, from wiggling your toes to remembering your best friend's birthday. This discovery gives scientists a way to find out exactly which brain cells do which job, which could someday help treat diseases that affect people you might know. It shows how a strange fact about pond algae turned into a tool that helps human health.
Read the whole story

Some tiny algae that live in ponds have a special trick. They have proteins in them that react to light, kind of like how your eyes react to light, except these proteins can switch a cell on or off.

Two scientists named Georg Nagel and Peter Hegemann found these light-sensitive proteins in algae. Another scientist, Karl Deisseroth, figured out how to put these proteins into brain cells of animals being studied in labs.

Once the proteins were inside, scientists could shine a light on a single brain cell and switch it on, almost like flipping a light switch for one bulb in a huge house full of lights. This let them find out exactly which brain cells cause which actions or feelings, something nobody could do precisely before.

This method is now used in science labs all over the world. It has helped scientists understand brain diseases better, and some researchers hope it could one day help blind people see again by controlling cells in the eye with light.

Words worth knowing
  • optogenetics — A lab method where scientists use light to turn specific brain cells on or off.
  • neuron — A single brain cell that sends messages to other cells, like a tiny wire carrying signals.

Nobel Prize Honours the Scientists Who Taught Us to Switch Brain Cells On and Off With Light

On Monday, the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine went to three scientists — Karl Deisseroth in the US, and Georg Nagel and Peter Hegemann in Germany — for inventing a technique called optogenetics. It lets researchers switch individual brain cells on or off using beams of light, something that sounds like science fiction but is now a routine tool in neuroscience labs worldwide.

Why this mattersThis isn't a treatment you or your family will get next year, but it's the kind of discovery that eventually becomes one — many current medicines started as a strange lab finding decades before anyone could use them in a hospital. It also shows how prizes like the Nobel often reward the tool that made other breakthroughs possible, rather than a single dramatic cure.
Read the whole story

Every October, Sweden's Karolinska Institute, a medical university that has decided the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine since it began in 1901, announces who has most changed how we understand the human body. This year's winners earned it for something that started as a curiosity about pond algae and ended up reshaping how scientists study the brain.

The discovery traces back to proteins found in light-sensitive algae — proteins that physically change shape when hit by light, acting like tiny switches. Nagel and Hegemann identified these proteins and worked out how they functioned. On their own, they were a biological curiosity: useful for explaining how algae swim toward sunlight, not obviously useful for anything else.

Deisseroth's contribution was turning that curiosity into a tool. He found ways to insert the genetic instructions for these light-sensitive proteins into specific neurons — the cells that carry electrical signals through the brain and nervous system — so that shining a light on them would switch those exact cells on or off, instantly and reversibly. Before this, researchers studying the brain had to rely on blunter methods: removing tissue, using drugs that spread broadly, or inserting electrodes that disturb the area around them. Optogenetics let them isolate single types of neuron and ask, directly, what happens to behaviour when just those cells fire.

That precision has driven a wave of findings. Researchers have used the technique to trace which brain circuits produce specific behaviours in animals, to better understand what goes wrong in Parkinson's disease (a condition where certain brain cells that control movement gradually stop working) and in epilepsy (where clusters of neurons misfire together, causing seizures). It is also being tested as a possible way to partially restore vision in people with certain forms of blindness, by making remaining retinal cells light-sensitive again.

None of this year's prize involves scientific controversy — the three scientists' roles in the discovery are well documented and not disputed among researchers. The genuine debate, voiced regularly in science commentary, is about pace: optogenetics has transformed laboratory research in animals, but turning it into an approved treatment doctors can prescribe to patients is a much longer and less certain road, and nobody can say with confidence how many years that will take.

A Small Mountain Town Asks Big Oil Companies to Pay for Climate Change

The town of Boulder, Colorado is suing two giant oil companies, ExxonMobil and Suncor Energy. Boulder says the companies's oil and gas caused climate change, and that fixing the damage costs money. The case has gone all the way to the U.S. Supreme Court, the highest court in the country, where nine judges just started arguing about it.

Why this mattersIf the Supreme Court says yes, cities and towns everywhere could start suing energy companies for climate damage, like floods, fires, and heat waves. If they say no, those lawsuits mostly disappear, and any changes to energy and climate rules would have to come from Congress instead. Either way, it could shape how America deals with a warming planet for years to come.
Read the whole story

Boulder is a city at the bottom of tall mountains in Colorado. As the world gets hotter, those mountains have less snow, and nearby forests catch fire more easily. Boulder says it has already spent a lot of money dealing with these problems, like fighting fires and fixing roads after floods.

Boulder wants ExxonMobil and Suncor, two huge oil companies, to help pay those costs. The city says the oil these companies sold for decades is part of why the planet is warming.

The oil companies say this isn't fair. They argue that climate change is a problem for the whole country to solve with one set of rules, not something a single city can decide in its own courtroom. They warn that if Boulder wins, they could owe billions of dollars — and so could other companies, in dozens of similar lawsuits waiting across the country.

The Supreme Court isn't deciding if climate change is real this time. They're only deciding something narrower: can a city even bring this kind of lawsuit in a state court? Whatever they decide will affect more than two dozen similar cases waiting for an answer.

The Supreme Court Just Heard a Case That Could Decide Whether Oil Companies Pay for Climate Damage

Boulder, Colorado, wants ExxonMobil and Suncor to pay for the costs of climate change the city says their products caused. The Supreme Court isn't deciding if climate change is real — it's deciding something narrower but just as consequential: whether lawsuits like this can even be heard in state court. More than two dozen similar cases across the country are waiting on the answer.

Why this mattersThis case will likely determine whether local governments can use lawsuits to make oil companies pay for climate-related costs, or whether that fight has to happen entirely through national laws and elections instead. Whatever the Supreme Court decides will shape who pays for the costs of a warming planet — companies, taxpayers, or some mix of both — for years to come.
Read the whole story

Boulder's lawsuit makes a simple claim: oil companies knew for decades that burning fossil fuels warms the planet, kept selling the product anyway, and the city has had to spend real money dealing with the consequences — wildfire prevention, heat emergency plans, infrastructure built to handle more extreme weather. Boulder wants ExxonMobil and Suncor Energy to help cover those costs, the same way a court might order a company to pay for damage caused by a product it sold while hiding known risks.

The oil companies' defense isn't that climate change isn't happening. It's that this kind of claim doesn't belong in a state courtroom at all. Their argument is that greenhouse gas emissions cross state and national borders, so regulating them is inherently a job for the federal government and international agreements — not something a single city's lawsuit in Colorado should be able to control by threatening a Texas-based company with a massive bill. If every city and state could sue separately over the same global problem, the companies argue, the result wouldn't be fair or consistent; it would be chaos, with different courts reaching different verdicts about the same emissions.

This is why the case matters so much beyond Boulder. More than two dozen other cities and counties have filed nearly identical lawsuits, and all of them are on hold, waiting to see what the Supreme Court decides. If the justices let Boulder's case go forward, that likely opens the floodgates: those other lawsuits proceed, and energy companies could face damages verdicts running into the billions of dollars across multiple jurisdictions. If the justices side with the oil companies, it likely closes the door on this entire legal strategy — not just for Boulder, but for nearly every city attempting the same approach.

It's worth being precise about what's actually being argued in front of the justices, because it isn't what it might look like from the headlines. The Court is not weighing in on whether climate change is real or whether it's caused by human activity — both sides in this case accept the basic science. The dispute is a narrower, more technical legal question: can a damages lawsuit like this be heard in state court, or does it belong exclusively in federal court, or nowhere at all because it's really a matter for Congress and regulatory agencies to decide? Lower courts around the country have disagreed on this exact question, which is part of why the Supreme Court agreed to take the case.

A ruling isn't expected immediately. The Supreme Court's term runs into next summer, and a decision of this size — one that could reshape how climate liability works across the entire country — will likely take months of deliberation. Until then, Boulder's case and the dozens like it stay frozen in place.

  • damages — Money a court orders one party to pay another to cover the cost or harm they caused.
  • federal matter — An issue the oil companies argue should be decided by national government and courts, not by individual states or cities acting separately.

America Added Far Fewer Jobs Than Expected Last Month

In September, the United States added only 29,000 new jobs. Experts had guessed about 90,000 jobs would be added. The share of people without jobs who want one went up too, from 4.1% to 4.2%. This was the last big jobs report before an important election, so people are arguing about what it means.

Why this mattersIf fewer jobs are being created, it can be harder for people, maybe someone in your own family, to find work or get a raise. It can also change how expensive it is to borrow money for things like a car, which affects many families' budgets.
Read the whole story

Imagine your teacher expected 90 new kids to join your school this year, but only 29 showed up. That is roughly what happened with jobs in America last month. Economists guessed about 90,000 new jobs would appear. Only 29,000 did.

At the same time, the unemployment rate ticked up. That is the share of people who want a job but do not have one. It went from 4.1% to 4.2%, a small but noticeable rise.

There was more surprising news. Back in July, the government first said a small number of jobs were added. Now, after checking again, they say about 10,000 jobs were actually lost that month. So the job market has been weaker for longer than anyone first thought.

This matters because a group called the Federal Reserve watches numbers like these closely. They decide how expensive it is to borrow money, like for a car or a house. Weak job numbers might push them to make borrowing cheaper, which could ripple through the whole economy.

U.S. Hiring Almost Stalls, and the Last Jobs Report Before the Midterms Lands With a Thud

The U.S. economy added only 29,000 jobs in September — a fraction of the roughly 90,000 economists expected — and the unemployment rate ticked up to 4.2%. It's the final official jobs report before the midterm elections, and buried in it was an even bigger surprise: July's job gains, first reported as modest growth, have now been revised into an actual loss of about 10,000 jobs.

Why this mattersIf you have a parent, older sibling, or neighbor who is job-hunting, this report is the kind of statistic that shapes whether companies are confident enough to hire this winter. It also affects interest rates, which touch everyday things like car loans, credit card costs, and how expensive it is for a family to buy a house.
Read the whole story

Every month, the U.S. government's Bureau of Labor Statistics surveys tens of thousands of employers and households to estimate how many jobs the economy added or lost, and what share of people looking for work couldn't find any. September's report showed hiring had nearly ground to a halt: 29,000 new jobs, far below forecasts, while the unemployment rate rose from 4.1% to 4.2%. That's not a dramatic jump — it still means roughly 19 out of 20 people who want jobs have them — but it moved in the wrong direction at the wrong moment.

More striking than the September number itself was what happened to July's. Statistical agencies don't get every month's estimate right on the first try; they revise earlier figures as more complete data comes in, usually by small amounts. This time the revision wasn't small: July, originally reported as a modest gain, now shows an outright loss of about 10,000 jobs. That changes the story entirely — instead of a labor market that wobbled once in September, it looks like one that's been losing strength for months, and nobody noticed until now because the first estimates were too rosy.

This report matters well beyond the headline numbers because of what it means for the Federal Reserve, the U.S. central bank that sets interest rates — essentially the cost of borrowing money, for everything from credit cards to business loans to mortgages. When hiring is strong, the Fed often keeps rates higher to prevent the economy from overheating and prices from rising too fast. When hiring weakens and unemployment rises, that combination is historically one of the clearest signals that the Fed should cut rates to make borrowing cheaper and encourage businesses to invest and hire again. This report pushes hard in that direction, and investors are now watching closely for the Fed's next move.

The timing makes this report unusually politically charged. Because it's the last official jobs data released before the midterm elections, it's the final hard number either party gets to point to before voters decide. The party in power will likely argue the economy remains fundamentally sound and that one weak month, even with a revision, doesn't undo years of job growth. The opposition will argue the revision proves the labor market has been quietly weakening for months while officials claimed otherwise, and that voters deserve to know that before they vote.

What nobody disputes is the data itself — it comes from the same government survey both parties normally treat as authoritative. What's contested is what it means: whether it's a genuine warning sign of a slowing economy, a one-off blip, or mainly a political football to be read however each side needs it read heading into an election.

Words worth knowing
  • Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) — The U.S. government agency that surveys employers and households each month to measure jobs and unemployment.
  • Unemployment rate — The share of people actively looking for work who don't currently have a job.
  • Interest rate — The cost of borrowing money, set in part by the Federal Reserve, which rises or falls depending on how the economy is doing.
