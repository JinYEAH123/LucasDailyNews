Judges Ask: Can One Law From the 1970s Let the President Tax Stuff From 80 Countries?

Three judges in New York spent a day listening to arguments about new taxes on things bought from other countries, called tariffs. Small business owners and several U.S. states say the president does not have the right to set these taxes this big. The government says an old law gives him that power.

Why this mattersIf the judges allow these tariffs, things your family buys, like sneakers, toys, or electronics made in other countries, could cost more. If the judges say the tariffs aren't allowed, prices might stay the same, but it also means courts can stop a president's plans, which changes how much power one person has over the whole country's shopping.
Read the whole story

Imagine every toy, shoe, and phone shipped into America from another country gets a little extra charge added on, like a toll on a highway. That extra charge is called a tariff. Companies that bring in the goods pay it, and often pass the cost on to shoppers.

The U.S. government just put new tariffs, between 10 and 12.5 cents on every dollar, on goods from about 80 countries, including China, India, and Japan. It used an old law from the 1970s called Section 301 to do this.

This isn't the first try. Earlier this year, even bigger tariffs were stopped by the Supreme Court, the highest court in the country, because they were made using a different law that the court said didn't allow it. So the government switched to this older law instead.

Now three judges are deciding if this old law really lets the president set tariffs this huge. They asked the government's lawyers tough questions. If the judges say no, trillions of dollars in trade could be affected, that's more money than everyone in a big city could count in a lifetime.

  • Tariff — Extra money added to the price of something bought from another country, kind of like a toll you pay to bring goods across a border.
  • Court of International Trade — A special court where judges decide if trade rules and taxes, like tariffs, follow the law.

A Second Legal Fight Over Trump's Tariffs Lands in Court

On September 30, 2026, three federal judges in Manhattan heard arguments over whether a fresh round of tariffs on goods from about 80 countries is even legal. This is the government's second attempt at broad tariffs this year — the first version was struck down by the Supreme Court for using the wrong law. Small businesses and a group of Democratic-led states say the new version has the same problem; the Trump administration says it is on solid legal ground this time.

Why this mattersIf you've noticed everyday things costing a little more — a phone charger, a pair of sneakers, a bike part — tariffs like these are often part of why, since the cost usually passes from importer to store to shopper. The case also tests whether a president can reshape trade policy largely on his own, which matters for how much say voters and Congress end up having over decisions that hit household budgets.

Imagine you run a small shop that imports kitchen gadgets from Japan and India. Over the past year, the price you pay at the border has jumped by 10 to 12.5 percent on almost everything you bring in — not because your suppliers raised their prices, but because the U.S. government now taxes those goods at the port. That tax is called a tariff, and it's the center of a lawsuit a coalition of small business owners and state governments brought against the Trump administration, arguing the tariffs were never legally authorized in the first place.

This isn't the administration's first attempt at tariffs this broad. Earlier this year, Trump imposed sweeping tariffs using a law called the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA), which lets a president regulate trade during a declared national emergency. The Supreme Court ruled that law doesn't actually give the president power to impose tariffs on this scale — emergency powers, the Court said, aren't a blank check to rewrite trade policy. Rather than drop the tariffs, the administration switched legal justifications, reissuing similar duties under a different, older law: Section 301 of a 1974 trade act, which lets a president respond to foreign trade practices judged unfair to American businesses.

That's what brought everyone to the Court of International Trade in Manhattan on September 30. The judges — three of them, hearing the case together as a panel — reportedly grilled government lawyers hard, asking whether a law meant to address specific unfair practices by specific countries can really justify blanket tariffs of 10 to 12.5 percent on goods from roughly 80 nations, including major trading partners like China, India, and Japan. The plaintiffs argue this is the same overreach dressed in a new legal coat; the administration says Section 301 was written broadly enough to cover exactly this kind of response.

The stakes are enormous and concrete. Trillions of dollars in trade flow through these tariffs. If the judges rule against the administration, prices on a huge range of imported goods — electronics, clothing, car parts, appliances — could drop back down, and the government would need to refund tariffs already collected or abandon plans to keep collecting them. If the tariffs are upheld, they become a durable tool the president can use not just for trade leverage but as a steady source of federal revenue, and a precedent for how far a 50-year-old law can stretch to fit modern disputes.

Underneath the legal argument is a bigger question about how the U.S. government is supposed to work. The Constitution gives Congress — not the president — the power to set taxes and tariffs. Over the decades, Congress has passed laws handing some of that power to presidents for specific situations, like responding to unfair trade or handling emergencies. This case asks whether those older grants of power can be read so broadly that a president ends up with something close to free rein over tariffs, or whether courts need to hold the line and send disputes like this back to Congress.

A ruling isn't expected immediately — these cases usually take weeks or months to decide, and whichever side loses is expected to appeal, possibly all the way back to the Supreme Court. Until then, the tariffs stay in effect, shops keep paying them, and the shop owner from our opening example keeps passing some of that cost on to customers.

Words worth knowing
  • Tariff — A tax a government charges on goods brought in from another country, which is usually paid by the business importing them and often passed on to customers through higher prices.
  • Section 301 — Part of a 1974 U.S. trade law that lets the president impose tariffs in response to foreign trade practices judged unfair to American businesses.
  • IEEPA (International Emergency Economic Powers Act) — A 1977 law allowing the president to regulate trade during a declared national emergency — the law the Supreme Court said does not authorize the broad tariffs the administration first tried to impose.

  1. Should courts read a decades-old trade law broadly enough to allow tariffs this sweeping, or does that stretch the law past what Congress intended?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Read it broadly

    • Congress wrote Section 301 with deliberately flexible language so presidents could respond quickly to changing global trade conditions, not just narrow, predictable disputes.
    • Trade relationships shift constantly, and a law that only covers the exact situations lawmakers imagined in 1974 would be useless for problems that didn't exist back then.
    • Courts second-guessing the technical details of trade strategy risks tying the president's hands in negotiations where speed and flexibility matter.

    Read it narrowly

    • Laws delegate specific, limited powers, and letting one clause justify blanket tariffs on 80 countries at once stretches the text far past its plain meaning.
    • If any broadly worded law can be used to justify virtually unlimited executive action, the distinction between what Congress authorized and what it didn't starts to disappear.
    • Allowing this reading mostly rewards legal creativity — reaching for whichever old law still works — rather than genuine compliance with what the law says.

  2. Is it better for the president to have strong, fast power to set tariffs, or should Congress reclaim tighter control over trade decisions?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Keep power with the president

    • Trade disputes and negotiations often move quickly, and requiring a full vote in Congress every time could make the U.S. slow and predictable in ways other countries could exploit.
    • The president is positioned to weigh diplomatic, economic, and security factors together in a way that's harder for a divided legislature to do quickly.
    • Presidents are accountable to voters nationally, which gives them a mandate to act on trade in a way individual members of Congress, focused on local districts, may not have.

    Return power to Congress

    • Taxing imported goods affects household budgets nationwide, and decisions with that much impact deserve the debate and compromise that lawmaking is built for.
    • Concentrating this much economic power in one office makes trade policy swing wildly every time a different president takes office, which is hard for businesses to plan around.
    • Courts having to repeatedly step in to check tariff actions suggests the current balance of power already leans too far toward the executive branch.

China's Factories Report a Good Month, After a Rough Patch

Every month, China checks on its factories, kind of like a report card. For two months, the report card said factories were slowing down. Now, in September 2026, the report card turned around: factories and service businesses are both growing again.

Why this mattersA lot of things you own, from phones to toys to clothes, were probably made in a factory in China. When those factories are busy, it can mean more goods on shelves, sometimes at lower prices. But if China makes too much and sells it very cheaply everywhere, factories in other countries can struggle to compete, which can affect jobs far from China too.
Read the whole story

Imagine a giant scoreboard that tells you if factories across a whole country are busier or slower than last month. In China, that scoreboard is called the PMI. A score above 50 means things are getting busier. Below 50 means things are slowing down.

For two months, China's factory score was stuck below 50. Then in September it jumped to 50.1. A second scoreboard, which tracks things like shops, building projects, and services, also crossed back over 50, landing at 50.2.

Why the turnaround? China's government poured a huge pile of money into the economy, about $119 billion. That's enough to buy over a million houses. The money went toward roads, factories, and new projects, plus there's been a boom in building computer chips and hardware for artificial intelligence.

People who watch China's economy closely, including news outlets outside China, agree the numbers are real. But some are cautious. They worry China is making so many goods that other countries feel flooded and squeezed out of their own markets.

China's Factories Start Growing Again — But Nobody's Sure It Will Last

A key measure of China's factory activity climbed back above the line that separates growth from shrinkage in September 2026, after two months stuck below it. The rebound followed a massive government spending push — about $119 billion — plus a surge in demand for AI-related hardware. Economists agree the numbers are real, but they disagree sharply about what comes next.

Why this mattersIf you've bought an electric scooter, a laptop, solar panels, or pretty much any gadget recently, there's a good chance parts of it were made in a Chinese factory — so whether those factories are thriving or struggling eventually shows up in prices and product availability everywhere. It's also a preview of an argument playing out in many countries, including likely wherever you live: how much should a government spend to rescue a slowing economy, and who ends up footing that bill later.
Read the whole story

Every month, Chinese officials survey factory managers across the country and ask a simple question: is business better or worse than last month? The answers get turned into a single number called the Purchasing Managers' Index, or PMI. Fifty is the dividing line — above it means more factories reported growth than decline, below it means the opposite. For July and August 2026, China's PMI sat under 50, a signal that its enormous manufacturing sector, the engine that has powered the country's rise for three decades, was quietly stalling.

In September, it crossed back over to 50.1. A parallel index covering services and construction also moved into growth territory, at 50.2. That matters because China's economy is the second-largest in the world and a major buyer of raw materials from Africa, Latin America, and Australia, and a major seller of everything from solar panels to smartphones to the rest of the world. When Chinese factories slow down, the effects ripple outward — fewer orders for iron ore miners in Brazil, cheaper steel flooding into European markets, slower growth in neighbouring Asian economies.

The turnaround didn't happen on its own. Beijing rolled out roughly 800 billion yuan — about $119.3 billion — in what are called policy-based financial instruments, a kind of targeted lending the government uses to get money into specific industries and infrastructure projects quickly, alongside more conventional stimulus spending. On top of that, demand for hardware tied to artificial intelligence — the chips, servers, and components needed to build and run AI systems — has been rising, giving manufacturers an extra boost that had nothing to do with government policy at all.

Independent outlets like CNBC and Bloomberg have checked the official PMI figures and found them consistent with other data, and they credit the stimulus for at least part of the improvement. But both add a caution: China's export growth remains fragile, because the same cheap, abundant Chinese manufacturing that's lifting this PMI number is also making China's trading partners nervous. If Chinese factories are producing more than China's own market can absorb — a situation economists call overcapacity — that surplus often gets sold abroad at low prices, which can undercut factories in other countries and provoke them to respond with tariffs or trade restrictions.

China's state-owned media, including China Daily, has presented the rebound straightforwardly as proof that the government's stimulus strategy is working. That's not a wrong reading of the numbers — independent reporting confirms the same PMI figures — but it's a selective one, since state media has an obvious interest in making government policy look successful and tends not to dwell on the overcapacity worry that independent analysts keep raising.

Taken together, this is the clearest sign all year that Beijing's spending push is doing something measurable to the real economy. Whether it's the start of a genuine recovery or a temporary sugar high paid for with borrowed money and dependent on an AI boom that could cool, nobody — including the economists studying the same numbers — can say for certain yet.

Words worth knowing
  • Purchasing Managers' Index (PMI) — A monthly score based on surveys of factory managers, where above 50 means more businesses are growing than shrinking, and below 50 means the opposite.
  • Stimulus — Extra government spending or lending aimed at speeding up a slowing economy, usually by putting money into people's or businesses' hands faster than normal.
  • Overcapacity — When factories make far more of something than buyers at home want, so the extra gets sold cheaply abroad, which can hurt factories in other countries.

Tech Bosses Promise to Be Careful With Super-Smart Computers — But Who Checks?

About 30 of the world's biggest computer bosses visited the White House this week. They make the smartest computer programs in the world, called AI. They all signed a promise to be careful and safe with it — but nobody is checking if they keep that promise.

Why this mattersAI is already inside things you might use, like apps that answer questions or games that learn how you play. If the companies building these powerful programs don't have real rules to follow, mistakes could slip through unnoticed. This argument about who checks on powerful technology will shape what AI looks like by the time you're grown up.

Imagine 30 kids promising their parents they'll clean their rooms, but no parent ever checks. That's a bit like what happened at the White House this week. Company leaders like Elon Musk, Jensen Huang, and Mark Zuckerberg signed something called the 'White House Accord on Super Intelligence.' It's a written promise to keep their AI programs safe.

AI stands for artificial intelligence. It means computer programs that can write, talk, draw, and even make decisions, almost like a very fast, very knowledgeable robot brain. These companies are racing to build AI that gets smarter every year.

The tricky part is that nobody outside these companies will check if they're really keeping their promise. There are no inspectors, no tests, and no punishment if a company breaks its word. Some safety experts say this makes the whole system messy and hard to trust.

The same day, the president also announced a new website called America.gov. It uses AI to help people figure out how government services work, like a smart helper for finding information.

  • Artificial Intelligence (AI) — A computer program built to do things that normally need a human brain, like writing, talking, or solving problems.

AI Giants Promise to Police Themselves — But Who's Checking?

Last week, President Trump brought together about 30 of the most powerful people in artificial intelligence — the bosses of Nvidia, Meta, Google, Amazon, OpenAI, Anthropic, and more — and announced they'd signed a deal called the 'White House Accord on Super Intelligence.' The companies promised to monitor their own AI safety instead of waiting for the government to force them to. Within days, reporters found that the promise has no referee, no punishment for breaking it, and no clear rules at all.

Why this mattersThe AI tools you already use — in search engines, homework help, your parents' workplaces — are being built and released under rules the companies wrote for themselves, not rules passed by any elected government. Whether that turns out fine or turns out to be a problem won't be obvious for years, which is exactly why the disagreement over whether to trust it now is a real one, not a settled one.
Read the whole story

Picture a classroom where the teacher announces that, instead of grading homework, students will grade their own and report back honestly. That's roughly the arrangement the US government just struck with the companies building the world's most powerful artificial intelligence systems. At a White House event, leaders from Nvidia, Meta, Google, Amazon, OpenAI, Anthropic, Palantir, and Elon Musk's companies signed what's being called the White House Accord on Super Intelligence — a pledge to manage the safety risks of advanced AI through their own internal efforts rather than through laws Congress would write and enforce.

On its face, the agreement sounds reassuring: the same executives racing to build increasingly powerful AI systems also claiming they'll keep each other, and themselves, in check. But reporters who looked closer in the days afterward found something messier. There's no independent group checking whether any company is actually following through. There's no agreed way to measure whether a company's safety testing is any good. And if a company simply ignores its own promise, nothing happens to it — no fine, no lawsuit, no regulator showing up. One description of the current landscape called it a 'more chaotic state,' which is a notable thing to say about an agreement that was announced with such fanfare.

This matters because of how AI safety concerns typically work. The worry isn't that a chatbot will suddenly become dangerous overnight — it's slower and subtler than that. It's about AI systems being deployed widely before anyone fully understands their failure modes: how they might be used to generate disinformation, assist in cyberattacks, or make consequential decisions (who gets a loan, who gets a job interview) based on patterns that are biased or simply wrong. Independent oversight — audits by outside experts, legal consequences for cutting corners — is one of the main tools societies use to catch these problems before they spread. Remove that, and you're relying on companies to catch their own mistakes while they're also under enormous pressure to ship new products faster than their rivals.

At the same event, Trump also unveiled America.gov, a new AI-powered government website meant to help ordinary citizens find and use federal services — renewing a passport, applying for benefits, that sort of thing — through an AI assistant rather than digging through confusing agency websites. It's a smaller story next to the safety accord, but it's a useful reminder that the same government making decisions about how to regulate AI is also becoming one of its customers, which adds another layer to an already tangled relationship between Washington and the AI industry.

Supporters of the accord argue this is exactly how innovation should be handled in its early stages: light-touch, flexible, and written by the people who actually understand the technology, rather than by lawmakers who might slow everything down with rules that become outdated within a year. They point to the risk of falling behind China, whose AI companies face a different regulatory environment and aren't waiting around. Critics, including AI safety researchers who study this field professionally, say voluntary pledges have a long track record of failing precisely when they're tested — because a company facing intense competition and shareholder pressure to launch a wildly profitable new feature doesn't have much incentive to quietly hold it back over a safety concern nobody outside the company is checking.

Words worth knowing
  • Voluntary accord — An agreement companies choose to follow because they want to, not because a law requires it — meaning there's usually no outside penalty if they don't.
  • Enforcement mechanism — A way of actually making sure a rule is followed, like an inspector, a fine, or a court — without one, a promise relies entirely on good faith.
  • Regulation — Rules created by a government, usually backed by law, that a company must follow whether it wants to or not.
