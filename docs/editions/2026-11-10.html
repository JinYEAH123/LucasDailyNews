A Chip Company's Boss Causes a Stock Market Shake-Up

Jensen Huang runs Nvidia, a company that makes powerful computer chips used in video games and artificial intelligence. He admitted to a secret relationship with a woman that American officials worry may have helped China get chips it wasn't supposed to have. The news made Nvidia's stock price drop sharply, and chip companies all over the world lost value too.

Why this mattersThe chips Nvidia makes help power the video games, apps, and AI tools you might use every day. When something shakes a giant chip company like this, it can affect prices, jobs, and even which countries get access to the newest technology.
Read the whole story

Nvidia makes special chips called GPUs. These chips are like super-fast brains that power video games, phone apps, and artificial intelligence programs that can chat or create pictures.

Jensen Huang, the boss of Nvidia, admitted he had a secret relationship with a businesswoman named Alice Huang. American intelligence officials believe she may have been working for Chinese government spies, trying to get restricted Nvidia chips sent to China even though rules are supposed to stop that. One news outlet reported that around 240,000 of these chips, worth about $7.5 billion, may have moved to China this way — but so far, only that one source has reported these numbers, so nobody else has confirmed them yet.

After the story came out, Nvidia's stock price fell hard — first by about 4%, then by another 5-6% the next day. That's the kind of drop that hasn't happened since 2019. Other chip companies dropped too, and together they lost an estimated $4-5 trillion in value over just two days — an amount of money bigger than the yearly earnings of most entire countries.

Because of the falling stock price, Microsoft briefly became more valuable than Nvidia again. The story has also made people ask harder questions about whether the United States is doing enough to stop restricted chips from reaching China.

Nvidia's CEO Admits an Affair — and a National Security Question Follows the Company's Stock Down

Nvidia's board has launched an independent investigation after CEO Jensen Huang admitted to an affair with a businesswoman that U.S. intelligence officials reportedly believe was recruited by Chinese state security to influence the company's chip-export decisions. A single outlet's reporting — not yet confirmed elsewhere — claims the relationship helped move billions of dollars of restricted chips into China. Nvidia's stock has fallen hard for two straight trading days, and the shockwaves have wiped trillions off chip-related stocks worldwide.

Why this mattersIf you've used an AI chatbot, played a game with realistic graphics, or seen self-driving cars on the news, you've benefited from the kind of chips Nvidia makes — and the rules around who gets to buy them shape which countries lead in AI for years to come. This story is also a reminder that stock markets can react instantly and enormously to claims that haven't been proven, which is worth remembering before trusting any single dramatic headline.
Read the whole story

Jensen Huang runs Nvidia, the company that makes the specialized computer chips — called GPUs — that power almost every major artificial intelligence system on earth, from ChatGPT to self-driving cars. Because those chips are so powerful, the U.S. government restricts which ones can be sold to China, worried they could be used to build AI for the Chinese military or to out-compete American technology. Nvidia has spent years navigating these restrictions, officially called export controls, designing slightly weaker chip versions specifically to stay legal for sale to Chinese customers.

This week, Huang admitted to an affair with a businesswoman named Alice Huang (no relation). According to a report from the tech-news outlet The Information, U.S. intelligence officials believe she was recruited by China's Ministry of State Security — the country's main civilian spy agency, roughly equivalent to a combined CIA and FBI — specifically to get close to Jensen Huang and influence Nvidia's export decisions. The same report claims a network tied to her helped move around $7.5 billion worth of Nvidia chips into China and obtained roughly 240,000 restricted GPUs. It is important to be clear about what is solid here and what isn't: these specific dollar figures and chip counts come from one outlet's sourcing, and no other news organization has independently confirmed them. The affair itself has been admitted; the espionage claim and its scale have not been proven in public.

Markets reacted as though the claims were true, or at least as though investors couldn't afford to wait and find out. Nvidia's stock dropped about 4% on Monday and another 5-6% on Tuesday — its worst two-day stretch since 2019 by one common measure of how sharply a stock has been sold off. Because Nvidia is so central to the AI industry, the panic spread: a basket of 62 chip-related companies tracked by Yahoo Finance lost an estimated $4-5 trillion in combined value over those two days. Microsoft, which had been trailing Nvidia in total market value, briefly overtook it again to become the world's second most valuable public company.

Nvidia's board has now opened its own investigation, separate from whatever U.S. intelligence agencies may be doing, to figure out whether the affair actually affected any business decisions — particularly decisions about which chips got approved for sale to China. That distinction matters a great deal. An executive having an affair is a personal and reputational problem for a company. An executive being manipulated by a foreign intelligence service into weakening national export controls is a different order of problem entirely, one that touches on whether the entire system of chip restrictions on China can be trusted to work.

The story lands at an awkward moment for U.S. policy on China and technology. Washington has spent years tightening and loosening chip export rules, arguing internally about how strict they should be and whether they even work — companies have been caught smuggling restricted chips through other countries before. If it turns out a sitting CEO's personal relationship genuinely compromised enforcement at the top, it would be the most serious failure of that system yet discovered. If it turns out the intelligence claims are exaggerated or wrong, the episode will instead be remembered as a case where unverified reporting nearly erased trillions in market value over two days.

  • Ministry of State Security — China's main civilian intelligence agency, responsible for both spying abroad and counter-espionage at home.
  • Oversold — A term used when a stock's price has fallen so fast and so far that some analysts think it has dropped more than the situation actually justifies.

Republicans Pick a New Leader After Losing Control of the House

The United States has a part of government called the House of Representatives, where elected people make laws. Republicans just lost the vote that decided who runs it. Now, instead of leading the whole House, Republicans had to choose someone to lead just their own team — and they picked a man named Jim Jordan.

Why this mattersThis is a bit like a class losing an election for class president, then the losing kids picking a new captain to speak up for them anyway. Even when a team doesn't get to make the final decisions, having a strong, clear leader still matters for being heard and making a plan to do better next time.
Read the whole story

Every two years, Americans vote for people to represent them in the House of Representatives. This time, the Republican Party lost enough seats that another party, the Democrats, now runs the House.

When a team loses control like this, it still needs a leader to speak for it and plan what to do next. Republicans had two choices: Jim Jordan, a strict rule-follower known for tough questioning, and Steve Scalise, who had been one of their top leaders before.

Jordan won. That means he will lead Republicans in the House for the next two years, even though his party is no longer in charge there.

Some Republicans wanted a new kind of leader after losing so many seats. Picking Jordan over Scalise shows that many in the party wanted a change in direction, not just the same team continuing as before.

House Republicans Pick a New Leader After Losing Control of the Chamber

After Democrats won enough House seats in last week's midterm elections to take control of the chamber, House Republicans had to decide who would lead them for the next two years — not as the party setting the agenda, but as the opposition. They chose Rep. Jim Jordan of Ohio, a conservative hardliner, over Steve Scalise, the man who had been their second-in-command. It's a sign of how much the party wants to change direction after a rough election night.

Why this mattersEven when a party loses control of Congress, its choice of leader still shapes which bills get fought over, which investigations get launched, and how gridlocked or workable Washington feels for the next two years. This is also a preview of an argument political parties have after every bad election — do you respond to defeat by changing course, or by getting tougher — and you'll see the same debate play out in other places, including eventually in whatever party you end up supporting.
Read the whole story

Every two years, all 435 seats in the House of Representatives are up for election, along with a third of the Senate and no presidential race — these are called midterm elections because they fall in the middle of a president's four-year term. This year, voters shifted enough seats toward Democrats that Republicans lost their majority, meaning they no longer hold more than half the seats and no longer control which bills get a vote, which committees investigate what, or who becomes Speaker of the House. Some outlets described the scale of the shift as a 'blue tidal wave' — blue being the color associated with the Democratic Party — though how big a wave it really was depends on which races you're counting and how close they were.

Losing a chamber doesn't just change who's in charge; it also changes who's in charge of the losing side. House Republicans hold their own internal vote, separate from the general election, to pick a leader for their 'conference' — the formal term for all the Republican members of the House acting as a group. That vote just happened, and it wasn't close to unanimous: Jim Jordan beat Steve Scalise, who had been the Majority Leader, the second-highest Republican position, responsible for scheduling floor votes and coordinating strategy while Republicans held power.

Jordan is a familiar figure to anyone who's followed Congress in recent years. He previously chaired the House Judiciary Committee, the panel that handles issues like federal courts, policing, and constitutional questions, and he built a reputation there as an aggressive questioner and a reliable vote for the most conservative positions in the party. Scalise, by contrast, was part of the leadership team that was actually running the House when it lost its majority — which made him, fairly or not, a stand-in for whatever went wrong.

That's really what this vote was about. When a party loses an election badly, its members have to decide whether the problem was the message, the messenger, or just bad luck in a tough political environment. Picking Jordan over Scalise is Republicans saying, in effect, that they want sharper opposition and a harder line against the new Democratic majority — not a continuation of whatever strategy was in place when they lost the chamber.

What Jordan actually does with the job will look different from what a majority leader does. In the minority, House Republicans can't pass laws on their own; their main tools are blocking or slowing down Democratic bills, using committee seats to investigate and ask pointed questions, and building a case for why voters should put them back in charge at the next election, two years from now. How combative or how cooperative Jordan chooses to be will shape daily life in Washington for the next two years, even though his party doesn't hold the gavel.

Words worth knowing
  • Midterm elections — Elections held two years into a president's term, when all House seats and some Senate seats are up for a vote but the presidency itself is not.
  • Majority / Minority — In Congress, the party with more than half the seats is the majority and controls the schedule and committees; the other party is the minority and mainly has power to slow things down or object.
  • House Judiciary Committee — A group of House members who handle laws and investigations related to courts, policing, and constitutional issues, and whose chair is a prominent and powerful role within the majority party.

Prices Crept Up in October — Now Everyone's Watching What the Fed Does Next

Every month, the U.S. government checks how much things cost — like bread, gas, and rent — and compares it to the month before. In October, prices went up a bit more than experts expected. That tiny difference is a big deal, because it affects a meeting happening soon where officials decide whether borrowing money gets cheaper or stays the same.

Why this mattersWhen prices rise, the money in your piggy bank buys a little less than it used to — a $5 toy might cost $5.20 next year. This report also affects things like how much it costs grown-ups to borrow money for a house or a car, and how much certain government payments (like the ones many grandparents get) increase to keep up with rising costs.
Read the whole story

Imagine you check the price of your favorite snack every single month. In October, across the whole country, prices on average went up by 0.4%. That might sound small, but it adds up when you're buying lots of things, from milk to sneakers.

Even more surprising: if you ignore food and gas prices (which jump around a lot), the rest of prices still rose 0.3%. Experts had guessed it would only be 0.2%. That's like predicting a 2-inch growth spurt and getting 3 inches instead — not huge, but noticeable.

This number matters because a group called the Federal Reserve is about to meet in December to decide something important: whether to make borrowing money cheaper. Many people thought they'd do this no matter what, and even with prices rising a little more than expected, most experts still think they will.

When this report came out, stock markets — basically giant marketplaces where people buy and sell tiny pieces of companies — dipped slightly. Investors were trying to figure out what the price news meant for what the Fed might do next.

  • Interest rate — An interest rate is like a fee you pay for borrowing money — a higher rate means borrowing costs more, a lower rate means it costs less.

U.S. Prices Rose Faster Than Expected in October — Right Before a Big Decision on Interest Rates

The U.S. government's main measure of inflation came in hotter than economists had predicted for October. Overall prices rose 0.4% for the month, and even the version that strips out food and energy costs rose 0.3% — higher than the 0.2% most forecasters expected. The timing matters: this is the last major inflation reading before the Federal Reserve meets in early December to decide whether to cut interest rates again.

Why this mattersIf you or your family have a mortgage, a car loan, or credit card debt, the Fed's rate decisions eventually show up in what you pay every month. And if a parent or grandparent relies on Social Security, inflation reports like this one directly shape how much that monthly check grows to keep up with rising prices.
Read the whole story

Every month, the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics — a government agency that tracks prices, jobs, and wages across the country — publishes the Consumer Price Index, a running tally of what a typical basket of everyday goods and services costs compared to a month earlier. In October, that basket got 0.4% more expensive. Economists also watch a stripped-down version called 'core' inflation, which removes food and energy prices because those two categories swing wildly for reasons that have nothing to do with the underlying economy — a hurricane can spike gas prices for a week, a bad harvest can spike egg prices for a season. Core prices rose 0.3% in October, a notch above the 0.2% that forecasters had penciled in.

That gap between 0.2% and 0.3% sounds tiny, but it's the kind of thing that moves markets, because it changes what people expect the Federal Reserve to do next. The Federal Reserve — the U.S. central bank, often just called 'the Fed' — sets a key interest rate that influences almost every other interest rate in the economy: what a bank charges you for a mortgage, what your credit card charges on an unpaid balance, what a car loan costs. When the Fed cuts its rate, borrowing gets cheaper, which tends to encourage spending and hiring. When inflation is running hot, the Fed usually hesitates to cut, because cheaper borrowing can push prices up even further.

That's the tension this report created. Inflation came in hotter than expected, which is normally a reason for the Fed to be cautious. But this is also the last inflation report the Fed will see before its December 8–9 meeting, and according to Bloomberg's coverage of the release, investors still mostly expect the Fed to cut rates anyway — because other parts of the economy, particularly a softening job market, are pulling policymakers in the opposite direction. The Fed doesn't just fight inflation; its official mandate is to balance stable prices against maximum employment, and right now those two goals are pointing in different directions.

Stock markets reacted almost immediately. The Dow, the S&P 500, and the Nasdaq — three widely watched indexes that track the overall health of U.S. company share prices — all slipped after the report came out. That's a fairly normal response: when inflation surprises to the upside, investors have to recalculate how likely a rate cut still is, and uncertainty about interest rates tends to make stock prices wobble in the short term, even before anyone knows what the Fed will actually decide.

Beyond Wall Street, this number touches ordinary financial life in ways that are easy to overlook. Inflation readings feed directly into cost-of-living adjustments for Social Security payments, into wage negotiations between unions and employers, and into the interest rates banks offer on savings accounts and charge on loans. A 0.1 percentage point surprise in one month's report won't change anyone's mortgage overnight, but a string of readings like this one is exactly how the Fed decides whether borrowing should get cheaper or stay where it is — a decision that eventually shows up in nearly every household's budget.

Words worth knowing
  • Consumer Price Index (CPI) — A monthly measurement of how much the prices of everyday goods and services have changed compared to a month or year earlier.
  • Core inflation — Inflation measured after removing food and energy prices, which jump around for reasons unrelated to the broader economy.
  • Interest rate cut — A decision by the Federal Reserve to lower the cost of borrowing money, usually meant to encourage spending and hiring when the economy needs support.

  1. Should the Federal Reserve cut interest rates in December even though inflation just came in higher than expected?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Cut anyway

    • The job market is showing signs of weakening, and waiting too long to support it risks a deeper slowdown that's harder to reverse.

    • Cutting rates while inflation is accelerating risks reinforcing the very price increases the Fed is supposed to be controlling.

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes, they matter

    • Each report is genuine new information about where the economy is actually heading, not noise to be ignored.
    • Markets exist to process information quickly, and reacting to real data is how prices stay accurate and useful.
    • A string of 'small' surprises in the same direction is exactly how larger economic turning points first become visible.

    No, it's overreaction

    • A single month's number is noisy and gets revised later; treating it as decisive encourages short-term panic over sound judgment.
