US Warplanes Hit Iran Again After Ships Attacked in a Busy Sea Passage

On Tuesday and Wednesday, American warplanes struck military sites inside Iran, a country in the Middle East. The US says it did this because Iran tried to place mines in the water and fired missiles at a US base. Two oil ships were also hit in a narrow strip of sea called the Strait of Hormuz. Why this mattersOil doesn't just power cars — it's used to make plastic, medicine, and even the fabric in your clothes. When fighting happens near a place where so much of the world's oil travels, prices for gas and everyday things can go up almost everywhere, including in your town. It's also a reminder that when countries argue, ordinary people — like the wedding guests in Iran — can end up caught in the middle.

US Strikes Iran Again After Sea Mines and Missiles Break a Month-Long Truce

Overnight Tuesday into Wednesday, the US military hit military sites inside Iran belonging to the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, a branch of Iran's armed forces separate from its regular army. The strikes followed Iran's attempt to plant sea mines in the Strait of Hormuz, a narrow waterway that carries roughly a fifth of the world's daily oil supply, and an Iranian missile attack on a US base in Jordan. President Trump called the strikes "large and powerful" in a social media post, describing them as payback for both incidents.

Why this mattersWhen shipping through the Strait of Hormuz gets disrupted, oil prices tend to rise within days, and that shows up at the gas pump and in the price of anything that gets shipped by truck or plane — which is most things. A conflict thousands of kilometers away can still reach into a family's monthly budget faster than almost any other kind of news event.

The exchange ended nearly a month of relative quiet between the two countries. US forces targeted air-defense radar systems, equipment Iran uses to lay sea mines, and communications facilities tied to the Revolutionary Guard, an organization that runs its own navy, missile programs, and intelligence operations separate from Iran's conventional military. The US framed the strikes as a direct response to two things: Iran trying to seed the Strait of Hormuz with mines, and an Iranian missile strike on an American base in Jordan.

The Strait of Hormuz matters here because of geography, not politics. It is barely 33 kilometers wide at its narrowest point, and nearly every ship carrying oil out of the Persian Gulf has to pass through it. Mining that waterway is not a symbolic gesture — it is a way to threaten the flow of oil to the rest of the world without necessarily sinking a single ship, since the mere presence of mines makes shipping companies reroute or pay far more for insurance. During this latest flare-up, at least two commercial oil tankers were hit by projectiles or mines while passing through the strait, which is the kind of incident that immediately moves global oil prices.

Alongside the military strikes, the US has been running a separate economic pressure campaign against Iran, led by Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent. This is a strategy of sanctions — legal restrictions that block a country's access to international banking, trade, or specific goods — aimed at limiting the money Iran's government can raise and spend, including on its military programs. Washington is essentially fighting on two fronts at once: bombing military infrastructure while trying to choke off the funding that rebuilds it.

Iranian state media outlets, Press TV and Fars, reported that one of the US strikes hit a wedding hall, killing several people, including a child. No independent, Western news organization has verified this claim, and it is currently supported only by outlets that operate under the direction of Iran's government. That does not automatically make the claim false — governments do sometimes report real civilian harm caused by an adversary — but it does mean the claim should be read as one side's account until other sources can confirm or deny it.

Analysts tracking the standoff warn that this pattern of strike, pause, and counter-strike could tip into something larger and harder to contain. A wider conflict in the Gulf region would not stay contained to Iran and the US; it would very likely disrupt oil shipments through Hormuz for weeks or months, and global oil markets react to that kind of uncertainty almost instantly, regardless of how much oil is actually lost.

A Mountain Lake Burst Its Banks — Now Over 1,100 People Are Confirmed Dead

A week ago, a giant lake made of melted glacier ice high in the Himalayan mountains suddenly broke open. A wall of water rushed down onto villages in Nepal and China. The number of people known to have died has now climbed past 1,100, and thousands more are still missing.

Why this mattersImagine your school, your street, and your family's electricity all disappearing in one afternoon — that is what happened to tens of thousands of families here. It matters everywhere too: as mountain ice keeps melting, these sudden mountain floods are becoming more common, not less.

High up in the mountains of Nepal, ice melts and forms lakes. Sometimes the walls holding these lakes back are just piles of rock and old ice. One of these walls broke. All that water rushed down the mountain at once, like a bathtub tipping over onto a whole valley.

The flood smashed through villages and destroyed bridges. It also damaged power stations that make electricity from river water. Nepal's disaster agency says at least 1,114 people have died. About 4,500 people are still missing. Around 84,270 people have been affected altogether — that is like every single person in a mid-sized town losing their home, school, or way to get food.

More than 32,000 of those affected are children. Rescuers have already saved almost 12,000 people, often by helicopter, since roads and bridges are gone. Sixteen people also died just across the border in China.

Nepal's government and the United Nations have now agreed to spend the next four months raising money and supplies to help families rebuild. Search teams are still looking for people who are missing, so the numbers may keep changing.

Nepal's Flood Death Toll Passes 1,100 as Search for the Missing Continues

A week ago, a lake high in the Himalayan mountains burst its banks without warning, sending a wall of water, mud and rock crashing down onto towns in Nepal and China. Nepal's disaster agency now says at least 1,114 people are confirmed dead, a sharp rise from the roughly 903 reported just days earlier. Around 4,500 people are still missing, and tens of thousands have lost their homes, roads, or electricity.

Why this mattersIt is easy to think of climate change as a slow, distant problem, but this flood shows how it can turn into a sudden, deadly event for people who did almost nothing to cause the warming that made it more likely. The same physics — melting ice, unstable dams of rock — is playing out in mountain ranges across the world, which is why disaster relief now increasingly means paying for damage caused somewhere else.
Read the whole story

The flood struck in late August, tearing through river valleys in Nepal's high mountains before crossing into Tibet, in western China. Whole sections of villages were swept away in minutes. A week on, Nepal's National Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Authority says the confirmed death toll has climbed to 1,114, up from about 903 just days before — a jump that shows how difficult it still is to find and count victims in terrain this remote. At least 16 more deaths have been confirmed on the Chinese side of the border.

What caused this was not ordinary rainfall. Glaciers — huge, slow-moving rivers of ice — often carve out natural lakes as they melt, held back only by loose piles of rock and ice called moraines, which act like a dam. As the planet warms, glaciers are melting faster, and these lakes grow larger and more unstable. When the natural dam finally gives way, sometimes triggered by an avalanche, an earthquake, or simply the pressure of too much water, the result is a glacial lake outburst flood: a sudden, violent release of water that can travel dozens of miles downstream with almost no warning.

The scale of the damage is still coming into focus. The United Nations estimates that 84,270 people across 17 municipalities have been affected, including more than 32,000 children. Roughly 4,500 people remain missing across Nepal and China combined, though officials expect that number to change — for better or worse — as rescue teams reach cut-off villages. Hydropower stations, which generate a large share of both countries' electricity, have been damaged on both sides of the border, and mountain communities that depended on a single road or bridge are now completely isolated.

Around 12,000 people have been pulled to safety so far, and Nepal's government has agreed with the UN's resident coordinator to launch a four-month humanitarian appeal — a formal request for international donors to fund emergency food, shelter, and medical care, followed by longer-term rebuilding. Because search operations are still ongoing in hard-to-reach terrain, the casualty and missing-person figures are being revised daily by Nepalese authorities, and readers should treat the numbers now circulating as still provisional rather than final.

This is being described as one of the deadliest Himalayan flooding disasters in years, but scientists who study the region have been warning for over a decade that it would not be the last. Thousands of glacial lakes now exist across the Himalayas, many of them growing every year as glaciers retreat, and millions of people live downstream in Nepal, China, India, Bhutan and Pakistan. A disaster that looks local on a map is, in that sense, a preview of a risk shared by an entire mountain range.

Words worth knowing
  • Glacial lake outburst flood (GLOF) — A sudden, violent flood caused when a lake formed by a melting glacier bursts through the loose rock or ice that was holding it back.
  • Moraine — A natural pile of rock, gravel and ice pushed up by a glacier, which can act like a dam holding back a glacial lake.
  • Humanitarian appeal — A formal request, usually made by a government together with the United Nations, asking other countries and donors to fund emergency relief and rebuilding after a disaster.

19 Countries Agree, 1 Says No: A Big Money Meeting Splits Over China

Leaders from 20 of the world's biggest economies met this week to talk about money and trade. Nineteen of them signed a joint statement agreeing on a problem. China was the only one who refused to sign.

Why this mattersWhen countries argue about trade, it can change the price of things you buy, like toys, phones, or bikes, because many of them are made in China. It also shows how the two biggest economies in the world, the US and China, don't always see things the same way, which affects how countries work together on bigger problems too.
Read the whole story

Twenty countries sent their top money officials to a meeting in Asheville, a town in North Carolina. This group is called the G20, and it includes the world's largest economies, like the United States, China, Japan, and Germany.

At the meeting, the countries usually try to agree on everything together. This time, 19 of them signed a statement saying some countries make too many cheap products and sell them to everyone else, which they said hurts fair competition. They didn't name China directly, but everyone knew who they meant, because China makes and exports more manufactured goods than almost any other country.

China said no. China's team didn't agree that its economy works the way the statement described, and refused to sign. This is unusual, because the G20 almost always finds a way to get everyone to agree, even if it takes a lot of talking.

This disagreement comes right before China's leader, Xi Jinping, is expected to visit the United States. Grown-ups are watching to see if this argument about trade will make that visit more tense.

Nineteen Countries, One Holdout: China Refuses to Sign G20 Trade Statement

At a G20 finance meeting in North Carolina this week, 19 of the 20 member countries signed a joint statement criticizing economies that flood the world with cheap exports — language clearly aimed at China. China refused to sign, breaking with the group's usual practice of only issuing statements everyone agrees to. The split happened just weeks before Chinese President Xi Jinping is due to visit the United States.

Why this mattersIf you've bought an electric car, solar panels, or cheap electronics recently, you've felt the effects of exactly the kind of export competition this dispute is about — and the outcome of this argument could shape prices, factory jobs, and tariffs in your own country for years. It also shows how international cooperation can fracture in public, which matters for anything else the world needs these countries to agree on, from climate policy to financial crises.
Read the whole story

The G20 is a group of the world's twenty largest economies — the US, China, Japan, Germany, Brazil, and others — whose finance ministers and central bankers meet periodically to coordinate on things like trade, debt, and financial stability. It has no power to force any country to do anything; its influence comes entirely from getting major economies to agree on a shared description of problems and, sometimes, shared solutions. That only works if everyone signs on. This week, at a meeting in Asheville, North Carolina, that broke down.

US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced that 19 of the group's members had agreed to a joint statement warning about 'non-market based economies pushing out a never-ending stream of cheap exports.' No country was named, but the phrase describes China: a system where the government, not just supply and demand, heavily shapes what gets built, how much, and at what price — often subsidizing factories so they can sell goods abroad more cheaply than market competition alone would allow. American and European officials have argued for years that this floods world markets with underpriced steel, solar panels, electric vehicles, and other goods, making it hard for factories elsewhere to compete.

China was the one country that would not sign. Its delegation rejected the description of its economy as 'non-market based,' arguing that framing misrepresents how China's economy actually works and unfairly singles it out. A 19-to-1 statement is unusual for the G20, which is built around consensus — normally, if even one major member objects, the group either waters down the language until everyone can accept it or issues no statement at all. Letting the disagreement stand on the record, rather than papering over it, is itself a signal: Bessent chose to present the 19-1 split as proof that most of the world agrees with the American view, rather than negotiate further to bring China in.

The timing sharpens the stakes. Xi Jinping is expected to visit the United States in the coming weeks, and this very public rupture — happening on American soil, with the US Treasury Secretary framing it as an international consensus against China — will sit in the background of that visit. Trade tension between the two countries has flared repeatedly over the past several years, through tariffs, export controls on technology, and disputes over industries like electric vehicles and semiconductors. A trade dispute aired this openly at a body that usually avoids public disagreement makes those underlying tensions harder to paper over during diplomatic visits.

Underneath the diplomatic language is a genuine disagreement about cause and blame that economists themselves argue about. One view holds that China's state-supported manufacturing genuinely distorts global trade by producing far more than its own population needs and exporting the surplus at prices that undercut foreign competitors, hurting factory jobs elsewhere. Another view holds that global trade imbalances are more complicated than one country's policies — that they also reflect how much the United States and other wealthy countries consume relative to what they produce, and that pinning the imbalance mainly on China's economic model oversimplifies a problem with many contributors.

Words worth knowing
  • G20 — A group of finance ministers and central bankers from the twenty largest economies in the world who meet to try to coordinate on trade and economic issues, though they have no power to force any country to act.
  • Non-market economy — A term for an economic system where government decisions, rather than open competition between buyers and sellers, heavily determine what gets produced and at what price.
  • Manufacturing overcapacity — A situation where a country's factories produce far more of something than its own market needs, so the surplus gets exported cheaply, which can undercut producers in other countries.
