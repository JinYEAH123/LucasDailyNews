America's Soldiers Leave Iraq After 20 Years

The United States has pulled every one of its soldiers out of Iraq, a country in the Middle East. The announcement came from the Pentagon, America's military headquarters, on Wednesday. Both the American and Iraqi governments called it the end of a very long chapter.

Why this mattersThis war started before most 9-year-olds were born, which shows how decisions grown-ups make can stretch on for an entire childhood and beyond. It's a reminder that ending something, even when everyone agrees it's time, can still be complicated and a little risky.
Read the whole story

In 2003, American soldiers invaded Iraq and removed its ruler, a man named Saddam Hussein, from power. American troops stayed in Iraq after that, first fighting a long war, then helping fight a dangerous group called ISIS that had taken over parts of the country.

Now, more than 20 years later, the very last soldiers have packed up and gone home. That is longer than most 9-year-olds' parents have had their jobs. Different news reports count the soldiers differently - one says about 1,500 left a single base, another says roughly 2,500 left the whole country - a bit like the difference between counting kids in one classroom versus the whole school.

Leaders in both Iraq and the United States celebrated the withdrawal. They called it a hopeful moment after two decades of foreign soldiers being stationed on Iraqi soil.

But not everyone is sure this is good news. Some experts worry that Iran, Iraq's powerful neighbor, could gain more influence now that American troops are gone. Others fear ISIS fighters, who were pushed back but never fully defeated, might try to return to areas in the north.

After 20 Years, the Last U.S. Troops Leave Iraq

The Pentagon announced this week that every American soldier has left Iraq, ending a military presence that began with the 2003 invasion. It is the longest continuous overseas deployment in U.S. history. Both Washington and Baghdad called it a milestone, but analysts warn the vacuum it leaves behind could be dangerous.

Why this mattersThis marks the end of the war that defined U.S. foreign policy debates for your entire lifetime — arguments about intervention, nation-building, and when it's right to use force abroad all trace back to Iraq. What happens next there will shape how the next generation of political leaders talks about war, and whether 'mission accomplished' ever means what it says.
Read the whole story

More than two decades after American tanks rolled into Baghdad and toppled the dictator Saddam Hussein, the last U.S. troops have packed up and gone home. The Pentagon — the building in Washington that serves as headquarters for the U.S. military and, by extension, a shorthand for the military itself — confirmed the withdrawal on Wednesday. Iraqi officials celebrated alongside them, treating the moment as the country finally reclaiming full control of its own territory.

The deployment outlived several different justifications. It started in 2003 as an invasion aimed at removing Saddam Hussein and searching for weapons of mass destruction that were never found. After the U.S. formally ended combat operations in 2011, troops stayed on in smaller numbers, and then returned in force after 2014 to help Iraqi and Kurdish forces fight ISIS — the extremist group that seized huge stretches of Iraq and Syria and declared itself a caliphate. That anti-ISIS mission became the deployment's main purpose for the last decade.

Exactly how many troops just left is oddly unclear. Al Jazeera reports about 1,500 personnel pulling out of a base near Erbil, in Iraq's Kurdish region, while ABC News puts the number at roughly 2,500 across the whole country. The two figures probably aren't contradicting each other so much as describing different things — one base versus the entire nation — but no single outlet has nailed down which number represents the true, final total. That kind of fog is common at the end of long, quietly-run deployments: there was no single dramatic announcement day, just units rotating out over time until the count reached zero.

What worries analysts isn't the troop count — it's what fills the space they leave. Iraq's government is influenced by militias with strong ties to Iran, Iraq's powerful neighbor and, for decades, its rival for regional influence. With no U.S. forces to act as a counterweight, those Iran-aligned groups may gain more freedom to shape Iraqi politics and security. At the same time, ISIS was defeated militarily but never fully destroyed as an idea or a network; sleeper cells still operate in remote areas, and Kurdish forces in the north — who did much of the actual ground fighting against ISIS with American air support — are especially exposed without that backup.

For Washington, the withdrawal closes a costly chapter: thousands of American lives lost, trillions of dollars spent, and a war that reshaped how the U.S. thinks about long, open-ended military commitments abroad. For Iraq, it's a test of whether the state built up over twenty years — its army, its institutions, its fragile balance between rival factions — can hold on its own. Nobody currently knows the answer, and that uncertainty is exactly why this story is being watched so closely rather than simply filed as good news.

Top Court Lets US Send Migrants to Countries They've Never Seen

On Tuesday, the U.S. Supreme Court made a big decision about deportation. Deportation means sending someone out of a country, usually back to where they came from. This time, the Court said the government can send some people to countries they have never even visited, without giving them a chance to say why that might be dangerous.

Why this mattersThis is about fairness — like being sent to a new school with no warning and no chance to explain why that's a bad idea for you. Millions of families in the US are affected by immigration rules like this one, and decisions from the Supreme Court can change lives very quickly, even before all the arguments are finished.

Imagine being told you have to move to a country you've never been to, where you don't know anyone. That's what could happen to some migrants now. Migrants are people who move from one country to live in another.

Lower courts had said migrants should get a chance to speak up first. They could tell a judge, 'That place isn't safe for me.' But the Supreme Court removed that rule. This is the second time this year the Court has done this.

The Court will still hear the full case later, with more arguments from both sides. But for now, deportations to these unfamiliar countries can start again everywhere in the US.

The White House also says it has deported over 605,000 people and that 1.9 million more left on their own. But those numbers come only from the government itself. No outside news group has checked if they're accurate.

Supreme Court Lets Deportations to Unfamiliar Countries Resume — For Now

On Tuesday, the U.S. Supreme Court cleared the way for the Trump administration to deport migrants to countries they have never lived in and have no ties to, without first letting them object. It's the second time this year the Court has stepped in to lift lower-court rules requiring that chance to object. The bigger legal fight over whether this is allowed at all is still coming — the Court has agreed to hear full arguments on it later.

Why this mattersThis case is about how much process a government owes someone before making an irreversible decision about their life — a question that echoes well beyond immigration, into anywhere legal protections and speed are in tension. The outcome of the fuller case the Court will hear later could reshape how much say any migrant gets before being removed from the country, for years to come.
Read the whole story

Imagine being told you're being sent to live in a country you've never set foot in, where you don't speak the language and know no one — not the country you came from, but a third one that simply agreed to take you. That's the situation at the centre of this fight. U.S. immigration law lets the government deport people to "third countries" when their home country won't take them back, but lower courts had ruled that migrants must at least get a chance to say why a particular third country is dangerous for them before they're sent there.

The Supreme Court disagreed — or rather, it removed that requirement while the larger legal battle continues. This matters because of how the Court did it: not through a full trial with written opinions explaining the reasoning, but through what's sometimes called the "emergency" or "shadow" docket — a fast, thinly-explained process for urgent requests. The administration asked the Court to lift a lower court's block, and the Court agreed, allowing deportations to resume nationwide immediately. This is the second time this has happened, which suggests a pattern: the Court is consistently siding with the government's deportation authority for now, even though it hasn't yet ruled on whether the underlying policy is actually lawful.

That distinction — between a temporary procedural ruling and a final decision on the merits — is easy to miss but important. The Supreme Court has separately agreed to hear full arguments on whether this third-country removal process violates migrants' rights. That case will take time. In the meantime, this ruling means the government can deport people without the objection process lower courts had required, and those deportations can't easily be undone once someone has already been sent abroad.

Due-process advocates — lawyers and groups who argue that fair legal procedure matters even when someone's immigration status is in question — say this is exactly the problem. If someone is deported to a country where they could face violence or persecution, and they're deported before they get a chance to raise that danger, the harm can't be reversed by a later court ruling. The administration's position, broadly, is that the deportation system needs to move quickly to be effective at all, and that giving every migrant a hearing over every destination country creates delays that undermine enforcement.

Separately, the White House has said that more than 605,000 people have been deported and 1.9 million more have "self-deported" — left the country voluntarily under pressure — since the immigration crackdown began. These numbers come only from the administration itself; no independent outside reporting has verified them. That doesn't mean they're false, but it means readers should treat them as a claim by one side in the debate, not as an established fact the way the Court's ruling is.

Words worth knowing
  • Third-country deportation — Sending a migrant to a country other than their home country or the one they tried to enter, often one they have no prior connection to.
  • Due process — The legal principle that a person must be given fair procedures — like a chance to be heard — before the government takes serious action against them.
  • Shadow docket — A nickname for the Supreme Court's process for deciding urgent requests quickly, usually without a full hearing or detailed written explanation.

Six Giant Tech Companies Promise to Watch Their Own AI

At the White House, the home and office of the American president, the bosses of six huge tech companies signed a promise about their AI programs. President Trump stood beside them for the signing. The promise says the companies will build safety teams to check their own AI. But here's the twist: nobody outside the companies is checking their work.

Why this mattersAI programs already help write your homework helper apps, choose the videos you see online, and are starting to help drive cars. Whether these six companies actually keep their promise matters for how safe those tools are for you and everyone else using them.

Six company leaders signed a paper at the White House. Their companies are called Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Meta, xAI, and Nvidia. These are the companies that build AI, which stands for artificial intelligence: computer programs that can write essays, answer questions, and make pictures, almost like they are thinking.

The paper is called the Joint Commitment on Frontier Responsibilities. Some people are calling it the "AI constitution," like a rulebook. It says each company will create its own safety team to watch its AI and make sure it doesn't do anything dangerous.

President Trump hosted the event and signed it with them. This happened after several scary moments where AI programs did unexpected or worrying things.

Here's the catch. This isn't a law. No government inspector will come check if the companies are keeping their promise. It's like a student grading their own homework, with no teacher checking the answers.

Six AI Giants Sign a Safety Pledge at the White House — But Who's Checking Their Work?

The CEOs of Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Meta, xAI and Nvidia stood alongside President Trump this week to sign a pact promising to build 'robust' safety controls into their most powerful AI systems. It's being called the first agreement of its kind on the most advanced ('frontier') AI models being built in the US. But it's a voluntary promise, not a law — which means the companies are essentially agreeing to police themselves.

Why this mattersThe chatbots and AI tools you already use for homework, games or advice are getting more powerful fast, and right now the main rules governing how carefully they're built are ones the companies wrote for themselves. Whether that turns out to be enough, or whether governments eventually step in with binding laws, will shape how much you can trust the AI tools in your pocket for years to come.
Read the whole story

Six of the most powerful companies building artificial intelligence just made a public promise about how carefully they'll handle it. At a signing event hosted by President Trump, the chief executives of Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Meta, xAI and Nvidia put their names to what's being called the Joint Commitment on Frontier Responsibilities — nicknamed, half-jokingly, an 'AI constitution.'

The agreement commits each company to put 'robust' monitoring systems in place for their most advanced models — the ones sometimes called 'frontier' AI, meaning systems pushing past what's been built before, with capabilities that aren't fully predictable even to the people who made them. Each company is also supposed to set up an internal team whose job is to watch for dangerous behaviour and enforce the rules the company itself has written.

This didn't happen in a vacuum. It follows a string of unsettling incidents involving AI systems — chatbots giving users harmful advice, models behaving in ways their own creators didn't anticipate or couldn't fully explain. Pressure had been building on Washington to do something. What Washington chose to do was not pass a law. It gathered the industry's biggest players in one room and got them to promise, on the record, to regulate themselves.

That distinction matters more than it might sound. A law comes with penalties a court can enforce — fines, injunctions, sometimes criminal liability — decided by a judge or regulator with no stake in the outcome. This agreement comes with none of that. There is no government regulator named as a referee, no outside body checking whether the 'robust' controls are actually robust, no penalty spelled out for a company that quietly falls short. The people checking the companies' homework are, for now, the companies themselves.

Supporters of the deal call it a meaningful first move — six fierce competitors, who normally guard every advantage jealously, agreeing in public to a shared set of safety principles is not nothing, and it gives everyone a baseline to be held to, even informally, by journalists, researchers and each other. Critics point to history: voluntary tech-industry pledges have been made before, on privacy and on content moderation, and have often quietly loosened once public attention moved on, precisely because nobody outside the industry had the power to enforce them.

Both readings can be true at once. The pact might be a genuine first step and also toothless — those aren't contradictions, they're just two different questions: whether the commitment is sincere, and whether sincerity is enough when the stakes involve systems whose behaviour the companies themselves don't always fully predict.

Words worth knowing
  • Frontier AI — The most advanced AI models being built right now, whose full abilities and risks aren't completely understood even by the companies that create them.
  • Voluntary agreement — A promise a company chooses to make and can choose to break, as opposed to a law, which comes with penalties a court can enforce.
  • Regulator — An independent government body with the legal power to inspect, fine, or shut down a company that breaks safety rules — something this agreement does not include.
