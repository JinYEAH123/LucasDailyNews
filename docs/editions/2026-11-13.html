The Boss of the World's Biggest Chip Company Suddenly Quit

Jensen Huang ran Nvidia, a company that makes the computer chips inside most AI systems, like the ones behind chatbots and video game graphics. He resigned on November 12 after news came out about his personal relationships, including one with a woman U.S. officials worry might be connected to Chinese spying. Nvidia's stock price dropped right after, and the company's total value fell below $4 trillion for the first time since July.

Why this mattersNvidia's chips are inside the AI tools millions of people use every day, from phone assistants to homework helpers. When the leader of a company this important and this connected to other countries suddenly leaves under a cloud of spying concerns, it makes people wonder who they can trust with powerful technology. It also shows how a leader's personal choices can shake an entire company, even one worth trillions of dollars.

Nvidia makes the computer chips that act like brains inside AI programs. These chips help computers recognize pictures, write text, and play games smarter than ever before. Nvidia grew so big and important that its value reached about $4 trillion — more dollars than you could count even if you counted one every second for over 100,000 years.

Then its boss, Jensen Huang, suddenly resigned. Reports said he had relationships outside his marriage, including one with a woman named Alice Huang who used to work at another tech company. U.S. officials are worried she might have passed information to Chinese intelligence agents — people who secretly gather information for another country's government. China says that is not true.

Nvidia quickly picked Colette Kress, the company's chief financial officer (the person in charge of its money), to be the temporary new boss. Jensen Huang is now expected to answer questions from Congress, the group of elected lawmakers who help run the country, about what happened and whether any secrets were at risk.

After all this news, Nvidia's stock price dropped again on Friday, falling just over 2%. That pushed the company's total value below $4 trillion, a line it had stayed above since the summer.

Nvidia's CEO Quits Over an Affair — and a China Spy Question Wipes Out Trillions in Value

Jensen Huang, the man who built Nvidia into the company that makes the chips powering most of the world's artificial intelligence, resigned on November 12th after reports surfaced of two affairs — one with a former tech executive that U.S. officials suspect may have links to Chinese intelligence. Nvidia's board moved fast, naming its chief financial officer as a temporary replacement, but the markets did not wait for answers: shares kept falling, and by Friday the company's total value had dropped back below $4 trillion for the first time since the summer.

Why this mattersNvidia's chips are inside the AI tools millions of people already use every day, from chatbots to the systems companies use to write code and analyze data, so instability at the top of that company can ripple into the pace and cost of AI development everywhere. It's also a reminder that in industries tangled up with national security, a CEO's personal choices can become everyone's business — including Congress's.

Jensen Huang didn't just run Nvidia — he was, for most investors, Nvidia. He co-founded the company, built it from a maker of video-game graphics cards into the single most important supplier of the specialized chips that train and run AI systems like ChatGPT, and became one of the most recognizable executives in tech, famous for his leather jacket and his confident predictions about AI's future. So when he resigned abruptly, it wasn't an ordinary personnel change. It was closer to a company losing its identity overnight.

The resignation followed reports of two extramarital affairs, one of them with Alice Huang, a former executive at Megaspeed, a company in the AI supply chain. What turned this from a personal scandal into a geopolitical one is that U.S. officials are reportedly looking into whether she has ties to Chinese intelligence services — a suspicion Beijing has flatly denied. Nothing has been proven. But the mere possibility that someone close to the CEO of America's most important chip company might have connections to a foreign intelligence service is the kind of thing that triggers national security reviews, not just gossip columns.

Nvidia's board tried to project stability, naming Colette Kress, the company's chief financial officer, as interim CEO — a temporary head meant to keep the company running while the board decides on something more permanent. But 'interim' is doing a lot of work in that sentence: nobody yet knows if Kress will get the job permanently, or if the board will go looking for someone else entirely once the dust settles. Huang himself is now expected to testify before Congress, where lawmakers will likely press him not about the affairs themselves but about what he knew, when he knew it, and whether any sensitive information about Nvidia's chip designs or customers was ever at risk.

Markets don't like uncertainty, and they especially don't like uncertainty layered on top of a national security question involving China — Nvidia's chips are central to the ongoing U.S.-China competition over AI, and Washington has already restricted which Nvidia products can be sold there. On Friday, Nvidia's stock fell another 2.01%, dropping its market capitalization — the total value of all its shares combined — below $4 trillion for the first time since July. To put that in perspective, that's a company worth more than the entire economy of a country like Japan, and the drop alone erased more money than most companies are worth in total.

What happens next depends on two separate things resolving at once. Congress has to decide how seriously to take the testimony — whether this becomes a real investigation into espionage risk or a brief, awkward hearing that fades from headlines. And Nvidia's board has to decide whether Kress is the long-term answer or just a safe pair of hands while they search elsewhere. Until both of those questions settle, investors are essentially pricing in doubt about a company whose products sit at the center of the global AI race.

The Biggest Rocket Ever Built Tries to Land Itself — Both Halves, At Once

On Friday evening, a giant rocket called Starship will try something nobody has ever fully done before. Both of its two big parts — the booster that launches it and the ship that flies to space — will try to come back down in one piece. If it works, rockets could get used again and again, like a bus instead of a firework.

Why this mattersIf this works, trips to space could become as normal and cheap as taking a long flight on an airplane, instead of something only a few countries can afford. That could decide who builds the first Moon base, and whether your generation grows up watching rockets launch every week instead of once in a while.
Read the whole story

Starship is taller than a 30-story building and is the most powerful rocket ever built. It launches from Texas, made by a company called SpaceX.

Most rockets are thrown away after one flight, like a paper cup. That makes space travel very expensive. SpaceX wants to catch and reuse both parts of Starship so flying to space costs much less, like reusing a water bottle instead of buying a new one every time.

This matters because NASA wants to use Starship to land astronauts on the Moon again, as part of a plan called Artemis. China is also racing to build a base near the Moon's south pole, so whoever gets reusable rockets working well first has a big advantage.

The test launch happens Friday evening, after most news is already written for the day. That means nobody knew yet whether it worked when this page was made — it's a story still happening.

SpaceX Tries to Catch Its Giant Rocket — Both Halves — for the First Time

On Friday, November 13, SpaceX will launch Starship, the biggest and most powerful rocket ever built, from a test site in Texas. For the first time, the company will try to bring back both the booster and the upper-stage ship intact, rather than letting either burn up or crash into the ocean. If it works, it would be a huge step toward making rockets as reusable as airplanes.

Why this mattersIf reusable rockets become routine, the cost of putting things in space — satellites that provide internet and weather data, telescopes, eventually people — could fall sharply within your lifetime, the way flights and shipping got cheaper once reuse became normal. It also matters because which country controls access to the Moon's south pole could shape rules and resources there for decades, similar to how early claims on valuable land or sea routes shaped later disputes.
Read the whole story

Rockets have always been mostly disposable. You build an enormous machine, burn through most of its parts getting to space, and the bulk of it is destroyed or dumped into the ocean on the way. SpaceX already reuses the booster — the bottom section that provides the initial thrust — on its smaller Falcon 9 rocket, landing it upright after launch. But Starship is a different scale of problem: it is taller than the Statue of Liberty and made of two giant reusable parts, the Super Heavy booster and the Starship upper-stage ship that actually carries cargo or people into orbit. Catching both of them intact, on the same flight, has never been done.

The reason companies and governments care so much is money. A huge share of what it costs to reach space is the hardware you throw away. If you can land a rocket, inspect it, refuel it, and fly it again within days — the way an airline flies the same plane dozens of times a week — the cost per launch could fall dramatically, the way air travel became radically cheaper once planes stopped being single-use. That is why engineers have nicknamed full reusability the 'holy grail' of rocketry: not a nice bonus, but the thing that could finally make space travel routine rather than rare and extravagant.

The stakes go beyond SpaceX's balance sheet. NASA's Artemis program — its current plan to send astronauts back to the Moon's surface for the first time since the 1970s — is counting on a giant reusable rocket like Starship, or a rival lander being built by Jeff Bezos's company Blue Origin, to actually get astronauts down to the lunar surface and back. Both the United States and China have said they want to build a permanent base near the Moon's south pole, where ice trapped in permanently shadowed craters could one day be turned into drinking water, breathable oxygen, or rocket fuel. Whoever lands there first and stays effectively gets to write the rules for how that territory is used, which is why this is being treated less like a scientific milestone and more like a race.

The launch window runs from 5:45 to 7:00 p.m. Eastern time on Friday — early-to-mid afternoon on the US West Coast, where the test range actually sits — which falls right around or after when this page is being written and published. That means nobody yet knows, as you read this, whether the attempt succeeded, partially succeeded, or failed. Test flights of brand-new rocket hardware fail often; several previous Starship test flights have ended in explosions, which SpaceX treats as expected data-gathering rather than disaster, since each attempt teaches engineers something that gets fixed on the next vehicle.

Recovering the booster typically means catching it with giant mechanical arms on the launch tower, nicknamed 'chopsticks,' as it descends — an operation that looks less like a landing and more like someone catching a falling telephone pole out of the sky. Recovering the upper-stage ship as well, on the same flight, would be new territory entirely. Whatever happened on Friday will shape how quickly NASA can commit to a Moon landing date, and how seriously to take the idea that getting to space might soon cost a small fraction of what it does today.

Words worth knowing
  • Reusability — Designing a rocket so its parts can be recovered, checked, and flown again, instead of being thrown away after one use.
  • Booster — The large bottom section of a rocket that provides the powerful initial thrust needed to leave the ground, and is usually the first part to separate.
  • Artemis program — NASA's current plan to send astronauts back to the Moon's surface and eventually build a lasting base there.
