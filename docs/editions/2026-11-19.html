Three of the World's Most Powerful Leaders Meet for the First Time Ever

On Thursday, the leaders of the United States, China, and Russia sat down together for the first time ever. Donald Trump, Xi Jinping, and Vladimir Putin met in Shenzhen, a big city in southern China. They talked about nuclear weapons and the war in Ukraine. At the end, they signed a short statement saying a nuclear war should never happen, but they did not agree on any real changes.

Why this mattersThese three leaders control huge armies and enough nuclear weapons to change life for everyone on Earth, including you. When they talk instead of ignoring each other, it can lower the chance of a terrible war. But when they don't agree on real changes, big problems like the war in Ukraine keep going.
Read the whole story

Three of the most powerful leaders in the world met in one room. That has never happened before. Donald Trump leads the United States. Xi Jinping leads China. Vladimir Putin leads Russia. They met in Shenzhen, a big city in southern China.

Trump wanted to talk about nuclear weapons. These are super powerful bombs that can destroy whole cities. He also wanted to stop Iran from building its own nuclear weapons. Putin wanted the war in Ukraine to end in a way that helps Russia. Xi mostly wanted to stay out of both fights.

At the end, the three leaders signed a joint declaration. That is a shared written statement. It said nuclear war must never happen. But it didn't say how many weapons anyone would get rid of. It also didn't set a deadline. And it didn't mention Ukraine or Iran at all.

News reporters everywhere agree on one thing. Nothing major actually changed. The real news was that these three leaders sat in the same room and talked at all.

Trump, Xi and Putin Meet for the First Time Ever — and Agree on Almost Nothing

On Thursday, the leaders of the United States, China and Russia sat down together for the first time in history, in the Chinese city of Shenzhen. Donald Trump wanted to talk about cutting nuclear weapons and stopping Iran from building a bomb; Vladimir Putin wanted an end to the Ukraine war that favored Russia; Xi Jinping mostly wanted to stay out of both fights. They signed a short joint statement about nuclear risk, but it contained no new arms-reduction deal and didn't mention Ukraine or Iran at all.

Why this mattersNuclear weapons, trade routes, and wars thousands of miles away can feel abstract until you remember that decisions made by three men in a room in Shenzhen shape whether wars end, whether new countries get nuclear weapons, and how safe the world's most dangerous arsenals stay. For a generation that will spend the next fifty years living with whatever nuclear order gets built now, a summit that produces vague language instead of real limits is a missed chance rather than a harmless one.
Read the whole story

Three men who run the world's most powerful militaries have never before sat in the same room as sitting heads of state — not during the Cold War, not after it. So the meeting itself, held in the southern Chinese tech hub of Shenzhen, was the headline before anyone said a word. The three leaders arrived with completely different goals. Trump wanted a conversation about reducing nuclear arsenals and keeping Iran from acquiring nuclear weapons. Putin wanted to use the moment to push for a Ukraine settlement that locked in Russian gains. Xi, hosting on his own turf, seemed content to let the other two talk past each other while China avoided being pulled into either dispute.

What came out of it was a joint declaration — a formal written statement all three governments sign their names to — focused on nuclear risk and energy security. Its one memorable line echoed a phrase used by nuclear powers since the 1980s: that "a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought." That sentence sounds significant, but it's also something these countries have said before, including separately. What the declaration did not contain mattered more than what it did: no timetable for actually reducing nuclear weapons, no mention of Ukraine, no mention of Iran. Three leaders walked into a room to talk about exactly those two conflicts, and the paper they walked out with named neither.

News organizations across the political spectrum — from outlets sympathetic to Trump to those critical of him, and in China and Russia too — converged on the same read: there was no breakthrough. The value, if there was one, was symbolic. Putin got photographed as an equal partner at a table with the American and Chinese presidents, which helps him domestically and pushes back against years of Western efforts to isolate him diplomatically over the invasion of Ukraine. Trump got to say he brought the three biggest military powers to the same table, something no American president had managed. Xi got to host the event on Chinese soil without having to commit China to a position on either war.

It's worth being precise about why nothing concrete emerged. Nuclear arms reduction normally requires years of technical negotiation between militaries, verification systems, and treaties that survive changes of government — not something settled in an afternoon. Ending the Ukraine war requires Russia and Ukraine to agree on territory, security guarantees and reconstruction, none of which the US or China can simply hand down from outside. And Iran's nuclear program involves a separate set of countries and inspectors entirely. A single summit, however historic its seating chart, was never going to resolve any of these on its own — the question is whether it moves anything forward at all, or just produces a photograph.

Words worth knowing
  • Summit — A meeting between the top leaders of different countries, usually to discuss major issues directly rather than through lower-level officials.
  • Joint declaration — A written statement that two or more governments agree to sign together, showing shared positions even when it doesn't create binding rules.
  • Nuclear nonproliferation — The effort to stop more countries from acquiring nuclear weapons, usually through treaties, inspections, and diplomatic pressure.

Computer Chip Makers Told to Stop Selling Their Best Chips to China

Three huge companies that make the tiny chips inside computers and phones have just stopped sending some of their best ones to China. The United States government told them they now need special permission first. One company, Nvidia, even had to stop selling chips it was already allowed to sell last year.

Why this mattersThe chips inside your tablet, game console, and your parents' phone come from this same industry, so decisions like this can change what gets made and how much it costs. It also shows how the biggest countries in the world sometimes use important inventions, not weapons, to compete with each other.
Read the whole story

Inside every phone, computer, and game console is a tiny piece called a chip. It's smaller than your thumbnail, but it's like a brain that does all the thinking.

Two companies, called TSMC and Samsung, make chips for lots of other companies around the world, including ones in China. The US government just said: if a chip is advanced enough, you need our permission before sending it to China.

A third company, Nvidia, makes chips that are extremely good at powering artificial intelligence, the kind of computer smarts behind things like chatbots. Nvidia had already gotten permission last year to sell one of its chips to China, and sold about $2.4 billion worth of them. If you spent one dollar every single second, it would take you more than 75 years to spend that much money. Now, even those sales have stopped.

This is part of a bigger plan by the US to make it harder for China to get the newest, most powerful chips.

Words worth knowing
  • Computer chip — A tiny piece of material, smaller than a fingernail, that acts like a brain inside phones, computers, and other devices.
  • Export license — A special permission a government gives before a company is allowed to sell certain things to another country.

Taiwan, South Korea and Nvidia Pull Back Chip Shipments to China as US Rules Tighten

This week three of the world's biggest chipmakers — TSMC, Samsung and Nvidia — stopped sending some of their most advanced chips to customers in China. The pause follows new US government rules that require a license before shipping high-end chips abroad. It's the latest step in a years-long campaign by Washington to keep its most powerful computing technology out of Chinese hands.

Why this mattersThe AI tools you might already use — chatbots, image generators, recommendation systems on your phone — all run on chips like these, and the companies racing to build the next generation of AI depend on this same supply chain. Whoever controls access to advanced chips has real influence over how fast AI develops and who gets to build it, which is why this fight between Washington and Beijing is likely to shape prices, available technology, and global tension for years to come.
Read the whole story

TSMC, the Taiwanese company that makes chips for Apple, Nvidia and almost every other major tech firm, has told customers that orders from Chinese chip designers must stop unless those customers first get a license from the US government. According to reporting that originated with the Financial Times and was carried onward by Reuters, the cutoff applies to chips made using a manufacturing process of 16 nanometres or smaller — because that detail traces back to a single reporting chain rather than several independent newsrooms, it's worth treating as likely accurate but not yet confirmed from multiple sources. Samsung, South Korea's chip giant, has made similar moves.

A nanometre is a billionth of a metre — far too small to picture directly, but the number matters because it describes how tiny the components packed onto a chip are. Smaller numbers mean more transistors squeezed into the same space, which means more computing power using less energy. Chips at 16 nanometres or smaller aren't the newest chips on the market, but they're still advanced enough to power serious AI systems, which is exactly why the US wants a say in who gets them.

Nvidia's situation shows how fast the rules are shifting. Last year, the US government allowed Nvidia to sell a specially modified version of its Hopper chip — a powerful processor built for running AI software in data centers — to Chinese customers. Nvidia sold $2.4 billion worth of these licensed chips in China in just the third quarter of this year. Then, without a new law being passed, Nvidia simply stopped shipping them. A product that was approved for sale became unsendable almost overnight.

The common thread tying all three companies together is the export license — government permission a company must obtain before shipping certain technology to certain countries. Washington has been widening the list of chips and countries that require this permission, and earlier this month it added more rules demanding approval before top AI chips can go to several other nations too. The effect is that China's access to the chips that train and run advanced AI systems is narrowing, one license requirement at a time.

Chips this advanced sit at the centre of a genuine power struggle. They're the engine behind AI research, advanced weapons systems, and the data centers that run everything from voice assistants to scientific modelling. The US argues that letting China freely buy the best chips would hand it tools that could be used militarily or to out-compete American firms. China, for its part, calls the restrictions an attempt to contain its growth — and has been pouring money into building its own chip industry so it no longer needs permission from anyone.

Words worth knowing
  • Export license — Official government permission a company must get before it's allowed to sell certain products — in this case, advanced chips — to buyers in another country.
  • Nanometre process — A measurement describing how small the components on a computer chip are; smaller numbers generally mean a more powerful and efficient chip.
  • AI chip (GPU) — A specialised processor, like Nvidia's Hopper chips, designed to handle the huge calculations needed to train and run artificial intelligence software in data centers.

World's Top Court Says Myanmar Hurt the Rohingya People So Badly It Was Genocide

On Thursday, the United Nations' highest court made a huge decision. It said that the country of Myanmar committed genocide — the worst crime there is — against a group of people called the Rohingya. This is the first time in 80 years that this court has ever said a country did this.

Why this mattersThis case says that even when something terrible happens far away, the world can still write down what happened and call it by its true name. Rohingya families who lost everything finally have an official answer: yes, this was genocide, and it was wrong. It also shows that smaller countries like Gambia can stand up for people they've never even met.
Read the whole story

The Rohingya are a group of Muslim people who used to live in Myanmar, a country in Southeast Asia. A few years ago, Myanmar's army attacked them. The court found that soldiers killed at least 10,000 Rohingya people — that's like wiping out everyone in a small town.

More than 730,000 Rohingya people ran for their lives into the country next door, Bangladesh. That's more people than live in most entire cities you've visited. Many of them are still living in crowded refugee camps today, almost ten years later.

A small country called Gambia, far away in Africa, decided someone had to speak up for the Rohingya. In 2019, Gambia brought the case to the court, even though it wasn't Gambia's own people who were hurt. It has taken this long — years of waiting — for the judges to finally decide.

The court has now officially said: this was genocide. But here's the tricky part. This court doesn't have police or soldiers of its own to force Myanmar to do anything. So what happens next is still unclear.

  • genocide — Genocide means trying to destroy a whole group of people because of who they are, like their religion or where their family comes from.
  • refugee — A refugee is someone who has to run away from their home country, usually because it's too dangerous to stay, and go live somewhere else.

World's Top Court Rules Myanmar Committed Genocide — After Survivors Waited Nearly a Decade

On Thursday, the International Court of Justice — the United Nations' highest court — ruled that Myanmar's military committed genocide against the Rohingya, a Muslim minority group. It is the first time in the court's 80-year history that it has found a country guilty of genocide. The case took almost six years to decide, and the events it judges happened even earlier than that.

Why this mattersThis case sets a legal marker that other courts, governments, and future genocide investigations can point to, which matters even to people who will never meet a Rohingya refugee. It also raises an uncomfortable question worth sitting with: what is justice worth if the people found guilty can simply ignore it?
Read the whole story

The Rohingya are a Muslim ethnic group who lived for generations in Myanmar's Rakhine State, but Myanmar's government has long refused to recognise them as citizens, treating them instead as illegal immigrants from Bangladesh even though many families had lived there for over a century. In 2017, Myanmar's military launched a violent crackdown that human rights investigators say killed at least 10,000 Rohingya people and forced more than 730,000 to flee across the border into Bangladesh, where hundreds of thousands still live in sprawling refugee camps — temporary settlements that, for many, have now lasted longer than some readers of this page have been alive.

In 2019, a small West African country called Gambia — which has no direct connection to Myanmar — brought the case to the ICJ on behalf of a group of Muslim-majority nations, arguing that the killings, rapes, and village burnings amounted to genocide: the attempt to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group. Genocide is considered the gravest crime recognised in international law, and proving it requires showing not just that people were killed, but that they were killed *because of* who they were, with intent to wipe out the group itself.

This week's ruling found that evidence met that bar. Judges pointed to the military's own internal planning, the scale and coordination of the attacks on Rohingya villages, and the systematic nature of the sexual violence used against Rohingya women as proof that the campaign went beyond ordinary wartime brutality. For survivors who have spent years testifying, giving evidence, and waiting, the judgment is the first time an international court has formally agreed with what they have said happened to them.

But the ruling comes with a hard limit: the ICJ has no police force, no army, and no way to physically force Myanmar to do anything. It can order a country to stop certain actions or pay compensation, but enforcement depends on other countries choosing to act — through sanctions, diplomatic pressure, or referrals to other courts — or on the country itself deciding to comply. Myanmar has been run since a 2021 military takeover by the same armed forces the ruling blames for the genocide, and that government has previously dismissed similar accusations as politically motivated.

Rohingya advocacy groups have welcomed the ruling as a long-overdue acknowledgment, even while acknowledging it will not immediately bring anyone home or rebuild a single village. What happens next — whether governments use this ruling to justify new sanctions, whether it strengthens separate criminal cases against individual military commanders, or whether it mostly stays a historical and legal milestone — is still unclear.

Words worth knowing
  • Genocide — The attempt to destroy all or part of a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group, which international law treats as the most serious crime a state or its leaders can commit.
  • International Court of Justice (ICJ) — The United Nations' main court for settling legal disputes between countries, separate from any court that prosecutes individual people.
  • Refugee camp — A temporary settlement set up to shelter people who have fled violence or disaster in their home country, though in practice many last for years or decades.

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    • Sometimes the people being hurt can't speak up for themselves, so someone else needs to do it.
    • It shows that countries can care about fairness everywhere, not just within their own borders.

    No, it shouldn't be allowed

    • A country that wasn't directly affected might not fully understand the situation the way the people living through it do.

    • It might be fairer for decisions like this to come from the affected people and their closest neighbours, not countries from far away.
