Two Countries Promised to Stop Fighting in 60 Days. Today the Time Ran Out.

In June, the United States and Iran promised each other something: we will stop fighting within 60 days. Today was day 60. They did not stop. And the American president warned another country, Oman, to stay out of the way.

Why this mattersOil is not only for cars. It is in the plastic of your water bottle, in the fuel of planes, and in what it costs to bring food to the shop near your home. When one narrow bit of sea becomes frightening, prices go up everywhere — including where you live.

Sometimes when two people are fighting, they say: let us stop by Friday. The United States and Iran did that in June. They picked a day 60 days later. Today was that day.

They did not stop. The American president said he will not give them more time. So the fighting will probably keep going.

Then he said something else. Next to Iran there is a very narrow strip of sea called the Strait of Hormuz. At its thinnest it is only about 33 kilometres across. Enormous ships full of oil squeeze through it every single day. Out of every five buckets of oil in the world, one goes through that one gap.

The president warned Oman, the country on the other side of that gap, not to get in the way. When countries say frightening things near the oil ships, the price of oil goes up all over the world. Today it went up.

A 60-Day Deadline Ran Out — and the U.S. and Iran Are Still at War

Back in June, the U.S. and Iran gave themselves 60 days to end their fighting. Today the clock hit zero with no deal, and President Trump said he will not extend it — while warning the country of Oman not to "get in the way" near one of the most important stretches of water on Earth.

Why this mattersOil is not just for cars. It is in the plastic of your water bottle, the fuel of the plane that flies you to see family, and the cost of shipping food to Vancouver grocery stores. When one narrow channel gets risky, prices move everywhere — including here.
Read the whole story

Imagine two people in an argument who agree to a rule: "We have 60 days to work this out." That is roughly what the United States and Iran did in June, when they signed a memorandum of understanding — a written promise to try for a deal that would end their conflict.

Today, August 17, that 60-day clock ran out. There is no deal. President Trump said he is not interested in extending the deadline, which means the fighting is likely to continue.

Then things got tenser. Trump warned that the U.S. might strike Oman — a small country on the Arabian Peninsula that is normally a friend of the United States — if it "gets in the way" in the Strait of Hormuz.

Here is why that strait matters so much. Look at a map of the Persian Gulf: the only way out to the open ocean is a narrow gap of water between Iran and Oman. It is about 33 km wide at its tightest point — roughly the distance from downtown Vancouver to Abbotsford. Around a fifth of all the oil the world uses squeezes through that gap on giant tankers.

So when countries start threatening each other there, traders around the world get nervous, and the price of oil goes up. Today Brent crude — one of the main types of oil people buy and sell — climbed to about $89 a barrel. The Dow Jones and the S&P 500 (two big scoreboards for American company stocks) each dropped about half a percent.

Words worth knowing
  • Memorandum of understanding — A written promise between two sides to try to reach a real agreement. It is a serious handshake, not yet a signed contract.
  • Chokepoint — A narrow passage that lots of traffic must squeeze through — so whoever controls it has enormous power.
  • Barrel (of oil) — The standard unit oil is priced in — about 159 litres, or roughly two bathtubs full.

  1. You promise to finish something by Friday and you do not. Should there be a punishment?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes

    • A promise nobody has to keep is not really a promise.
    • If nothing ever happens, next time people will not try as hard.
    • The person waiting for you gave something up too.

    No

    • Some jobs turn out much harder than they looked when you promised.
    • Punishing someone can make them stop trying at all.
    • What matters is whether they really tried, not the date on the calendar.

  2. Oman is America's friend. Is it ever alright to say something frightening to a friend?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Sometimes it is

    • Telling a friend the hard truth early is kinder than surprising them later.
    • Saying it out loud can stop a mix-up that would hurt somebody.
    • Friends can disagree, even strongly, and still be friends.

    No

    • A friend who is afraid of you stops trusting you.
    • Your other friends are watching and wondering if they are next.
    • You can say exactly the same thing kindly, and it still works.

  3. One small gap in the sea decides the price of oil for the whole world. Is that a good way for the world to work?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    No, it is too risky

    • If that one place breaks, everybody's things get more expensive.
    • It gives one country enormous power over everyone else.
    • Sensible plans always keep a second way of doing things.

    It is just where the sea is

    • Nobody chose where the water is, or where the oil is.
    • People have already built other roads and pipes around it.
    • Because everyone needs it, almost everyone wants it kept open.

  1. A deadline passed and nothing happened. Was the deadline worth setting at all?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes — it did real work

    • For 60 days both sides were talking instead of only fighting. That is not nothing.
    • A deadline creates a public record. Everyone can now see who moved and who did not.
    • Hard problems rarely get solved on the first try. A failed attempt still narrows down what might work.

    No — it was for show

    • A deadline with no consequence is just a date. Nothing was actually at stake when it passed.
    • It let both sides look busy for two months while the fighting continued anyway.
    • Break a promise once and the next promise is worth less. That cost is real and lasts.

  2. Oman is supposed to be a friend of the U.S. Is it ever smart to threaten your own friend?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Sometimes yes

    • Countries act on their interests, not their feelings. Friendship does not cancel that.
    • A clear warning is cheaper than an actual attack — it tells the other side exactly where the line is.
    • Vagueness causes accidents. Saying the hard thing out loud can prevent a misunderstanding that kills people.

    No — it costs more than it gains

    • A friend who feels threatened starts quietly looking for other partners.
    • Other allies are watching. If this is how friends get treated, your promises to them are worth less too.
    • It can push a country that wanted to stay neutral onto the other side — the opposite of what you wanted.

  3. One narrow channel carries a fifth of the world's oil. Is that a flaw in how the world is set up?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes — it's a single point of failure

    • Anything important that has only one route is fragile. Engineers build backups for exactly this reason.
    • It hands one country enormous leverage over billions of people who never got a vote.
    • Everyone has known about this risk for fifty years and it still has not been fixed.

    No — geography isn't a design

    • Nobody drew that map. Oil happens to be there and the sea happens to be shaped that way.
    • The world has adapted: pipelines that go around it, emergency reserves, suppliers on other continents.
    • Because everyone depends on it, almost everyone — including Iran — has a reason to keep it open. Shared need is its own kind of safety.

China's Factories Made Less Than Expected — Except the Robot Parts

Every month, China tells the world how much its factories made and how much people bought. This month both numbers were smaller than the experts guessed. But one number jumped up a lot: the parts that go inside computers and robots.

Why this mattersLook around your room. A lot of what you can see was probably made in China. When its factories change what they make, the toys, bikes and gadgets in your shops change too — and so do their prices.
Read the whole story

China is where an enormous amount of the world's stuff gets made — phones, bikes, toys, electric cars. Every month it publishes a kind of report card.

This month most of the grades slipped a little. The factories made 4.5% more than in the same month last year. That is still more than before, but less more than people expected.

People also bought less in shops than expected. When making and buying both slow down at the same time, grown-ups start to worry.

But one line went up by more than 19%: the electronic parts that go inside computers, big servers and robots. So China is not simply slowing down. It is making fewer of the old things, and many more of the new ones.

Words worth knowing
  • Factory — A big building where machines and people make things in very large numbers.
  • Percent — A way of saying how much something changed. Up 5% means that for every 100 you had, you now have 105.

China's Economy Is Slowing — but the Robot-and-Chip Part Is Booming

China published its July numbers today. Factories and shops did worse than economists expected — but hidden inside the disappointing report was one number that jumped more than 19%: electronic equipment.

Why this mattersChina builds a huge share of the world's phones, toys, bikes, and EVs. When its factories slow, prices and product choices change in stores from Richmond to Rome. And the part that is booming — AI chips and machines — is exactly the thing the U.S. and China are competing hardest over.
Read the whole story

Every month, China releases a kind of report card for its economy. Today's covered July, and most of the grades slipped.

Industrial production — how much stuff the country's factories actually made — grew 4.5% compared to July last year. Growing is good, but that was slower than June, the first slowdown in three months, and less than experts had predicted. Retail sales, meaning how much people bought in shops and online, also came in under forecast.

Why does that worry people? Because for years China's growth engine was: build lots of things, sell lots of things. If both of those slow down at once, the whole engine sounds rough.

But here is the interesting twist. One line in the report shot up more than 19%: output of electronic equipment — chips, servers, the machines that AI runs on. Advanced manufacturing, high-tech investment, services, and tourists coming into the country are quietly becoming the new engine.

So the story is not simply "China is slowing down." It is closer to: China is switching engines mid-flight, and the old engine is losing power faster than the new one is gaining it.

Words worth knowing
  • Industrial production — A measure of how much a country's factories, mines, and utilities actually produced. Think of it as the nation's output odometer.
  • Retail sales — The total amount people spent buying things. It shows whether ordinary shoppers feel confident or cautious.
  • Missing forecasts — When the real number comes in below what economists predicted. The gap between expectation and reality often moves markets more than the number itself.

  1. One subject up 19%, everything else slipping. Is that a good report card?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes — it depends which subject

    • Not all subjects count equally. Being excellent at the one that decides your future beats being average at all of them.
    • The parts that are growing are the parts that will still be growing in ten years.
    • A number that is falling from a high level can still be a big number. 4.5% growth is growth.

    No — you can't live on one subject

    • Chip factories employ far fewer people than shops and ordinary factories do. The slipping parts are where most people work.
    • The weak numbers are happening now; the strong one is a bet on later.
    • One spectacular number is exactly what you would point at if you wanted to distract from the rest.

  2. Should a country try to make everything itself, or specialize in what it does best?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Specialize

    • Nobody can be best at everything. Doing a few things extremely well makes them cheaper and better for everyone.
    • Trading means you get things you could never have made yourself.
    • Countries that trade a lot with each other have more to lose from fighting — which makes fighting less likely.

    Make it yourself

    • If relations break down, the things you cannot make — chips, medicine, food — become the things you cannot get.
    • Depending on someone is a form of giving them power over you.
    • Factories are also jobs. A town that loses its factory does not get the lost work back just because prices fell.

  3. If people have stopped spending, should the government just hand them money?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes — put it in people's hands

    • Money given to people gets spent in shops, which keeps shops open and people employed.
    • Ordinary people know what they actually need better than a government planner does.
    • It reaches those with the least the fastest — they are the ones who spend it rather than store it.

    No — build something instead

    • If people are worried about the future, handed money often gets saved, not spent — and then it did nothing.
    • More money chasing the same goods can simply push prices up.
    • The same money spent on factories, labs, and railways is still there in twenty years. A one-time payment is gone in a month.

A Company That Loses Money Might Be Worth More Than Any Other

OpenAI is the company that made ChatGPT. Soon, ordinary people will be able to buy small pieces of it. The strange part: it loses an enormous amount of money every year, and people still want to buy.

Why this mattersThe computers and power stations being built right now for AI will still be there when you are choosing a job. What people are betting on today is whether AI becomes as ordinary as electricity.
Read the whole story

When a company lets ordinary people buy little pieces of it, those pieces are called shares. OpenAI is getting ready to sell shares, maybe as soon as next month.

People think all the pieces together are worth about one trillion dollars. A trillion is very hard to picture. If you counted one dollar every second and never slept, it would take you more than thirty thousand years.

Here is the strange bit. Every year, OpenAI spends about 14 billion dollars more than it earns. Normally, losing money makes something worth less, not more.

But people are not paying for what it earns today. They are guessing about later. Building AI means buying huge numbers of computer chips and paying gigantic electricity bills first, and hoping the customers arrive afterwards. Another company, Anthropic, does the same kind of work and has just started earning more than it spends. Two companies, two different plans. In a few years we will find out which one was smarter.

Words worth knowing
  • Share — One small piece of a company. If you own one, a tiny bit of that company belongs to you.
  • Profit — The money left over after everything has been paid for. A company can be famous and huge and still have none.

A Company Losing $14 Billion a Year May Be Worth $1 Trillion

OpenAI, the company behind ChatGPT, is preparing to sell shares to the public — possibly as soon as September — at a value above $1 trillion. The strange part: it loses roughly $14 billion every year. Meanwhile its rival Anthropic just turned its first profit.

Why this mattersThis is not just finance news — it is a bet about whether AI becomes as ordinary as electricity. The chips, power plants, and data centres being built right now will still be there when Lucas is choosing a career.
Read the whole story

When a private company decides to let ordinary people buy pieces of it on the stock market, that is called an IPO — an initial public offering. Today's news is that OpenAI is getting ready for one, possibly in September, at a valuation north of $1 trillion.

A trillion is hard to picture. If you counted one dollar every second without sleeping, a trillion seconds would take you about 31,700 years.

Now the puzzle: OpenAI loses around $14 billion a year. Normally, losing money makes a company less valuable, not more. So why would anyone pay?

Because investors are not buying what OpenAI earns today — they are betting on what it might earn later. Building AI means buying enormous numbers of computer chips and paying gigantic electricity bills first, and hoping the customers show up after. Nvidia, the company that makes most of those chips, is reportedly preparing to back roughly $100 billion in OpenAI financing. Microsoft, meanwhile, is running into a very physical wall: there is only so much land and electricity available for new data centres.

And here is the detail worth remembering: Anthropic, OpenAI's competitor, just turned its first profit. Two companies, same industry, opposite strategies — one spending enormously to grow fast, one earning money now. In a few years we will find out which bet was smarter. That is a real experiment happening in public, and Lucas gets to watch it from the start.

Words worth knowing
  • IPO (initial public offering) — The first time a private company sells shares of itself to the general public on a stock exchange.
  • Valuation — What people collectively think a company is worth — which can be very different from how much money it actually has.
  • Profit — Money left over after all costs are paid. A company can be famous, huge, and still have none.

  1. Would you spend your pocket money on something that might be worth a lot later, or on something you can enjoy today?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Later

    • Waiting can turn a small amount into a big one.
    • The thing you want now, you will probably still want later.
    • Learning to wait is useful for the rest of your life.

    Today

    • You cannot be sure the later thing will actually happen.
    • Being the age you are now only happens once.
    • A small sure thing can beat a big maybe.

  2. Two companies do the same job. One spends enormous amounts to grow fast; the other earns money now. Which would you rather run?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Grow fast

    • If you become the biggest, everyone uses you and nobody catches up.
    • You can always start earning later; you cannot always catch up later.
    • Big new things usually cost a lot before they pay anything back.

    Earn now

    • If you never earn anything, one day the money runs out.
    • Earning proves that people really want what you made.
    • When you pay for yourself, you do not have to ask anyone's permission.

  3. AI computers use as much electricity as a whole town. Should a town let one be built nearby?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes

    • It brings jobs, and money for schools and roads.
    • It is going to be built somewhere anyway.
    • Saying no does not make the world use less electricity.

    No

    • Everyone shares the electricity and the water, so everyone's bills can go up.
    • It needs fewer workers than people expect.
    • Once it is built, the town cannot change its mind.

There is no right answer to any of these. Say what you think first — then open the hints. Both sides are argued as well as they can be, so the hints won't decide for you. You still have to pick, and say why.

  1. Would you put your own allowance into a company that loses money now but might be enormous later?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes — you're buying the future

    • Amazon lost money for years and the people who held on did extremely well.
    • By the time a company is obviously safe, the cheap price is gone.
    • You can only lose what you put in, but the gain has no ceiling.

    No — most of them don't make it

    • We remember Amazon because it worked. We forget the hundreds of companies that burned money and vanished.
    • "Might be huge later" is a story. $14 billion of losses a year is a fact.
    • When you have a small amount of money, losing all of it hurts far more than a big win would help.

  2. Anthropic makes a profit; OpenAI does not but is worth more. What are investors actually paying for?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    For size later — and that's rational

    • A share is a claim on all future earnings, not this year's. Today's loss is an investment in being bigger.
    • In tech, the largest player often ends up taking most of the market. Second place is worth far less than half of first.
    • Spending heavily now on chips and data centres is how you make sure you are the one who gets that position.

    For a story — and crowds get carried away

    • Profit is something you can check. A projection is something someone chose to believe.
    • This has happened before: railways, radio, dot-coms. Each time the technology was real and the prices were still too high.
    • When everyone is buying because everyone else is buying, the price stops being about the company at all.

  3. An AI data centre needs a town's worth of electricity. Should a town let one be built nearby?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes — take the deal

    • It brings construction work, permanent jobs, and tax money that pays for schools and roads.
    • It is getting built somewhere regardless. Better that the benefits land here.
    • Turning it away does not reduce the world's electricity use by one watt.

    No — the costs are shared, the gains aren't

    • Power and water are shared. If demand jumps, everyone's bill can rise, including people who wanted no part of it.
    • Data centres need far fewer workers than a factory of the same size. The jobs promised are often fewer than the jobs described.
    • Once it is built, the town has very little say over what happens next.
