A Hole Found in the Code That Runs Most of the Internet — and a Race to Patch It Before Hackers Do

On October 21, 2026, security researchers revealed a serious flaw in PHP, the programming language that quietly powers an estimated seven out of ten websites on Earth. The bug, nicknamed 'PHP2Shell' in news coverage, could let an attacker seize control of an affected server. The FBI told organisations to patch immediately or take vulnerable systems offline, and companies including Amazon, Google and OpenAI scrambled to fix their own systems within hours.

Why this mattersEven if you've never written a line of code, the websites you use every day — for school, shopping, banking, games — very likely rely on PHP somewhere in the background, which is why a flaw like this is less like a crack in one house and more like a crack in the foundation material used across an entire neighbourhood. It's also an early, concrete example of AI changing the pace of cybersecurity itself, for both the people trying to break in and the people trying to keep them out.
Read the whole story

Most people have never heard of PHP, but almost everyone has used something built with it. It is a programming language written in the 1990s that handles the behind-the-scenes work of websites — pulling your order history on a shopping site, checking your password when you log in, deciding what to show you on a news feed. It runs quietly under huge chunks of the web, including many government, business and media sites, which is why a serious flaw in it is not a niche problem but a structural one.

The flaw discovered this week was severe because of what it allowed: an attacker who found the right weak spot could potentially run their own commands on someone else's server, essentially taking over a machine that was never supposed to be theirs to control. Researchers reportedly used AI tools to help find the exact weakness, which is itself part of the story — the same kind of artificial intelligence that can write an email can now help both defenders and attackers comb through code looking for cracks, and it can do it far faster than a human alone.

That speed is why the response was so urgent. The FBI's Cyber Division — the branch of the agency that deals with digital crime rather than physical crime — issued an advisory telling organisations to 'immediately update affected PHP installations,' and said that if a fix wasn't ready yet, the safer move was to disconnect the vulnerable system entirely rather than leave it exposed. That is an unusually blunt instruction; agencies don't often tell businesses to simply switch things off.

Big technology companies did not wait around. Amazon, Google and OpenAI reportedly patched their own affected systems within hours of the flaw becoming public, which is fast by the standards of large organisations, where updates normally go through layers of testing before anything changes. The hurry reflects a new worry in cybersecurity: if AI can help attackers find and exploit a weakness automatically, the window between 'the flaw is public' and 'someone is already using it' might shrink from weeks to hours.

As of this reporting, no confirmed large-scale break-ins tied to the flaw had been made public. That matters, because it means this is a story about prevention, not disaster — the race to lock the door before anyone tries the handle, rather than a report of a robbery that already happened. Whether that race was won will become clear over the coming weeks, as security researchers watch for signs that the flaw was used against targets who were too slow to patch.

  1. Should AI tools capable of finding security flaws in software be developed and shared openly, or kept tightly restricted?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Share it openly

    • Open access lets defenders everywhere, not just large companies, find and fix flaws in their own systems before attackers do.

    Yes, require it

    • A hard deadline forces organisations to treat security as urgent rather than something to schedule around other priorities.
    • When a flaw is this widespread, slow patching by even a few major systems creates risk for everyone connected to them.
    • Clear legal rules give security teams inside a company the authority to demand resources and attention immediately, rather than waiting for approval.

    No, keep it flexible

    • Rushed patches, applied without proper testing, can break other parts of a system and sometimes cause more damage than the original flaw.
    • Organisations vary hugely in size and complexity, and a deadline that works for a small website may be unrealistic for a hospital's medical records system.

Tech Company Stock Prices Take a Wild Rollercoaster Ride

This week, the prices of many big technology companies' stocks jumped up and then crashed down. People who own tiny pieces of these companies are nervous about one thing: the huge amount of money being spent on artificial intelligence, or AI. Even companies that made more money than expected still saw their stock prices fall hard.

Why this mattersLots of families have money saved for the future in things connected to the stock market, like retirement accounts, even if they don't realize it. When tech stocks swing wildly, it can affect how much money is in those accounts. It also affects which new technologies get built, since companies need money to keep inventing things you might use someday.
Read the whole story

Imagine a company is like a big pizza. You can buy a tiny slice of that pizza, called a share. If people think the pizza company will grow and make more pizzas, your slice becomes worth more money. If people get worried, your slice can be worth less, even quickly.

This week, slices of big tech companies went on a rollercoaster. One day, stock markets jumped up. The very next day, they dropped hard. Companies that make computer chips for AI, like Nvidia and AMD, lost a lot of value. Even Meta, the company behind Facebook and Instagram, saw its stock fall 8% in a single day, even though it had actually made more money than people expected.

Why would a successful company's slices get cheaper? Because AI needs enormous data centers; giant buildings packed with powerful computers that use huge amounts of electricity. Building and running these centers costs an incredible amount of money, like buying thousands of expensive video game consoles and leaving them running all day and night.

The boss of Nvidia, a man named Jensen Huang, said there isn't enough money available for everyone who wants to build these AI computer centers. That worried investors. They started wondering if companies are spending money faster than they can make it back, kind of like spending your whole piggy bank on toys before you know if you'll actually like them.

The AI Boom Hits a Nervous Patch on Wall Street

US stock markets lurched up and then down this week as investors got cold feet about the staggering cost of building artificial intelligence. The Nasdaq Composite, a stock index that tracks mostly tech companies, fell 2.9% on Thursday after a strong rally the day before, and even a company with excellent results, Meta Platforms, saw its shares drop 8%. The worry wasn't bad news about any single company — it was a sudden question hanging over the whole industry: can anyone actually afford all this?

Why this mattersIf you or your parents have money in a pension, a retirement account, or an index fund, there's a good chance a slice of it is tied up in exactly these companies, since AI-related stocks have been one of the biggest drivers of the entire stock market's growth. A sustained slowdown in AI spending wouldn't just hit tech workers — it could ripple into electricity prices, construction jobs building data centers, and the pace at which AI tools show up in schools, hospitals, and everyday apps.
Read the whole story

On Wednesday, strong earnings from companies linked to SpaceX and Tesla sent stocks climbing. By Thursday the mood had flipped entirely. The Nasdaq Composite — an index, or running scoreboard, that adds up the share prices of thousands of companies, heavily weighted toward tech — dropped 2.9% in a single day. The broader S&P 500, which tracks 500 of the largest US companies across every industry, fell 1.4%. These are large one-day moves for indexes that normally shift by fractions of a percent.

The strangest part of the day was Meta Platforms, the company behind Facebook and Instagram. Meta's results actually beat what Wall Street analysts had expected, and the company even gave upbeat guidance — its own forecast of how well it expects to perform in the months ahead. Normally that would send a stock up. Instead, Meta's shares fell 8%. Investors weren't doubting Meta's current business; they were worried about how much money Meta and its rivals are pouring into AI, and whether that spending will ever pay for itself.

Chipmakers took an even harder hit. Advanced Micro Devices fell more than 8%, Micron Technology dropped 6%, and Nvidia — the company whose chips power most of the world's AI systems — fell 4%. These companies make the hardware inside data centers, the huge warehouses full of computers that train and run AI programs. If investors think the AI buildout might slow down, the companies selling the shovels get hit even before the companies doing the digging. Shares in power and energy firms that supply electricity to data centers also fell, since those businesses only make sense if the data centers keep expanding.

Nvidia's own chief executive, Jensen Huang, put his finger on the underlying fear. 'There's a scarcity of capital,' he said — capital meaning the pool of money that banks, investors, and companies have available to lend or spend. 'The demand [for] the capital is much greater than the availability of the capital.' In plain terms: the amount of money companies want to spend building AI infrastructure is growing faster than the amount of money the financial system can comfortably supply, which raises the cost of borrowing and the risk that some bets won't pay off.

What makes this moment different from a normal bad day in the market is what it isn't about. Nobody is saying AI doesn't work, or that these companies are badly run. The question investors are suddenly asking out loud is whether the entire industry is spending money faster than it can be justified by what AI actually earns back — a reassessment of the whole boom, not a verdict on any one company's quarter.

Supreme Court Says: Don't Stop This Medical Care in Prisons Yet

On Wednesday, the United States Supreme Court made a decision about transgender people in federal prisons. Transgender people are people whose gender does not match the sex they were given at birth. The Court said the government must keep giving them certain medical care while a bigger court case about it keeps going.

Why this mattersThis story is about how courts can press 'pause' on a big decision while they figure out what's fair, kind of like a referee stopping a game to check a replay before deciding the call. It also shows how government rules can change, and how people who disagree with a rule can ask a judge to look at it closely before it takes effect.

The government, led by President Trump, wanted to stop giving special medical care to transgender people locked up in federal prisons. This care helps their bodies match their gender.

A court case started over whether the government is allowed to stop it. That case is still being argued. It could take a long time to finish.

The Supreme Court is the highest court in the country. It did not decide who is right yet. It just said: keep the care going for now, until the case is settled.

Supreme Court Blocks Trump Administration From Cutting Off Care to Transgender Federal Inmates — For Now

On Wednesday, the US Supreme Court ruled that the Trump administration must keep providing gender-affirming medical care to transgender people held in federal prisons while a lawsuit over the policy plays out in lower courts. The administration had been trying to end that care as part of a wider push to roll back federal recognition of transgender people. The ruling doesn't settle who's ultimately right — it just freezes things in place until the case is fully argued.

Why this mattersThis case is a window into how the US legal system actually slows down sudden changes in government policy — courts can freeze a decision in place while they work out whether it's lawful, rather than letting either side win by acting fastest. It also shows that a win in court right now isn't the end of a legal fight; it's often just one stage in a case that could run for years.
Read the whole story

Federal prisons in the US have, for several years, provided hormone therapy and other medical treatment to transgender inmates who request it and whose doctors recommend it, under a policy adopted by the Bureau of Prisons, the federal agency that runs the prison system. Earlier this year, the Trump administration moved to end that policy, arguing it was not required to provide this kind of care. Transgender inmates and civil rights groups sued, saying cutting off ongoing treatment could cause serious medical harm.

A lower federal court agreed to pause the administration's change while the lawsuit continues, meaning the old policy — keep providing the care — stayed in effect. The administration asked the Supreme Court to lift that pause so it could stop the care immediately. On Wednesday, the Court said no. The care keeps flowing while the legal fight continues.

It's worth being precise about what this ruling actually is. The Supreme Court did not decide whether the administration's underlying policy is constitutional or lawful — that question is still being argued in lower courts and could take months or years to resolve, and might eventually come back to the Supreme Court for a final answer. What the justices did this week was issue an injunction, a court order that keeps things as they currently are, used specifically to prevent harm that couldn't be undone if a court later ruled the other way. If a transgender inmate's hormone therapy had been cut off and the courts later found the cutoff illegal, that interruption couldn't simply be reversed after the fact — which is the kind of situation these emergency orders exist for.

This case sits inside a bigger story. Since taking office, the Trump administration has issued a series of executive orders and policy changes aimed at removing federal recognition and support for transgender people — covering passports, the military, school sports, and now prison healthcare. Supporters of these changes argue the federal government shouldn't be in the business of funding or facilitating gender transition, especially using taxpayer money for people convicted of crimes. Opponents argue that courts have long required prisons to provide medically necessary care to the people they incarcerate, and that gender-affirming treatment for some transgender inmates falls into that category under existing medical guidelines.

The specific reasoning the justices gave, and how each of them voted, wasn't fully detailed in the news coverage available — these emergency rulings are sometimes issued with limited or no written explanation, unlike the Court's major decisions after full argument. What's clear is the practical result: the policy stands for now, the lawsuit keeps moving through lower courts, and this almost certainly isn't the last time this dispute reaches the Supreme Court.

  • injunction — A court order that pauses an action or keeps a situation unchanged until a legal dispute about it is resolved.
  • Bureau of Prisons — The US federal agency responsible for running the country's federal prison system, as opposed to state or local jails.
