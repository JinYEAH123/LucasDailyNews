Can a Small Town Sue Big Oil Companies Over Storms and Heat?

The biggest court in the United States started listening to a huge argument this week. A small county in Colorado wants to sue giant oil companies, saying they should help pay for damage caused by climate change, like floods and wildfires. The oil companies say that is not fair, and that only the whole country's government should handle something as big as climate change.

Why this mattersIf your town ever had to fix a flooded street or a burned park, someone had to pay for it, usually out of tax money that families like yours give to the government. This case is about whether towns can ask the companies that make oil and gas to help pay instead, or whether that decision belongs only to the national government.
Read the whole story

Boulder County is a place in Colorado with mountains, rivers, and towns. Lately it has had worse floods, wildfires, and heat waves. Fixing roads and bridges after these costs a lot of money.

Boulder's leaders say the fires and floods are partly caused by climate change, and that big oil companies like ExxonMobil knew for decades that burning oil and gas warms the planet. They say the companies kept quiet about it and should now help pay the bill.

The oil companies say that's not how it should work. They say climate change affects the whole world, so it should only be handled by the federal government's rules, not by hundreds of different towns each suing in their own local courts.

The nine judges on the Supreme Court listened to both sides argue. Some judges seemed worried about allowing too many lawsuits like this one. Others seemed worried about leaving towns with no way to get help paying for damage. Their final decision could take months, but it will affect similar lawsuits already waiting in other cities, like New York and Honolulu.

  • lawsuit — When one person or group asks a court to decide that someone else caused harm and should have to pay for it.

Can a Colorado County Sue Big Oil for Climate Damage? The Supreme Court Just Heard the Case

On October 5, the U.S. Supreme Court opened its new term with a case that could decide whether cities and counties can sue oil companies in their own state courts for the local costs of climate change — think buckled roads, wildfire damage, and flood repairs. Boulder County, Colorado wants Exxon and Suncor to help pay for that, arguing the companies knew decades ago that burning fossil fuels caused warming and hid it. The companies say climate change is a national and global problem that only federal regulators can handle, not a patchwork of local courts. Hundreds of similar lawsuits across the country are waiting to see how this one turns out.

Why this mattersWherever you live, your local government pays for roads, flood control, and emergency response — and if courts say companies can be billed for climate-related damage, that could eventually affect insurance costs, gas prices, or local taxes everywhere, not just in Boulder. It's also a case about a much bigger question you'll keep running into: who pays when the harm from something is spread across the whole world but the lawsuit is filed in one small place.
Read the whole story

Boulder County and the city of Boulder sued Exxon and Suncor years ago, arguing that the companies' own internal research showed they understood as early as the 1960s and '70s that burning oil and gas would warm the planet — and that they spent decades publicly casting doubt on that science anyway. The lawsuit doesn't ask a court to regulate emissions or shut down drilling. It asks for money: compensation for local costs like heat-damaged roads, wildfire risk, and flood infrastructure that Boulder says it now has to pay for because of a warming climate the companies allegedly helped cause while misleading the public about it.

Exxon and Suncor's defense isn't that climate change didn't happen or that their products didn't contribute to it. It's a jurisdictional argument: climate change is an inherently global problem caused by emissions from billions of sources worldwide, they say, and Congress already built a national system — through the Clean Air Act and the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), the federal agency responsible for regulating pollution — to handle exactly this kind of issue. Letting a single county's state court judge and jury decide how much a global oil company owes for the planet's weather, the companies argue, invites chaos: thousands of different juries in thousands of different states could reach wildly different verdicts for the same underlying conduct.

The justices' questions during oral argument suggested a court genuinely split, not along the usual predictable lines. Some justices pushed back hard on the idea that any single state court could put a price tag on a planetary problem, worried about opening a floodgate — other lawsuits are already pending from New York City, Honolulu, and several California cities and counties, all making similar arguments. Other justices pushed back just as hard on the companies, asking whether ruling against Boulder would mean local governments have no remedy at all, even if a jury found the companies had deliberately deceived the public for financial gain.

This is the first case of its kind to reach the Supreme Court, which matters beyond Boulder itself. If the justices rule that these climate-liability lawsuits can proceed in state courts, companies across the energy industry could face a wave of trials and potentially enormous payouts, similar in scale to the lawsuits that eventually forced tobacco companies into a historic settlement in the 1990s after similar claims that they had hidden what they knew about cigarettes and cancer. If the justices rule the other way — that only federal law and federal regulators can handle this — it would likely shut down most or all of the pending suits nationwide in one stroke, redirecting the entire fight over climate accountability away from courtrooms and toward Congress and federal agencies instead.

A decision isn't expected for months. But however it comes out, it will set the rules for how climate costs get paid for in America going forward — whether that burden falls on local taxpayers, on the companies whose products caused the emissions, or gets left to future federal lawmaking that may or may not happen.

Words worth knowing
  • state court — A court that handles cases under a particular state's own laws, separate from the federal court system that handles national law.
  • EPA (Environmental Protection Agency) — The U.S. federal agency responsible for writing and enforcing national rules about pollution and environmental protection.
  • climate liability lawsuit — A legal case that asks a court to make a company pay money for damage caused by climate change, usually by arguing the company knew the risks and hid them.

  1. Should local governments be allowed to sue oil companies in state court for climate damages, or should this only be handled through federal regulation?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Let local courts hear it

    • Local governments bear real, measurable costs from climate change right now, and federal regulators haven't compensated them for any of it.

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes, liability should apply

    • Legality at the time doesn't excuse a company from responsibility if it had specific knowledge of serious harm and chose to conceal it rather than warn the public.
    • The tobacco industry's internal research-versus-public-statements pattern set a precedent that concealment itself can be the basis for liability, independent of whether the product was legal.

    • Companies operating lawfully, under the regulations that existed at the time, made business decisions based on the rules and scientific consensus then available, not what's known decades later.
    • Judging 1960s and '70s conduct by today's climate science and today's moral standards risks punishing companies for not foreseeing what wasn't yet fully established or agreed upon.
    • If courts can impose liability for any past conduct later deemed harmful with hindsight, it creates enormous uncertainty for any industry trying to operate within current law today.

The US Bought Way More Than It Sold Last Month

In August, people and companies in the United States bought much more from other countries than they sold to them. The gap between buying and selling grew to $105.6 billion, the biggest gap in over a year. That's surprising, because new taxes on foreign goods called tariffs were supposed to shrink this gap, not grow it.

Why this mattersWhen tariffs change how much things cost to bring into the country, it can affect the price of things your family buys, like shoes, toys, or a new phone. It can also affect interest rates, which change how expensive it is for grown-ups to borrow money for a car or a house. So numbers like this one, even though they sound like grown-up business talk, can end up changing what things cost at your own kitchen table.
Read the whole story

Every month, people and companies in the US buy things from other countries, like oil, computer parts, and clothes. They also sell things to other countries, like airplanes and soybeans. When the buying is bigger than the selling, it's called a trade deficit.

New numbers show this gap grew to $105.6 billion in August. If you split that amount evenly among every single person in the US, each person's share would be about $310. That's more money than most kids get in a whole year of allowance.

Last year, the US government added tariffs, which are extra costs on things bought from other countries. The goal was to make people buy more things made in America instead, which would shrink the gap. For a little while, it worked. But now the gap is bigger than it was before the tariffs even started.

Why did it bounce back? Companies bought lots of oil and big machines from overseas, while selling American products to other countries grew more slowly. Some experts think companies rushed to buy things from abroad before new tariffs could raise prices even more. This number matters to the Federal Reserve too, the group that decides things like how expensive it is to borrow money, because they watch the trade gap closely when making those choices.

Words worth knowing
  • trade deficit — A trade deficit is when a country buys more stuff from other countries than it sells to them.
  • tariff — A tariff is an extra cost added to things bought from another country, kind of like a toll you pay to bring something across a border.

America's Trade Gap Just Hit a One-Year High — Right After Tariffs Were Supposed to Shrink It

New government data shows the U.S. bought $105.6 billion more from the rest of the world than it sold in August, the widest trade gap in over a year. That's awkward timing for the Trump administration, which imposed sweeping tariffs last year partly to argue they would shrink exactly this kind of gap. Instead, the numbers show it stretching back out.

Why this mattersEvery time a country buys more than it sells, the gap has to be paid for somehow, through foreign investment, borrowing, or the dollar's value shifting — and this report is the government's own scorecard on whether a major economic experiment is working. If you've seen prices change on imported electronics, clothes, or car parts, tariffs and trade numbers like this one are part of why.
Read the whole story

Every month, the U.S. Census Bureau counts up everything Americans bought from abroad and everything they sold abroad, then publishes the difference in a report called the FT900. When imports outpace exports, as they almost always do for the U.S., the difference is called the trade deficit. In August it jumped to $105.6 billion — the widest it's been since just before new tariffs (taxes charged on imported goods, paid by the companies bringing them into the country) took effect last year.

That timing matters because tariffs were sold, in part, as a fix for this exact number. The logic was simple: make imports more expensive, and Americans will buy fewer of them, narrowing the gap between what the country buys and sells. For a while, it looked like it might be working — the deficit did shrink after the tariffs landed. But August's numbers suggest that was a temporary effect, not a permanent fix.

Economists point to two things driving the rebound. First, rising imports of oil and what are called capital goods — the machinery, equipment, and industrial parts that businesses use to make other things, rather than goods a shopper buys directly. Second, export growth has been sluggish, so even as imports climbed, sales of American-made goods abroad didn't keep pace. On top of that, many companies appear to have front-loaded their importing last year — rushing shipments in before tariffs hit or finding new routes and suppliers to soften the blow — and that kind of adjustment tends to wear off over time, letting old import patterns creep back.

This single data release won't settle the argument over whether tariffs work, but it does complicate the administration's case. Supporters can point to the short-lived dip in the deficit as proof the policy had some effect. Critics can point to the current widening as proof that effect was temporary, and that businesses simply rerouted around the tariffs rather than genuinely buying less from abroad.

The number also matters beyond the tariff debate. Economists and the Federal Reserve — the U.S. central bank, which sets interest rates partly based on how fast or slow the economy is growing — treat the trade deficit as one ingredient in calculating GDP (gross domestic product, the total value of everything the country produces). A wider deficit, taken on its own, can subtract from measured growth, giving the Fed one more thing to weigh when it decides whether to raise, cut, or hold interest rates in the months ahead.

Words worth knowing
  • Trade deficit — The gap that appears when a country buys more goods and services from abroad than it sells to other countries.
  • Tariff — A tax charged on goods brought in from another country, usually paid by the company importing them.
  • Front-loading — Rushing to buy or import something early, often to beat a price increase or new rule before it takes effect.

Scientist Wins Nobel Prize for Catching Invisible Particles Frozen in Antarctic Ice

A scientist named Francis Halzen just won one of the biggest prizes in the world: the Nobel Prize in Physics. He won it for spending decades building a giant detector buried deep in the ice at the South Pole, used to catch tiny, ghost-like particles called neutrinos. These particles are almost impossible to see, but they carry secret messages from explosions happening across the universe.

Why this mattersEvery second of your life, your own body is being passed through by huge numbers of these invisible particles, yet you never notice. This prize is a reminder that some of the biggest discoveries come from patiently studying things nobody can even see, buried in one of the coldest, most faraway places on Earth.
Read the whole story

Francis Halzen was born in Belgium and spent much of his life chasing something almost nobody can see: a tiny particle called a neutrino. Neutrinos have barely any mass and no electric charge, which means they zoom straight through almost everything — walls, planets, even you — without stopping or bumping into anything.

Right now, trillions of neutrinos from the Sun and from deep space are passing through your body. You cannot feel a single one. That is exactly why they are so hard to study, but also why scientists want to study them: they travel in a straight line from wherever they were made, carrying clues about violent cosmic events like exploding stars and giant black holes.

To catch a few of these shy particles, Halzen helped build something enormous called IceCube. It is a cube of Antarctic ice one kilometer on each side — imagine a block of ice big enough to hold 400,000 Olympic swimming pools. Buried inside that ice are thousands of light sensors, waiting for the rare moment a neutrino crashes into an ice atom and makes a tiny flash of light.

Using those flashes, scientists have traced some neutrinos all the way back to specific galaxies far outside our own. That gives astronomers a brand new way to study the universe, alongside regular telescopes that only use light. The Nobel committee said this work helps explain some of the most powerful, least understood events happening in space.

  • observatory — A place built specially for watching and studying things in space, like stars, explosions, or in this case, invisible particles.

A Telescope Made of Ice: Physicist Wins Nobel for Catching Ghost Particles

Francis Halzen, a Belgian-born physicist, has won the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physics for spending decades chasing a particle so slippery that trillions of them pass through your body every second without you noticing. His tool was not a telescope pointed at the sky but a cubic kilometer of Antarctic ice, packed with sensors, built to catch the rare moment one of these particles actually hits something.

Why this mattersThis is a reminder that some of the biggest scientific payoffs come from projects that look, for a long time, like they are going nowhere — the kind of research that only makes sense if you are willing to fund curiosity for its own sake, long before anyone can promise a result. It also shows that understanding the universe does not always require building something in space; sometimes it means drilling two kilometers into ice and waiting.
Read the whole story

Neutrinos are strange even by the standards of physics. They have almost no mass, no electric charge, and almost no interest in interacting with anything else in the universe. A neutrino can pass straight through a planet the way light passes through a clean window. That makes them nearly impossible to detect — but it also makes them uniquely useful, because unlike light or charged particles, nothing deflects them on their way to Earth. A neutrino that reaches you is carrying a straight-line message from wherever it was born, even if that was a dying star or a black hole billions of light years away.

Halzen's answer to 'how do you catch something that refuses to interact' was to build an absurdly large trap and wait. The IceCube Neutrino Observatory, buried deep in the ice at the South Pole, is a grid of over five thousand light sensors spread through a full cubic kilometer of frozen water — roughly the volume of half a million Olympic swimming pools. On the vanishingly rare occasion a neutrino does strike a particle inside that ice, it produces a tiny flash of light, called Cherenkov radiation, which the sensors pick up. From the pattern of that flash, physicists can work out where the neutrino came from and how much energy it carried.

It took years of null results before IceCube found anything worth publishing, and Halzen kept the project alive through long stretches where critics doubted it would ever work. The breakthrough came when the observatory started tracing high-energy neutrinos back to specific objects outside our galaxy — including, in one notable case, a type of extremely active galaxy with a supermassive black hole at its center, violently flinging out matter and energy. That gave astronomers their first confirmed source of these cosmic messengers, and turned IceCube from a long-shot experiment into a working observatory.

This matters because most of what we know about the universe comes from light — visible light, radio waves, X-rays, all variations of the same thing. Neutrinos are a completely different kind of evidence, arriving from events and places that light alone cannot fully explain, including some of the most violent, energetic processes known to exist. Scientists increasingly combine neutrino data with traditional telescope images and gravitational-wave detectors, a strategy sometimes called multi-messenger astronomy, treating each type of signal as a different witness to the same cosmic event.

The Nobel committee in Stockholm placed Halzen among a short list of physicists honored not for discovering a single fact but for building an entirely new way of observing the cosmos — comparable to the invention of the radio telescope decades earlier. It is also a story about patience: IceCube took over a decade to build, cost hundreds of millions of dollars, and spent years producing nothing anyone outside the field found exciting, before quietly becoming one of the most important instruments in modern astrophysics.

Words worth knowing
  • Neutrino — A nearly massless, electrically neutral particle that passes through almost all matter without interacting, making it extremely hard to detect but useful for studying distant, violent events in space.
  • Cherenkov radiation — A faint flash of light produced when a particle moving through a material like ice or water travels faster than light does in that material, which is how instruments like IceCube detect otherwise invisible particles.
  • Multi-messenger astronomy — An approach to studying the universe that combines different kinds of signals — light, neutrinos, and gravitational waves — from the same cosmic event to build a fuller picture of what happened.

  1. Should governments keep funding huge, slow-moving science projects like IceCube, which took over a decade and hundreds of millions of dollars before producing a clear result?
