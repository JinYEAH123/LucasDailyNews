Trump Declares 'Economic D-Day' on Iran as Deadline Passes

A 60-day deadline for the US and Iran to reach a deal on Iran's nuclear program and sanctions has expired with no agreement. President Trump responded by threatening new sanctions on Oman, the country that had been quietly trying to broker peace, and promising a sweeping new sanctions campaign against any nation trading with Iran. Vice President JD Vance called it a 'new phase' of the US-Israel-Iran conflict, which has already caused hundreds of casualties.

Why this mattersRoughly a fifth of the world's oil passes through the Strait of Hormuz, so a blockade there can push up the price of gas at the pump wherever you live, even far from the Middle East. Decisions made in this standoff -- by the US, Iran, China, and Russia -- could shape whether this conflict cools down or spreads, at a moment when major powers are already choosing sides.

The United States gave Iran a 60-day countdown: reach a deal on its nuclear program and the crushing economic penalties called sanctions, or face what comes next. That clock ran out this week with no deal in sight.

What came next was blunt. President Trump threatened new sanctions against Oman -- the small Gulf nation that has spent months quietly trying to broker a deal between Washington and Tehran -- and promised what his administration is calling an 'economic D-Day': a wave of sanctions against any country anywhere that keeps doing business with Iran. Vice President JD Vance went further, describing the moment as a 'new phase' in a conflict between the US, Israel, and Iran that has involved actual fighting for months, not just economic pressure.

That fighting has already been costly. It has caused hundreds of casualties and led to a blockade of the Strait of Hormuz, a narrow waterway between Iran and Oman that roughly a fifth of the world's oil shipments pass through every day. Even figuring out exactly how many people have died has become complicated: the Pentagon recently changed how it counts and categorizes casualties from the conflict, meaning the same war can now produce very different casualty numbers depending on who is counting and how.

China's government publicly rejected the US pressure campaign, calling it unhelpful, while Iranian officials pushed back hard against what they called 'economic warfare.' Both reactions point to the same problem for Washington: sanctions only work if enough of the world agrees to enforce them, and right now two of the biggest players in the global economy are not on board. China buys more Iranian oil than any other country by a wide margin, giving Beijing real leverage to blunt whatever sanctions the US rolls out, and Russia has its own reasons to help Iran dodge economic isolation.

That leaves the US in a tricky spot. 'Economic D-Day' is a dramatic phrase -- it borrows from the codename of the massive Allied invasion that helped turn World War II -- but sanctions are only as strong as the countries willing to enforce them. If China and Russia keep trading with Iran anyway, the sweeping campaign Trump is promising could end up looking more like a warning shot than a knockout blow.

Words worth knowing
  • Sanctions — Penalties one country places on another, like blocking trade or freezing money, to try to force a change in behavior without going to war.
  • Strait of Hormuz — A narrow strip of water between Iran and Oman that roughly a fifth of the world's oil shipments pass through every day, making it a critical global chokepoint.
  • Leverage — The power one side has to influence another, often because the other side needs something from them -- like Iran needing to sell oil to China.

    Normal military bookkeeping

    • Militaries routinely update how they classify casualties as a conflict evolves, and that alone doesn't prove anything is being hidden.

Hong Kong Court Finds Two Vigil Organizers Guilty

A court in Hong Kong, a city in China, said two people broke the law by asking China to change how it is run. Lee Cheuk-yan, 69, and Chow Hang-tung, 41, used to lead a group that held a huge candlelight gathering every year to remember a protest from 1989. Now they could go to prison for up to 10 years, though the judge hasn't said the exact number yet.

Why this mattersThis story is about whether people should be allowed to say a government should change, even loudly and publicly. It also shows how a place that once let people remember a hard part of history openly, like a school letting kids talk about tough topics, might not be able to anymore.
Read the whole story

For more than 30 years, a group called the Hong Kong Alliance held a candlelight vigil every June 4th. Thousands of people would gather in a park, holding candles, to remember people who died in 1989 during protests in Beijing's Tiananmen Square. It was one of the only places in China where people could openly remember that event.

In 2021, Hong Kong's government forced the group to shut down. Lee and Chow were its leaders. This week, a court said they were guilty of 'inciting subversion' — that means encouraging people to try to overthrow the government — because the group had called for China to end one-party rule, where only one political party is allowed to hold power.

The charge comes from a law China put in place in 2020, called the national security law, which lets Hong Kong punish people it says threaten China's control. Britain and the European Union said the verdict shows that Hong Kong is losing its freedom to speak openly. Hong Kong and Chinese officials say the law is necessary to keep the city safe and stable.

  • vigil — A quiet gathering, often at night with candles, where people come together to remember someone or something important.

Hong Kong Court Convicts Two Tiananmen Vigil Organizers of 'Subversion'

A Hong Kong court on Friday found two veteran pro-democracy activists, Lee Cheuk-yan and Chow Hang-tung, guilty of inciting subversion for their work organizing the city's annual Tiananmen Square vigils. They now face up to 10 years in prison under a national security law Beijing imposed in 2020.

Why this mattersThis case is about whether remembering a historical event — one your own government would rather you forget — can be treated as a crime. It's worth asking what would happen if, someday, a country decided that organizing a memorial or a peaceful protest at your own school or town square counted as trying to overthrow the government.
Read the whole story

On Friday, a Hong Kong court found two well-known pro-democracy activists guilty of a serious political crime. Lee Cheuk-yan, 69, and Chow Hang-tung, 41, were convicted of "inciting subversion" — encouraging people to try to overthrow or seriously undermine China's government — under a law Beijing imposed on Hong Kong in 2020. They could be sentenced to as much as 10 years in prison; the judge has not yet decided exactly how long.

Both activists used to lead the Hong Kong Alliance in Support of Patriotic Democratic Movements of China, a group that for more than three decades organized huge candlelight vigils every June 4th in Hong Kong's Victoria Park. Those vigils remembered the hundreds, possibly thousands, of protesters killed by Chinese troops near Tiananmen Square in Beijing in 1989 — an event mainland Chinese authorities have spent 35 years trying to erase from public memory, banning any mention of it inside China. For decades, Hong Kong was the only place on Chinese soil where people could publicly mourn Tiananmen without being arrested, thanks to the "one country, two systems" arrangement made when Britain returned the city to China in 1997.

That changed after huge, sometimes violent pro-democracy protests swept Hong Kong in 2019. Beijing responded in 2020 by imposing a national security law that made broad categories of activity — including "subversion" — a crime punishable by up to life in prison. The Hong Kong Alliance was forced to disband in 2021, and its leaders, including Lee and Chow, were arrested. Prosecutors argued that the group's calls to "end one-party rule" in China amounted to inciting people to overthrow the state.

The British and European Union governments called the verdict further proof that free speech and independent civil society are shrinking fast in Hong Kong, a city once known for its relatively open press and courts. Hong Kong and Beijing officials reject that entirely, insisting the national security law is a legitimate and necessary tool against real threats — like foreign interference and violent unrest — and that the courts, not politics, decided this case based on evidence. A date for sentencing has not yet been set.

Lee and Chow are not the only ones convicted under this law. Dozens of activists, journalists, and pro-democracy politicians have faced similar charges since 2020, and most independent legal observers count this case among the clearest signs yet that the space for peaceful political dissent in Hong Kong has been closing.

Words worth knowing
  • national security law — A law Beijing introduced in Hong Kong in 2020 that made a wide range of activities against the Chinese government illegal, with punishments as severe as life in prison.
  • incite subversion — A crime under the national security law that means encouraging other people to try to overthrow or seriously weaken a government.
  • vigil — A quiet, peaceful gathering — often held at night with candles — where people come together to remember or honor someone or something.

The U.S. Now Owes More Than 40 Trillion Dollars

The United States government now owes more money than it ever has before: $40.05 trillion. That's more than double what it owed just ten years ago. And in July alone, the government spent $432.3 billion more than it collected in taxes.

Why this mattersWhen a government owes this much money, it has to spend more on paying interest, which is money it can't use for schools, roads, or helping people. Grown-ups disagree about whether this debt will affect you when you're older, through higher taxes or fewer government services, or whether it's just a normal part of how big countries manage money.
Read the whole story

Imagine you get $10 allowance a week, but you spend $15. You'd have to borrow $5 from somewhere. Do that every week for years, and you'd owe a lot of money. That's basically what the U.S. government has been doing.

The total amount it owes is called the national debt. Right now that's $40.05 trillion. If you tried to count to 40 trillion by saying one number every second, without stopping to eat or sleep, it would take you over a million years.

Ten years ago, the debt was $19.4 trillion. It has more than doubled since then. Reasons include tax cuts (the government collecting less money), spending on programs like healthcare for older people, and paying interest on money it already borrowed.

A group called the Peterson Foundation, which studies government money, thinks the debt could grow to $50 trillion in about six years if nothing changes. Some experts think this is a huge problem that needs fixing soon. Others think it's manageable, as long as the economy keeps growing too.

  • budget deficit — A budget deficit happens when a government spends more money in a certain time, like a month or a year, than it takes in from taxes.

America's Debt Just Hit $40 Trillion — And It's Growing Fast

The U.S. government now owes more than $40.05 trillion — twice what it owed just ten years ago. In July alone, the government spent $432.3 billion more than it collected in taxes, the worst July shortfall in five years, and one respected research group thinks the total debt could hit $50 trillion within six years.

Why this mattersBy the time you're an adult, this debt and its interest payments will still be there, shaping how much tax you pay and how much money is left for things like schools, research, and infrastructure. The choices made now about taxes and spending will decide who ends up carrying that bill.
Read the whole story

Think of the national debt as one giant, never-fully-paid credit card bill that the U.S. government has been running since the country's founding. Every year the government spends more than it collects, it borrows the difference — and that borrowed amount just keeps adding up. This month, Treasury data confirmed the total passed $40.05 trillion, roughly double the $19.4 trillion it owed just a decade ago.

That $40 trillion is the total pile built up over the government's entire history. The 'deficit' is something different: it's the gap between what the government spends and what it collects in a single year (or month). In July, that gap was $432.3 billion — the biggest July deficit in five years, meaning the hole is getting deeper faster than it has in a while.

The nonpartisan Peterson Foundation, a think tank that tracks federal spending, projects the debt could balloon to $50 trillion in about six years if nothing changes. Part of why it grows so fast is that debt works like a snowball rolling downhill: the government has to pay interest on everything it already owes, and that interest itself gets added to future bills.

Three things are driving the climb. Tax cuts mean the government collects less money than it used to. 'Entitlement spending' — mandatory programs like Social Security and Medicare that pay out to retirees and the sick — keeps growing automatically as the population ages, with no yearly vote required. And interest payments on past debt have become one of the government's biggest expenses all by themselves.

The consequences reach beyond a big scary number. As debt piles up, lenders sometimes demand higher interest rates to keep lending to the government, which means even more of each year's budget goes toward interest payments instead of schools, roads, or the military. That squeezes future choices — money spent paying off yesterday's borrowing is money not spent on anything else.

Economists don't agree on how urgent this is. Some argue the U.S. needs to act now, before rising interest costs spiral out of control. Others point out that America has carried large debts for decades without disaster, and that panicking could lead to cuts that hurt real people more than the debt itself does. They also disagree sharply on the fix — higher taxes, lower spending, or some mix of both — and which popular programs, if any, should be touched.

Words worth knowing
  • National debt — The total amount of money a government owes, built up from years of spending more than it collects.
  • Federal budget deficit — The gap between what the government spends and what it collects in taxes during one year, which gets added to the national debt.
  • Entitlement spending — Money the government must pay out automatically to programs like Social Security and Medicare, without a yearly vote.
