Iran is a country in the Middle East. For years, other countries have worried that Iran might be building a nuclear bomb. Sixty days ago — about as long as a summer vacation — the United States told Iran to make a deal or face serious consequences. This week, that deadline passed with no deal in sight.

President Trump says he will now launch what he calls an 'economic D-Day' — a huge wave of new sanctions. Sanctions are rules that stop countries from trading with each other, kind of like taking away someone's allowance until they behave. Trump says any country, even a friendly one like Oman, could get punished too if it keeps doing business with Iran.

Vice President JD Vance called this a 'new phase' of a war already happening between the US, Israel, and Iran. That war has already caused hundreds of deaths and led to ships being blocked from passing through the Strait of Hormuz — a narrow strip of ocean where a huge share of the world's oil travels through, almost like a single tollbooth for oil tankers.

China called the US pressure unhelpful, and China has real power here — it buys more of Iran's oil than any other country in the world. Iran also pushed back hard against what it called 'economic warfare.' Making things messier, the Pentagon, which runs the US military, recently changed how it counts deaths from the war, so nobody even agrees on how many people have really died.

The United States gave Iran a 60-day countdown: reach a deal on its nuclear program and the crushing economic penalties called sanctions, or face what comes next. That clock ran out this week with no deal in sight.

What came next was blunt. President Trump threatened new sanctions against Oman -- the small Gulf nation that has spent months quietly trying to broker a deal between Washington and Tehran -- and promised what his administration is calling an 'economic D-Day': a wave of sanctions against any country anywhere that keeps doing business with Iran. Vice President JD Vance went further, describing the moment as a 'new phase' in a conflict between the US, Israel, and Iran that has involved actual fighting for months, not just economic pressure.

That fighting has already been costly. It has caused hundreds of casualties and led to a blockade of the Strait of Hormuz, a narrow waterway between Iran and Oman that roughly a fifth of the world's oil shipments pass through every day. Even figuring out exactly how many people have died has become complicated: the Pentagon recently changed how it counts and categorizes casualties from the conflict, meaning the same war can now produce very different casualty numbers depending on who is counting and how.

China's government publicly rejected the US pressure campaign, calling it unhelpful, while Iranian officials pushed back hard against what they called 'economic warfare.' Both reactions point to the same problem for Washington: sanctions only work if enough of the world agrees to enforce them, and right now two of the biggest players in the global economy are not on board. China buys more Iranian oil than any other country by a wide margin, giving Beijing real leverage to blunt whatever sanctions the US rolls out, and Russia has its own reasons to help Iran dodge economic isolation.

That leaves the US in a tricky spot. 'Economic D-Day' is a dramatic phrase -- it borrows from the codename of the massive Allied invasion that helped turn World War II -- but sanctions are only as strong as the countries willing to enforce them. If China and Russia keep trading with Iran anyway, the sweeping campaign Trump is promising could end up looking more like a warning shot than a knockout blow.

The deadline that just lapsed was not the beginning of this confrontation but its latest checkpoint. For two months, Washington had held out the possibility of a negotiated settlement — some combination of limits on Iran's nuclear program in exchange for the lifting of sanctions that have strangled its economy for years. When the sixty days ran out with no deal, Trump did not simply reimpose the old sanctions; he announced what he called an 'economic D-Day,' invoking the language of the Allied invasion of Normandy to describe a coordinated push meant to cut Iran off from the world economy entirely. He also threatened Oman directly, a striking move given that Oman had been the trusted go-between shuttling messages between Washington and Tehran for months.

This is happening against the backdrop of an active shooting war, not a cold standoff. Israeli and American strikes on Iranian targets, and Iranian retaliation, have killed and wounded hundreds of people over recent months, and Iran has responded in part by disrupting shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway between Iran and the Arabian Peninsula through which roughly a fifth of the world's oil passes each day. Vance's description of a 'new phase' suggests the administration sees the sanctions push not as an alternative to military pressure but as an extension of it — economic weapons deployed alongside, not instead of, actual weapons.

Iran did not accept the framing quietly. Officials in Tehran pushed back publicly on what they called 'economic warfare,' arguing that sweeping sanctions on third countries amount to collective punishment aimed at ordinary Iranians rather than at the government's nuclear program specifically. China's foreign ministry went further, rejecting the entire US pressure campaign as unhelpful — a notable statement given that China is not a bystander in this dispute but Iran's single largest oil customer, and therefore one of the primary targets of any 'secondary sanctions' regime that punishes countries for trading with Tehran.

That Chinese objection points to the real weakness in Washington's strategy. Sanctions only isolate a country if enough of the world agrees to enforce them, and both China and Russia have concrete reasons and real means to blunt an American pressure campaign — China needs Iranian oil, and both governments have their own reasons to resist a strategy that would let Washington set the rules of who can trade with whom. Analysts point out that this is the structural problem with any 'maximum pressure' approach in a world where the US no longer has uncontested control over global energy markets: the sanctions can be sweeping in language while being porous in practice.

Adding to the uncertainty is a dispute over the basic facts of the conflict's human cost. The Pentagon has changed how it categorizes casualties from this war, a shift that has made the true toll of dead and wounded a genuinely contested number rather than a simple statistic — different counting methods can produce very different headlines from the same events on the ground, and it is not yet clear which method the public is now seeing. CNN, NPR, Bloomberg, and Al Jazeera have each reported pieces of this story independently; Al Jazeera, it's worth noting, is funded by the government of Qatar, a fact relevant to how it frames Gulf and Iran-related coverage even where its factual reporting lines up with the others.

What happens next depends on choices that have not yet been made. If the sanctions campaign proceeds as described, it will test whether the US can still compel global compliance through financial pressure alone, or whether an economy the size of China's simply routes around it. If it stalls, Washington may face pressure to escalate militarily instead, given how explicitly Vance has tied the sanctions to the broader war effort. Either path affects oil prices, shipping costs, and diplomatic alignments well beyond the Middle East.

刚刚过去的这一期限并非冲突的开端，而只是最新一个节点。过去两个月里，华盛顿一直留有谈判解决的可能——大致思路是伊朗限制核计划，换取美方解除多年来重创伊朗经济的制裁。六十天期限届满、协议未能达成后，特朗普并未只是简单恢复旧制裁，而是宣布发动他所称的“经济诺曼底”，借用二战盟军诺曼底登陆的说法，形容一场旨在将伊朗彻底排除出世界经济体系的协同行动。他还直接向阿曼发出威胁——考虑到阿曼数月来一直是华盛顿与德黑兰之间信任的传话人，这一举动格外引人注目。

这一切发生在一场正在进行的实弹战争背景下，而非一场冷对峙。以色列和美国对伊朗目标的打击，以及伊朗的报复行动，近几个月来已造成数百人伤亡，伊朗则部分以扰乱霍尔木兹海峡航运作为回应——这条位于伊朗与阿拉伯半岛之间的狭窄水道，每天承担着全球约五分之一的石油运输量。万斯所说的“新阶段”，暗示美方并不将制裁攻势视为军事压力的替代方案，而是其延伸——经济武器与实际武器并用，而非取而代之。

伊朗方面并未默默接受这一说法。德黑兰官员公开反驳所谓“经济战争”的说法，认为对第三国的全面制裁实质上是一种集体惩罚，打击的是普通伊朗民众，而非政府的核计划本身。中国外交部的表态更进一步，公开拒绝美方整个施压行动，称其无助于问题解决——这一表态格外值得关注，因为中国在这场争端中并非旁观者，而是伊朗最大的石油买家，因此也是任何针对与德黑兰有生意往来的国家实施“次级制裁”政策的首要打击对象。

中国的这一反对恰恰点出了华盛顿战略的真正软肋。制裁要真正孤立一个国家，前提是世界上足够多的国家愿意配合执行，而中国和俄罗斯都有充分的现实理由与切实的手段来削弱美国的施压行动——中国需要伊朗的石油，两国政府也各自有理由抵制一种由美国单方面决定谁能与谁做生意的战略。分析人士指出，这正是任何“极限施压”策略在当今世界所面临的结构性难题：在美国已不再对全球能源市场拥有绝对控制力的情况下，制裁措辞可以很全面，执行起来却可能千疮百孔。

接下来会发生什么，取决于尚未做出的选择。如果制裁行动按预期推进，它将检验美国是否仍能仅凭金融压力迫使全球遵从，还是说像中国这样体量的经济体终究能绕开限制。如果行动陷入停滞，鉴于万斯已如此明确地将制裁与更广泛的战争行动挂钩，华盛顿或将面临转向军事升级的压力。无论走哪条路，其影响都会波及中东以外的石油价格、航运成本与外交格局。

On Friday, a Hong Kong court convicted Lee Cheuk-yan and Chow Hang-tung of "inciting subversion of state power," a charge that exists only under the national security law Beijing imposed on the city in 2020. Both had led the Hong Kong Alliance in Support of Patriotic Democratic Movements of China, the organization that for more than three decades ran the huge candlelight vigils marking June 4 — the anniversary of the 1989 Tiananmen Square crackdown, when Chinese troops killed protesters who had gathered to demand political reform. The Alliance disbanded in 2021 rather than risk prosecution under the new law.

The charge centers on the Alliance's longstanding public call to "end one-party rule" in China, a slogan it used for years alongside demands for democracy and an official reassessment of the 1989 crackdown. Prosecutors argued that campaigning to end the Communist Party's monopoly on power amounts to an attempt to overthrow the state — an offense the security law punishes severely, with life imprisonment possible in the gravest cases. Lee and Chow now face up to ten years each; sentencing has not yet been scheduled.

The national security law was drafted in Beijing in 2020, after a year of mass anti-government protests in Hong Kong, and applied to the city without passing through its own legislature. It created several new offenses — secession, subversion, terrorism, and collusion with foreign forces — defined broadly enough that, in serious cases, defendants can be tried without a jury. Chinese and Hong Kong officials describe the law as necessary to restore order and protect national sovereignty after the 2019 unrest. Critics, including Western governments, argue its vague language lets prosecutors treat ordinary political speech, including slogans and public advocacy, as a threat to the state itself.

Friday's verdict drew swift criticism from the UK and the EU, which said it showed how far freedom of expression and independent civic organizing have narrowed in Hong Kong since the law took effect. Before the Alliance was dismantled, Hong Kong was the only place on Chinese soil where June 4 could be publicly commemorated at scale, with vigils once drawing crowds of well over 100,000 people. Hong Kong and Beijing authorities rejected the foreign criticism, insisting the law was enacted lawfully and applies to conduct rather than opinion — that no one is punished for remembering June 4 privately, only for organizing to end the political system that governs China.

Lee and Chow are among dozens of pro-democracy figures — journalists, lawmakers, lawyers, and activists — convicted or awaiting trial under the same law since 2020. Think of the national debt as one giant, never-fully-paid credit card bill that the U.S. government has been running since the country's founding. Every year the government spends more than it collects, it borrows the difference — and that borrowed amount just keeps adding up. This month, Treasury data confirmed the total passed $40.05 trillion, roughly double the $19.4 trillion it owed just a decade ago.

That $40 trillion is the total pile built up over the government's entire history. The 'deficit' is something different: it's the gap between what the government spends and what it collects in a single year (or month). In July, that gap was $432.3 billion — the biggest July deficit in five years, meaning the hole is getting deeper faster than it has in a while.

The nonpartisan Peterson Foundation, a think tank that tracks federal spending, projects the debt could balloon to $50 trillion in about six years if nothing changes. Part of why it grows so fast is that debt works like a snowball rolling downhill: the government has to pay interest on everything it already owes, and that interest itself gets added to future bills.

Three things are driving the climb. Tax cuts mean the government collects less money than it used to. 'Entitlement spending' — mandatory programs like Social Security and Medicare that pay out to retirees and the sick — keeps growing automatically as the population ages, with no yearly vote required. And interest payments on past debt have become one of the government's biggest expenses all by themselves.

The consequences reach beyond a big scary number. As debt piles up, lenders sometimes demand higher interest rates to keep lending to the government, which means even more of each year's budget goes toward interest payments instead of schools, roads, or the military. That squeezes future choices — money spent paying off yesterday's borrowing is money not spent on anything else.

Economists don't agree on how urgent this is. Some argue the U.S. needs to act now, before rising interest costs spiral out of control. Others point out that America has carried large debts for decades without disaster, and that panicking could lead to cuts that hurt real people more than the debt itself does. They also disagree sharply on the fix — higher taxes, lower spending, or some mix of both — and which popular programs, if any, should be touched.

To put $40 trillion in perspective: the entire U.S. economy — everything produced and sold in the country in a year — is worth roughly $29 trillion. That means the government now owes more than the whole country earns annually, a ratio economists watch closely because it shows how much of future economic growth is already spoken for.

There's a difference between the debt and the deficit, and it matters. The deficit is the yearly gap between what the government spends and what it collects in taxes — like an annual overspend on a credit card. The debt is the running total of every year's overspend, plus the interest charged on carrying that balance forward. July's $432.3 billion deficit is one month's addition to a pile that's been growing for decades.

Three forces are driving the pile higher at once. Tax cuts passed in recent years reduced how much revenue the government collects. Entitlement spending — Social Security, Medicare, and Medicaid, the programs that pay for retirement and health care — keeps rising automatically as the population ages, regardless of who's in office. And interest costs have become a problem in their own right: as interest rates rose from near-zero in 2022 to much higher levels, the government now pays substantially more just to service the debt it already has, which adds to next year's deficit, which adds to the debt — a loop that feeds itself.

That loop is why the Peterson Foundation's projection is alarming to some economists: not just that the debt is large, but that its growth is accelerating rather than leveling off. Reaching $50 trillion in six years would mean adding roughly $10 trillion in a period shorter than it took to add the previous $10 trillion.

The immediate consequence is competition for the federal budget. Every dollar spent paying interest to bondholders is a dollar not available for defense, infrastructure, or tax relief — and interest payments are now one of the fastest-growing lines in the budget. There's also a broader economic worry: heavy government borrowing can push up interest rates across the whole economy, meaning higher costs on mortgages, car loans, and business borrowing for everyone, not just the government.

Economists don't agree on what to do about any of this, or even on how urgent it is. Some argue the trajectory is unsustainable and demands painful choices now — cutting entitlement growth, raising taxes, or both. Others point out that the U.S. borrows in its own currency, that investors worldwide still buy U.S. bonds, and that similar debt-to-GDP levels have been worked down before without collapse — meaning gradual fixes may be wiser than a crisis response that risks slowing the economy further.

    • 经济体量和债务规模同样重要,而美国经济一直在随着债务增长而持续增长。

    Raise taxes应主要加税

    • Cutting programs like Social Security and Medicare would hurt millions of retirees and sick people who depend on them to survive.
    • Tax rates on high earners and corporations have been lower in recent decades than in much of the 20th century, leaving room to raise them.
    • Spending cuts alone historically haven't closed deficits this large without also slowing the economy, which can shrink tax revenue further.
    • 削减社会保障金和联邦医疗保险这类项目,会伤害数百万依赖它们维持生计的退休人员和病患。

    Cut spending应主要削减支出

    • Raising taxes too much can discourage businesses from investing and hiring, which can slow economic growth for everyone.
    • Entitlement programs are the fastest-growing part of the budget, so ignoring them means other spending gets squeezed instead.
    • Government programs sometimes contain waste or inefficiency, and trimming spending can force overdue reforms rather than just collecting more money.
    • 加税幅度过大可能会打消企业投资和招聘的积极性,进而拖慢整体经济增长。
    • 法定支出项目是预算中增长最快的部分,如果不加以调整,其他支出就会被进一步挤压。
    • 政府项目中有时存在浪费或效率低下的问题,削减支出可以倒逼推行早该进行的改革,而不只是一味多收钱。

    Urgent crisis紧迫危机

    • The debt is growing faster than the economy that has to eventually pay it off, which cannot continue indefinitely.
    • Interest costs are now one of the fastest-rising items in the federal budget, crowding out other priorities.
    • Waiting for a crisis of confidence among bond buyers to force action would be far more painful than adjusting course now.
    • 债务的增速已经超过必须为其买单的经济体本身的增速，这种状态无法无限持续下去。
    • 利息支出已成为联邦预算中增长最快的项目之一，挤压了其他重要开支。
    • 如果等到债券买家信心崩溃才被迫行动，代价将远比现在主动调整要惨重。

    Manageable, not urgent可控，并不紧迫

    • The U.S. borrows in its own currency and controls its own central bank, unlike countries that have faced debt crises before.
    • Global investors still buy U.S. Treasury bonds in huge volumes, showing continued confidence rather than alarm.
    • History shows debt-to-GDP ratios can be brought down gradually through growth, and rushing austerity now risks triggering the very recession that would make the debt harder to manage.

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it想不出来？两方各有说法

    Raise taxes加税

    • Recent tax cuts reduced government revenue significantly, and reversing some of them would close a real part of the gap without touching benefits people rely on.
    • Cutting entitlement spending falls hardest on retirees and low-income households who depend on those programs most.
    • Effective tax rates on high earners and corporations have fallen over past decades even as their share of national income rose.
    • 近年的减税政策大幅降低了政府收入，撤销部分减税措施能在不触及民众依赖的福利前提下，真正缩小缺口。
    • 削减福利性支出对退休人员和低收入家庭打击最大，而他们恰恰最依赖这些项目。

    Cut spending削减支出

    • Entitlement programs are structurally unsustainable given an aging population, regardless of how much revenue is raised.
    • Higher taxes can slow economic growth and investment, which would shrink the tax base the government relies on.
    • The growth in spending, not a shortfall in revenue, is the primary force widening the deficit year over year.
    • 无论加多少税，福利性项目在人口老龄化的结构性压力下都难以持续维系。
    • 加税可能抑制经济增长和投资，从而缩小政府依赖的税基。
    • 支出的持续增长，而非收入不足，才是逐年扩大赤字的主要推手。
