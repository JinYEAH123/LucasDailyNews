A Big Scorekeeper Says One Team Is Pulling Ahead in America's Election Race

In the United States, people are about to vote for 435 seats in a part of government called the House of Representatives. A respected group that predicts elections just said one political party, the Democrats, looks likely to win a lot more seats than expected. That could change who gets to make big decisions in the country for the next two years.

Why this mattersEven if you can't vote yet, decisions made by the House of Representatives can affect things like how much money schools get, what gets taxed, and what new rules exist. Who wins these elections can shape life for families like yours.

Imagine a classroom where everyone votes for new class monitors every two years. In the United States, something similar happens for a group called the House of Representatives. It has 435 seats, and right now, one party called the Republicans controls more of them.

A group called the Cook Political Report studies these elections closely. Lots of politicians and reporters trust their predictions. On Friday, they said Democrats could flip somewhere between 12 and 22 seats away from Republicans. If it's a high number, it would be bigger than any swing in nearly 20 years.

This news came out while top politicians were busy trying to help their teams. President Trump gave a speech in Florida. Former President Obama gave speeches in Ohio to help Democrats. Both were trying to convince people how to vote before Election Day arrives in November.

Whoever controls the House gets to help decide what new laws get made, and they can also check on what the President is doing. So this isn't just about numbers on a scoreboard. It's about who has more power in the country for the next two years. But nothing is final yet. These are just predictions, and the real answer comes when people actually vote.

Election Forecasters Say Democrats Could Have a Very Good Night in November

With less than two weeks until the U.S. midterm elections, a respected nonpartisan group that predicts election results shifted its forecasts for 19 House of Representatives races in Democrats' favor. The group now thinks Democrats could gain anywhere from 12 to 22 seats, a range that at its top end would beat the famous "wave" elections of 2006 and 2018. Both parties are scrambling to respond, with former presidents and the current one campaigning hard in swing states this weekend.

Why this mattersWhoever controls the House after this election decides what gets voted on in Congress for the next two years — including budgets, new laws, and whether to investigate the president — so this isn't just a number on a forecasting website, it shapes what the U.S. government can actually do. If you're following politics for the first time, this is also a good moment to see how election predictions work: they're informed estimates built from real data, not guarantees, and they can and do change right up until votes are counted.

Every two years, Americans vote not just for president but for all 435 seats in the House of Representatives, the larger of the two chambers of Congress. These votes, held in the years between presidential elections, are called midterm elections, and they matter because whichever party controls the House gets to decide what bills come up for a vote and can launch investigations into the White House. Right now, Republicans hold a narrow majority, meaning Democrats only need to flip a relatively small number of seats — win districts currently held by Republicans — to take control.

On Friday, October 23, the Cook Political Report, a forecasting outfit that both parties treat as credible because it doesn't openly favor either side, moved its ratings on 19 separate House races toward the Democrats. That's not a small tweak. The report now estimates Democrats are likely to gain somewhere between 12 and 22 seats. To put that upper number in context: in 2006, when voter anger over the Iraq War helped Democrats take the House, they gained 31 seats. In 2018, reacting to Donald Trump's first term, they gained 41. A 22-seat swing wouldn't quite match those historic "wave" years, where one party sweeps up seats across the map, but it would be close, and some individual races in this new round of ratings are shifting as if a wave is already building.

What makes this notable is that it's not a one-time blip. Two weeks earlier, on October 9, Cook had already moved 18 seats toward Democrats. Normally you'd expect some of that movement to reverse as Election Day approaches and undecided voters make up their minds in unpredictable directions. Instead, the trend accelerated — more seats moved the same direction, not back the other way. That pattern is what political strategists watch most closely, because a steady drift usually reflects something real happening with voters, not just noise in the polling.

The political fight on the ground matches the numbers. President Trump held a rally in Florida, and former President Obama spent Saturday, October 24, campaigning in Ohio for Democratic candidates — a reminder that both parties see these races as genuinely competitive enough to deploy their biggest names. Midterms are historically rough for whichever party holds the White House, since they often become a referendum on how the sitting president is doing rather than a vote on local issues, and campaigns on both sides are leaning into that dynamic.

None of this means the outcome is settled. Forecasts like Cook's are built from polling, fundraising numbers, and how individual candidates are performing — all things that can shift again before voters actually cast ballots. Close races can break either way at the last minute, and a forecaster moving a rating doesn't mean the seat is decided; it means the odds have changed. What's clear is that control of the House, and with it the ability to pass or block legislation for the next two years, is genuinely up for grabs, and the people running campaigns on both sides are treating it that way.

Words worth knowing
  • midterm elections — U.S. elections held halfway through a president's four-year term, where voters choose all of the House of Representatives and about a third of the Senate, without the president on the ballot.
  • flip a seat — When a political seat held by one party is won by the opposing party in the next election.
  • wave election — A U.S. election year where one party wins an unusually large number of seats, often driven by widespread dissatisfaction with the party in power.

American Businesses Are Still Growing Fast — But Prices Are Climbing Too

A big survey of American businesses came out on Friday, October 23. It found that companies are still growing quickly — almost as fast as any time in the last few years. But there's a catch: businesses are also raising their prices faster than they have in over four years.

Why this mattersWhen businesses raise their prices, it can mean your family pays more for things like groceries, toys, or a trip to a restaurant. The decisions the Federal Reserve makes about money also affect things like how expensive a house or a car loan is for grown-ups you know.
Read the whole story

Imagine a thermometer that doesn't measure heat, but measures how busy businesses are. Every month, a company called S&P Global asks thousands of businesses across America simple questions: Are you selling more? Are you hiring more people? Are your costs going up?

They turn the answers into one number. If the number is above 50, businesses are growing. If it's below 50, they're shrinking. This month's number was 57.6 — way above 50, meaning business is strong. That's actually a tiny bit lower than last month's number, but it's still one of the highest scores in years.

Here's the twist. Companies said they're charging their customers more money than usual — the fastest price increases in over four years. That's a problem because when prices rise quickly everywhere, it's called inflation, and it makes everyday things cost more for everyone.

This puts a group called the Federal Reserve in a tricky spot. The Federal Reserve is like the manager of the whole U.S. economy. They've been thinking about making it cheaper to borrow money, which usually helps businesses and families. But if prices are already rising fast, doing that could make inflation even worse.

US Businesses Are Still Booming — But So Are the Prices They Charge

A major survey released Friday found that American businesses are still growing strongly this October, almost as fast as any month since 2022. But the same survey showed something that worries economists: the prices companies charge are rising at their fastest pace in over four years, which could make it harder for the Federal Reserve to cut interest rates.

Why this mattersIf businesses keep raising prices the way this survey suggests, things you or your family buy regularly — food, clothes, services — could get noticeably more expensive. And if the Fed decides to keep interest rates higher to fight that, it gets pricier for families to borrow money for things like a car or a home, even while the job market stays strong.
Read the whole story

Every month, a company called S&P Global surveys thousands of American businesses — factories, shops, service companies — and asks a simple question: is business getting better or worse? The answers get turned into a number called the composite index. Anything above 50 means the economy is growing; anything below means it's shrinking. This October, the number came in at 57.6. That's down slightly from September's 58.4, but it's still the second-highest reading since 2022, meaning businesses are telling surveyors that demand remains strong, they're hiring more people, and new orders keep coming in.

Here's the catch. Buried inside that same survey is a smaller measure that tracks how fast businesses are raising the prices they charge their customers — for everything from a haircut to a hotel room to a hammer. That measure jumped to its fastest pace in more than four years. In plain terms: companies are busy and confident enough that they feel they can charge more, and customers are still paying it.

That combination — strong growth plus rising prices — puts the Federal Reserve, the United States' central bank, in an awkward spot. The Fed's main tool for managing the economy is the interest rate it sets, which ripples out to affect the cost of mortgages, car loans, and credit cards across the whole country. When inflation (the general rise in prices over time) is a worry, the Fed usually wants to keep interest rates higher, because expensive borrowing cools down spending and takes pressure off prices. But when growth looks fragile, the Fed wants to cut rates to make borrowing cheaper and keep the economy moving. Right now, this single survey is handing the Fed arguments for both actions at once.

It's worth being clear about where this information comes from. Bloomberg, a financial news service, did the original reporting on the S&P Global data. A version of the same story also appeared on Yahoo Finance, but it's the identical Bloomberg article running on a different website — not a second newsroom independently checking the numbers. That matters because when only one organisation has actually reported a story, readers should treat it as one account of events, not confirmation from two separate sources.

None of this tells us yet whether the price pressure in this survey will actually show up in the government's official inflation reports, which come from a different, more thorough measurement process and are what the Fed watches most closely. Economists will be looking closely at those numbers over the coming months to see whether this is the start of a real trend or just a blip in one survey.

Words worth knowing
  • Composite index — A single number built from survey answers that sums up whether business activity overall is growing or shrinking, with 50 as the dividing line.
  • Inflation — A general, ongoing rise in the prices of goods and services, which means each dollar buys a little less over time.
  • Federal Reserve — The central bank of the United States, which sets interest rates to try to keep the economy growing steadily without letting prices rise too fast.

Gunmen Attack Mourners at a Church Funeral in Jordan

On Friday, October 23, gunmen opened fire inside a Greek Orthodox church in the Jordanian city of Karak, where a funeral was underway. People died and dozens more were wounded, though news organizations and the Jordanian government have given different counts of exactly how many. As of now, nobody has said who did it or why.

Why this mattersWhen gunfire reaches a funeral inside a church, it's an attack on the idea that grief and worship are supposed to be off-limits — a line that, once crossed, makes people everywhere wonder what else might not be safe. It's also a reminder that in the first chaotic hours after any violent event, even something as basic as a death toll can be genuinely unsettled, not just inconvenient to report.
Read the whole story

It happened during a funeral — a moment when a community gathers specifically because someone has already died, and expects, reasonably, to be safe doing it. Mourners had packed into a Greek Orthodox church in Karak, a city in southern Jordan, when gunmen started shooting. It is the kind of attack that is rare almost anywhere, and especially rare in Jordan.

Exactly how many people were killed is still unsettled, and that's worth sitting with rather than smoothing over. CBS News, The Washington Post, and NBC News each reported at least 7 dead and about 40 wounded. CNN, reporting separately, put the death toll at 9. A spokesperson for the Jordanian government, meanwhile, told ABC News that 6 people were killed and 28 wounded. These aren't wild contradictions — in the chaos right after a shooting, hospitals, witnesses, and officials are often counting different things at different times — but it means no single number here should be treated as final yet.

Jordan matters to this story's size. It's a country with a Muslim majority and a small, long-established Christian minority, and unlike several of its neighbors, it has largely stayed outside the sectarian violence — attacks aimed at people specifically because of their religious or ethnic group — that has repeatedly torn through other parts of the Middle East. That reputation for relative calm is a big part of why an attack like this one, inside a church, during a funeral, is making headlines well beyond Jordan's borders.

Jordanian authorities have not named a motive or identified any suspects. That silence is its own kind of information: it means every explanation currently circulating is speculation, not confirmed fact, however confidently it might be stated.

The one official response so far came from King Abdullah, Jordan's monarch, who on Saturday ordered what was described as a firm response to the attack. That description comes from Al Jazeera, a news outlet funded by the government of Qatar — a fact worth keeping in mind, since a state-funded outlet covering a neighboring government's response isn't the same as independent reporting, even when nothing it says is false.

Words worth knowing
  • Greek Orthodox — A branch of Christianity with its own ancient traditions and leadership, practiced by communities across the Middle East, Greece, and elsewhere.
  • Sectarian violence — Violence aimed at people specifically because of their religious or ethnic group, rather than because of anything they personally did.
  • State-funded outlet — A news organization financially supported by a government, which can shape what stories it covers and how, even when its reporting is accurate.
