Americans Vote to Share the Power in Washington

On Tuesday, millions of Americans voted in an election called the midterms. The Democratic Party won control of both parts of the U.S. Congress, taking power away from the Republican Party, which had controlled everything for the last two years under President Trump.

Why this mattersWhen one group controls everything, there is nobody to say 'wait, let's check that first.' Now Democrats in Congress can ask tough questions and demand documents from the president's team, a bit like a referee who can finally blow the whistle. It means decisions in Washington for the next two years will need more agreement between different groups, just like sharing a game console means you have to take turns.

Imagine a classroom where one team has been in charge of every decision for two years straight. Now, after a vote, a different team gets to share control of the rules. That is what just happened in the United States government.

Congress has two parts: the House and the Senate. Democrats won at least 250 of the 435 House seats, more than half, while Republicans won about 160. In the Senate, Democrats won at least 54 of the 100 seats, Republicans 42.

This had not happened in 20 years: a Republican president losing control of both parts of Congress partway through his term. Democrats won seats in places that usually vote Republican, like Texas and South Carolina.

People do not fully agree on why. Vice President Vance says a war with Iran made voters angry. President Trump says the war was worth its cost. Others point to high gas prices making everyday life more expensive. Both the war and the cost of living probably played a part.

Voters Take Congress Away From Trump's Party

On Tuesday, November 3, American voters handed control of both houses of Congress to the Democratic Party, ending two years in which Republicans held the presidency and both chambers at once. It's the first time in two decades that a sitting Republican president has lost both the House and the Senate in a single midterm election. President Trump still has two years left in his term, but he will now have to share power with a Congress run by the opposition.

Why this mattersWhen one party controls the presidency and Congress, it can pass laws and set policy with little resistance; when control is split, as it now is, almost everything requires negotiation or stalls entirely. That affects decisions far beyond Washington — budgets, wars, and the price of things like gas are all shaped by who holds the power to say yes or no in Congress.
Read the whole story

Every two years, Americans vote in what's called a midterm election — a nationwide vote for Congress that falls halfway through a president's four-year term. This year's midterms, held on Tuesday, delivered a sharp result: according to projections from CNBC's election desk, Democrats won at least 250 seats in the 435-seat House of Representatives to the Republicans' 160, and at least 54 of the 100 Senate seats to the Republicans' 42. Both chambers flipped from Republican to Democratic control in a single night.

To understand why that matters, it helps to know what the last two years looked like. Since Trump took office, Republicans had controlled the presidency, the House, and the Senate all at once — a situation called unified government, where one party can pass laws with little need to negotiate with the other side. That arrangement is now over. Al Jazeera pointed out that this is the first time in 20 years a sitting Republican president has lost both chambers of Congress in a midterm, a sign of how unusual Tuesday's result was by historical standards.

The scale of the shift showed up in places Republicans normally count on. Democrats flipped House and Senate seats in Texas, South Carolina, Iowa, Michigan, and Kansas — states that have leaned Republican for years. CBS News reported that Democrats also won Senate seats in North Carolina and Ohio, two states that were expected to be closely fought but still came as a blow to Republican hopes of holding the Senate.

What pushed voters in these states to switch sides? CBS pointed to two main pressures: an unpopular American war with Iran, and high prices at the pump for gas and diesel fuel, which raised costs for everything from commuting to shipping groceries. Together, these issues widened the number of competitive states — places that would normally be safe for one party suddenly became winnable for the other.

Not everyone agrees on which of those two forces mattered more. Vice President Vance publicly blamed the Iran war for the Republican losses, suggesting it was the deciding factor. President Trump pushed back, calling the war's cost 'peanuts' compared to what the country gained from it — implying the economy, not the war, was the real driver of voter anger. It's a genuine disagreement: pollsters and political analysts will spend months arguing over how much of the result traces back to the war versus the price of gas, and the honest answer may be that both fed into each other.

Whatever the exact cause, the practical consequences are clear. Control of the House gives Democrats subpoena power — the legal authority to demand documents and testimony from the government, which lets them investigate the administration's decisions, including the Iran war, in ways they couldn't before. For the next two years, Trump will be negotiating with a Congress that can block his bills and dig into his administration's choices, a sharp change from the free hand he had before Tuesday.

Words worth knowing
  • midterm election — A nationwide US election held halfway through a president's four-year term, when all of the House and about a third of the Senate are up for a vote.
  • unified government — A situation where one political party controls the presidency and both houses of Congress at the same time, making it easier to pass that party's agenda without compromise.
  • subpoena power — The legal authority, held by whichever party controls a chamber of Congress, to demand documents or force officials to testify as part of an investigation.

  1. Is it better for a country when one party controls the presidency and Congress, or when control is split between them?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Unified control is better

    • A single party with full control can act quickly on urgent problems instead of being stuck in constant negotiation.
    • Voters who elect a president and a same-party Congress are sending a clear mandate, and divided government can block that mandate from being carried out.
    • Split control often turns Congress into a battleground for investigations and political point-scoring rather than passing laws that help people.

    Divided government is better

    • An opposition party with subpoena power can expose decisions the public would otherwise never see, which is a real check on abuse.
    • Needing both parties to agree tends to produce laws that are more broadly supported and harder to reverse later.
    • No single party should be able to push through extreme or untested policy without any real pushback or debate.

  2. When a war causes higher gas prices and economic pain, is it fair for voters to punish the president for it at the ballot box?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes, punish it

    • Higher fuel prices hit ordinary families and businesses immediately, and voters are entitled to judge leaders by that real, lived cost.
    • A government chooses to go to war, and accepting the public costs of that choice — including economic ones — is part of being accountable for it.
    • Short-term economic pain is often the clearest signal ordinary people have for deciding whether a war was worth fighting at all.

    No, look past it

    • A temporary price spike might be a reasonable cost for a longer-term strategic gain that voters can't fully see or judge yet.
    • If leaders know they'll be punished for any short-term economic pain, they may avoid necessary but costly decisions in the future, which could hurt the country more in the long run.
    • Judging a war purely by its effect on gas prices ignores other outcomes — security, alliances, deterrence — that take years to show their value.

China Pours Billions Into Its Shaky Stock Market

China's stock market just had its worst month in ten years. Scared that prices would keep falling, the government jumped in with huge piles of money to try to stop the slide. But lots of regular people are still pulling their savings out, showing they don't fully trust the rescue.

Why this mattersLots of families around the world, including grown-ups you know, keep some of their savings in stock markets, hoping the money grows over time. When a big stock market wobbles this much, it can affect jobs, prices, and how confident people feel about spending money \u2014 even far from China. It's also a real-life example of something you might feel yourself: when someone tells you not to worry, but your gut still says to be careful.
Read the whole story

Imagine a big scoreboard that tracks how much all of China's biggest companies are worth together. That scoreboard is called the CSI 300 index. Last month, it had its worst drop in ten years, meaning the companies on it lost a lot of value very fast.

China's government did not want to watch the number keep falling. So it had special state-backed funds, nicknamed the 'national team,' buy up huge amounts of stock to push prices back up. In just one day, a group of nine investment funds that these state buyers like received 76.7 billion yuan, which is about $11.5 billion. That's roughly enough money to buy a new bicycle for every single person in New York City, with billions of dollars still left over. China's central bank, the institution that controls the country's money, also made it easier for banks to borrow and trade China's currency, the yuan, to help keep things stable.

Even with all that help, many everyday investors are not convinced. Traders who borrow money to buy stocks cut back their holdings to the lowest level in a year. And the number of people opening new stock accounts dropped by more than 30% in October. That's like a third fewer kids showing up to join a club that used to be popular \u2014 a sign that trust is shaky.

So right now, there's a tug of war. The government is pushing stock prices up with huge amounts of money, while many ordinary people are quietly stepping back, still worried that the market isn't as safe as leaders say it is.

Words worth knowing
  • stock market — A stock market is a place where small pieces of ownership in companies, called shares, are bought and sold, and their prices rise or fall depending on how much people believe the companies are worth.
  • central bank — A central bank is the part of a country's government that controls its money, like lending to banks or changing rules, to try to keep the economy steady; in China, this is called the PBOC.

China's Government Pours Billions Into the Stock Market — But Ordinary Investors Aren't Convinced

China's main stock index just had its worst month in ten years, so the government launched an unusually aggressive rescue — secret state buying, a flood of share buybacks, and new tools from the central bank to prop up both stocks and the currency. Prices went up. But the people who actually trade in China's markets are pulling back, not piling in.

Why this mattersChina's stock market is the second-largest in the world, and wobbles there ripple into global trade, the value of things your family might own through pension or investment funds, and how much confidence companies worldwide have in doing business with China. It's also a clean example of a question that comes up everywhere money and government meet: how much can officials actually control an economy by spending, when the people who have to believe in it are the ones holding back?
Read the whole story

The CSI 300, an index that tracks 300 of China's biggest listed companies (similar to how the S&P 500 tracks major U.S. firms), just finished its worst month in a decade. Rather than let the market find its own bottom, Beijing responded with a coordinated push across several arms of government at once — a scale of intervention that signals real alarm about what a prolonged crash could do to confidence in the broader economy.

Much of the buying came from what traders call the 'national team' — a loose nickname for state-backed funds and institutions that quietly buy shares when the government wants to stop a slide, without ever officially announcing it. According to Bloomberg, these funds increased their purchases, and more listed companies stepped up buybacks, where a company uses its own cash to repurchase its shares, which reduces the number available to trade and tends to push the price up. The clearest sign of how much firepower was involved: a basket of nine exchange-traded funds (ETFs, which bundle many stocks into one tradeable product) favored by state money pulled in 76.7 billion yuan, about $11.5 billion, in a single day — the second-largest one-day inflow on record for these funds.

The central bank, the People's Bank of China (PBOC), joined in too, rolling out a new currency swap facility and cutting relending rates — essentially making it cheaper for other banks to borrow from it. These moves weren't just about stocks. A falling Chinese currency, the yuan, can make investors even more nervous and encourage money to leave the country altogether, so supporting the currency and supporting the stock market became part of the same effort.

Yet the people the rescue is ultimately meant to reassure don't seem reassured. SCMP reported that leveraged traders — investors who borrow money to buy more stock than they could otherwise afford, betting that the price will rise enough to cover the loan — trimmed their holdings to a one-year low even as prices climbed. New stock-account openings fell by more than 30% in October compared with the previous month. In other words, the state can move prices with its own money, but it can't force ordinary citizens to believe the rally is real.

Nikkei and Caixin, reporting separately from inside China, both confirmed the PBOC's liquidity measures and the wave of buybacks, which gives the core facts more than one independent trail. Worth knowing, though: Caixin operates under constraints set by the Chinese government, and SCMP is owned by Alibaba, one of China's largest companies — both are useful for understanding how the story is being told inside China, but neither is a fully independent check on it.

Words worth knowing
  • National team — A nickname for state-backed funds and institutions in China that buy shares to support the stock market, usually without officially announcing it.
  • Share buyback — When a company uses its own money to repurchase its shares, which reduces how many are available to trade and tends to push the price up.
  • CSI 300 — An index tracking 300 of China's largest publicly listed companies, used as a key measure of how China's stock market is doing overall.

  1. Should governments step in to stop a stock market from crashing, or let it fall and recover on its own?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Step in

    • A crash can spiral into panic selling, where falling prices scare more people into selling, so quick intervention can stop a correction from becoming a wider crisis.

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes, it worked

    • Stopping the crash was the main goal, and halting panic selling keeps the whole financial system stable, which matters more urgently than how investors feel in the short term.
    • Confidence usually returns only after prices have been stable for a while, so judging the rescue after just one month of retail data is premature.

    • A stock market is supposed to reflect real belief in the value of companies, so if ordinary investors are pulling back and opening fewer accounts, the rally looks artificial rather than genuine.
    • Relying on state funds to keep buying isn't sustainable forever, so an intervention that doesn't restore real trust just postpones the actual test.
    • If underlying company earnings and the broader economy haven't improved, pushing up index prices while real participation falls may just be buying the appearance of stability rather than fixing the problem.

A Tiny Computer Part Just Helped Make a $5 Trillion Company

A company called AMD makes special computer chips that help computers 'think' for AI programs. AMD said it expects to sell about $17 billion worth of these chips in just three months, way more than experts guessed. On the very same day, another chip maker, Nvidia, became worth more than $5 trillion, the most valuable company on Earth.

Why this mattersEven if you've never touched one of these chips, they're probably inside the phone or tablet you use, powering the AI helpers and games you play with. When companies like AMD and Nvidia do well or poorly, it affects jobs, new gadgets, and even how much things cost — in your town and around the world.
Read the whole story

AMD makes tiny chips that go inside computers. These chips are the 'brains' that let computers run AI programs, the kind that can write stories, make pictures, or answer questions.

AMD told everyone it expects to sell about $17 billion worth of these chips soon. That's such a big number that if you spent a million dollars every single day, it would still take you more than 46 years to spend it all. People who study companies were surprised because they thought AMD would sell less.

That same day, Nvidia, another chip company, became worth more than $5 trillion. If you tried to spend $5 trillion one dollar every second, it would take you over 150,000 years, longer than humans have had writing. Nvidia is now the single most valuable company in the whole world.

Behind all this is a race between the United States and China to build the best AI chips. One tech boss warned that some free Chinese AI programs are like a 'Trojan horse': a gift that secretly brings along Chinese-made chips and computer systems, which could make people use less of Nvidia's American chips.

Words worth knowing
  • AI chip — A tiny computer part specially built to help run AI programs, like the ones that create pictures or chat with you.
  • Trojan horse — A phrase from an old Greek story about a hidden trick gift; today it means something that looks helpful but secretly brings a hidden downside.

AMD's Giant Sales Forecast and Nvidia's $5 Trillion Milestone Show How Much the World Runs on AI Chips Now

AMD, a company that makes computer chips, told investors it expects to sell about $17 billion worth of chips in the final three months of the year — far more than analysts had guessed. Its stock jumped 7% on the news. On the very same day, rival chipmaker Nvidia's stock rose enough to push its total value above $5 trillion, making it the most valuable public company on Earth, ahead of giants like Apple and Microsoft.

Why this mattersThe chips at the center of this story are the same technology behind the AI tools many people already use for homework help, photo editing, or video game graphics, so how this competition plays out affects what those tools can do and who controls them. It's also a preview of a bigger pattern in your lifetime: major economic and political power increasingly flowing toward whoever controls the most advanced computing technology.
Read the whole story

Numbers this size are hard to picture, so start with what's driving them: artificial intelligence. Training and running AI systems — the kind behind chatbots, image generators, and the recommendation engines that run social media — requires specialized chips that can do enormous amounts of calculation at once. AMD and Nvidia are the two biggest companies making these chips, and right now, demand for them is outrunning even optimistic predictions. AMD's $17 billion forecast for just one quarter beat the average analyst estimate of $16.3 billion — a gap of over half a billion dollars that investors read as proof the AI boom still has room to run.

Nvidia's situation shows the same story from a different angle. Its stock rose 3% that day, pushing its total market value — the price of all its shares added together, which is how investors measure what a company is "worth" — back above $5 trillion. To put that in perspective, it's roughly the size of the entire economies of the UK and France combined. Nvidia had briefly lost the title of world's most valuable company to a rival and then took it back, which tells you how tightly the market is now watching AI chip sales as a signal of where the whole economy is headed.

Underneath the stock moves sits a quieter, more serious contest: the United States and China are racing to control who builds the chips and software that power AI, because whoever leads in AI is expected to have major advantages in everything from the military to medicine to manufacturing. The U.S. has restricted exports of advanced chips to China for exactly this reason, worried that Chinese companies and the Chinese government could use them for weapons development or mass surveillance. China, in turn, has been building its own chip industry and promoting AI software it gives away for free.

That's where a warning from Reflection AI's chief executive comes in. His company builds AI systems, and he argued that free, publicly available Chinese AI models — ones anyone can download and use, no purchase required — aren't simply generous gifts. He called them "Trojan horses," a reference to the ancient Greek story where soldiers hid inside a giant wooden horse offered as a gift, then attacked the city from within once it was inside the gates. His point: using a free Chinese AI model can quietly pull a company toward also using Chinese-made chips and Chinese data centers — the physical buildings full of servers that actually run the software — which could slowly shift AI infrastructure away from U.S. companies like Nvidia, especially in countries that aren't siding firmly with either the U.S. or China.

All of this happened inside a broader rally — a day where AI-related stocks across the board climbed, not just AMD and Nvidia. That matters because it shows investors aren't just betting on one company; they're betting that AI spending overall is going to keep growing fast, which has consequences well beyond the stock market, since it shapes what gets built, where factories expand, and which countries end up supplying the technology everyone else depends on.

Words worth knowing
  • Market valuation — The total worth of a company, calculated by multiplying its share price by the number of shares it has issued.
  • Sales forecast — A company's own prediction of how much money it expects to make in a future period, which investors compare against outside analysts' guesses.
  • Trojan horse (as used here) — A metaphor for something that looks like a helpful gift but is designed to create hidden dependence or vulnerability — here, free AI software that pulls users toward a country's chips and infrastructure.
