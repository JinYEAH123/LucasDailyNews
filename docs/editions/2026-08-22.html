The US and Canada Are in a Big Money Fight

The United States and Canada have been friends and trading partners for a very long time, buying and selling things back and forth every single day. Last night, talks to keep that trade friendly broke down right before a deadline. Now the U.S. is charging an extra 50% tax on Canadian goods like wine, hockey sticks, cement, and plywood — and Canada says it will charge the same tax back on American goods starting next month.

Why this mattersIf your family buys Canadian maple syrup, wine, or wood for building things, those prices could go up soon. It also matters because the U.S. and Canada have been each other's biggest trading partner for decades, so when they stop cooperating, businesses and workers on both sides of the border feel it.

Imagine your neighbor buys your lemonade every day, and you buy their cookies. That's kind of what the U.S. and Canada do, but with things like cars, wood, and food — worth way more money.

Last night, the two countries could not agree on new trading rules before a midnight deadline. So the U.S. put a 50% tariff — a special extra tax — on about $20 billion worth of Canadian goods. If you spent one dollar every single second, it would take you more than 600 years to spend that much money.

A hockey stick that used to cost $50 to bring into the U.S. now costs $75, because of that extra tax.

Canada's leader, Prime Minister Mark Carney, said the U.S. terms were unfair. He promised Canada would tax American goods back by the exact same amount, starting next month.

U.S. and Canada Slap Huge Tariffs on Each Other After Trade Talks Collapse

Trade talks between the United States and Canada fell apart just before a midnight deadline. The U.S. responded by placing a 50% tax on about $20 billion worth of Canadian goods — including wine, hockey sticks, cement and plywood — and Canada says it will hit back with matching tariffs next month.

Why this mattersEven if you don't buy Canadian wine or hockey sticks, tariffs like these tend to ripple into prices for everyday things — construction materials, packaged goods, anything built using parts or ingredients that cross that border. It's also a reminder that even friendly countries with decades of connected economies can hit a wall when their governments can't agree on what's fair.
Read the whole story

At midnight, the clock ran out. American and Canadian negotiators had been trying to reach a new trade deal, but talks broke down right before the deadline. Hours later, the United States imposed a 50% tariff — a tax on imported goods — on roughly $20 billion worth of products from Canada. The list includes things you might not expect: wine bottles, hockey sticks, bags of cement, and sheets of plywood used to build houses.

A 50% tariff means a Canadian company selling a $100 hockey stick to a store in the U.S. now effectively has to pay $50 extra just to get it across the border. That cost usually gets passed on — either the store charges American shoppers more, or the Canadian company earns much less, or both. Multiply that across thousands of products and you start to see why $20 billion in tariffs is a very big deal.

Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney did not hide his frustration. He called the American terms 'uneconomic' and 'unfair,' saying they 'undermined the net benefits for Canada' — in plain terms, that the deal on the table would have cost Canada more than it gained. Carney promised that Canada will respond 'dollar for dollar,' meaning it plans to impose its own tariffs on American goods worth roughly the same amount, starting next month.

This matters more than a normal disagreement between two countries. The U.S. and Canada have long shared the closest and largest trading relationship either country has with anyone. Car parts cross the border multiple times before a vehicle is finished. Farmers, energy companies, and factories on both sides depend on cheap, easy trade with their neighbor. A rupture this size doesn't just affect wine drinkers and hockey players — it touches jobs and prices in industries most people never think about.

Exactly who caused the breakdown depends on who you ask. American officials and Canadian officials each blame the other for demanding unreasonable terms, and there's no independent referee to settle it. It's also unclear how long the new tariffs will stay in place, or whether the two sides will go back to the negotiating table before Canada's retaliation kicks in next month. For now, businesses on both sides of the border are left waiting to see how expensive their neighbor is about to become.

Words worth knowing
  • tariff — A tax a government puts on goods coming in from another country, usually paid by the company importing them.
  • retaliate — To respond to an action by doing something similar back — here, Canada plans to tax American goods because the U.S. taxed Canadian goods.
  • trade deal — An agreement between countries about the rules for buying and selling goods across their borders.

The US Says It Will Try to Cut Off Iran's Money

The United States and Iran have been locked in a tense standoff for months, and it just got more serious. The US President says America is preparing an "economic D-Day" — a massive push to block Iran's money. Iran says it would rather lose everything it has than back down.

Why this mattersWhen two powerful countries fight with money instead of only weapons, it can still change prices you notice, like the cost of gas for cars. It also shows that disagreements between countries don't always end quickly — sometimes both sides keep pushing, even when it costs them a lot.

The US and Iran have been arguing for a long time. A deadline for the two countries to make a deal came and went with no agreement. Now President Trump says the US is planning something huge: an "economic D-Day," a big wave of penalties meant to cut off the money that keeps Iran's government running.

These penalties are called sanctions. Think of it like taking away someone's allowance so they can't buy anything. When the US puts sanctions on Iran, it tries to stop banks and companies everywhere from doing business with it — especially buying Iran's oil, one of its biggest sources of money.

Iran's top parliament leader said Iran is ready to "sacrifice our wealth" rather than lose its "dignity and honor." In other words, Iran might rather go broke than look weak. China, which buys a lot of oil from Iran, said the sanctions plan doesn't help anyone.

The conflict is also costing the US expensive machines. New reports say at least 45 of America's MQ-9 Reaper drones — planes flown by remote control with no pilot inside — have been shot down. That's about one out of every four of these drones, worth more than $1.3 billion altogether, which is more money than almost anyone could spend in a hundred lifetimes.

It started with a missed deadline. Talks aimed at a new nuclear agreement between the U.S. and Iran passed their cutoff date with no deal reached. President Trump then announced that the U.S. is preparing what he called an 'economic D-Day' — borrowing the name of the famous World War II invasion that turned the tide of the war, to signal that this new round of pressure is meant to be decisive and overwhelming. Sanctions are official rules a country uses to cut off another country's trade and money, aimed at forcing a change in behavior. This time, the goal is to make Iran's economy collapse so badly that its government struggles to function at all.

Iran is not backing down, at least not yet. Iran's parliament speaker — the head of the country's national legislature, similar to the Speaker of the House in the U.S. — said Iran is prepared to 'sacrifice our wealth' to defend the nation's 'dignity and honor.' That is a clear signal: Iran does not plan to give in anytime soon.

This fight also pulls in other countries. China, one of the largest buyers of Iranian oil, publicly pushed back against the sanctions plan, saying it does not 'fit the interest of any party.' That shows this is no longer just a two-country dispute — it touches global energy markets and trade relationships far beyond Washington and Tehran.

The costs are not only diplomatic. A separate analysis found that at least 45 of the U.S. military's advanced MQ-9 Reaper drones — about a quarter of the entire fleet, worth more than $1.3 billion — have been shot down or lost during the broader conflict. The Reaper is a large, remotely piloted aircraft the U.S. uses for spying and airstrikes, and one of its most expensive tools. That number is a reminder that this standoff already carries real military costs, not just angry statements.

It's worth noting that coverage of these latest developments traces back to only two newsrooms: CNN and Al Jazeera. Al Jazeera is funded by the government of Qatar, which is worth keeping in mind when weighing how it frames Iran's side of the story. And whether this new sanctions push will actually force Iran back to the negotiating table, or simply harden its resistance, is something even experts disagree about right now.

More Than a Week Without Power: Indiana's Long Wait After the Storm

A deadly storm tore through Indiana more than a week ago, snapping trees and knocking down power lines, and tens of thousands of people still don't have electricity. Schools remain closed and businesses can't open, so families are relying on flashlights, generators, and patience.

Why this mattersEven if you don't live in Indiana, this story is a reminder of how much everyday life depends on an invisible system, the power grid, and how fragile that system can be when severe weather hits. It's also a preview of a question more places may face as extreme storms become more common: how much should communities invest to prevent this from happening, and who should pay for it?
Read the whole story

More than a week ago, a powerful storm swept across Indiana, snapping trees, tearing down power lines, and leaving tens of thousands of homes and businesses without electricity. The storm was deadly, and the damage was severe enough that in many areas, the power still has not come back on.

A week without electricity is not a small inconvenience, it reshapes almost everything about daily life. Refrigerators stop keeping food cold, furnaces and air conditioners shut off, phones and laptops run out of battery, and in homes that get their water from a well, even the water pump stops working because it runs on electric power. Traffic lights go dark, turning ordinary intersections into confusing four-way stops. For families that depend on electric medical equipment, like oxygen machines, the stakes are even higher.

The outage has closed schools across the affected areas for over a week, meaning students have missed classes they can't easily make up. Businesses, from grocery stores to auto shops, have been unable to reopen because they can't run cash registers, refrigerate goods, or simply turn on the lights, cutting off both paychecks for workers and services the community relies on.

Utility crews, sometimes called linemen, have been working long hours to restore power, and when damage is this widespread, companies often ask utilities from other states for extra workers and trucks, an arrangement known as mutual aid. But when a storm topples trees across many miles of small towns and rural roads at once, restoring power isn't as simple as flipping a switch: crews often have to clear fallen trees before they can even reach damaged lines, then repair or replace poles and wires section by section.

Right now, the exact number of people still without power and a firm date for full restoration haven't been confirmed by more than one news organization. This story is currently based on reporting from NPR alone, so some of the numbers here should be treated as provisional until other outlets can confirm them independently.

Words worth knowing
  • power outage — A period of time when the electricity supply to homes or businesses is cut off, also often called a blackout.
  • power grid — The network of wires, poles, and equipment that carries electricity from power plants to homes and businesses.
  • mutual aid — An arrangement where companies or organizations, like power utilities, send workers and equipment to help each other during emergencies.

  1. Should power companies be required to spend huge amounts of money burying power lines underground to prevent long outages like this one, even though it would raise electric bills for everyone, including in years with no storms at all?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Bury the lines

    • Underground lines are far less likely to be knocked down by falling trees or high winds, so outages become rarer and shorter.
