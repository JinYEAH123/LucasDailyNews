Two Powerful Leaders Are Meeting: Trump and Xi Sit Down at the White House

This week, the leader of China, Xi Jinping, is coming to meet the leader of the United States, Donald Trump, at the White House. This is Xi's first trip to America in almost three years. The two leaders will talk about trade, technology, and a place called Taiwan.

Why this mattersWhen the leaders of the two biggest countries argue or agree, it can change the price of things you buy, like toys, phones, or sneakers, because of tariffs. It can also affect how safe places like Taiwan feel, and how countries get along with each other for years to come.

China and the United States are the two biggest countries in the world when it comes to money and power. Their leaders don't always get along. But this week, they are going to sit down together in person.

The meeting will happen at the White House, which is where the American president lives and works. It is expected to happen around Thursday. Before the meeting, helpers from both countries already talked about trade — that means buying and selling things between countries.

One big topic will be tariffs. A tariff is like an extra fee a country adds to things bought from another country. The two sides also disagree about Taiwan, an island near China that China says belongs to it, but which governs itself.

Nobody knows yet what will actually be decided. China's government says this meeting is about calming things down. America's government says it will push hard on trade rules and on protecting Taiwan. We will have to wait and see what actually happens Thursday.

Xi and Trump to Meet at the White House — Their First Face-to-Face in Nearly Three Years

China's leader, Xi Jinping, is travelling to Washington to meet President Trump, likely on Thursday, in his first trip to the United States in almost three years. The meeting follows weeks of trade talks between officials from both governments and lands in the middle of arguments over tariffs, Taiwan, and who leads the race in artificial intelligence. No deal has been announced — this is a conversation between two rivals who need each other, not a signing ceremony.

Why this mattersTariffs between these two countries show up as higher prices on everyday things — phones, clothes, electronics — because so much of what's sold worldwide is made in China or depends on Chinese supply chains. Whether the US and China cooperate or clash on AI will shape which jobs and industries grow over the next decade, and how the technology gets used in daily life. And an unresolved dispute over Taiwan carries the rare possibility of a conflict between two major powers, which would affect far more than the region it happens in.

Xi Jinping last set foot in the United States before the pandemic scrambled global travel and relations between the two countries turned openly hostile — trade wars, tech restrictions, military posturing near Taiwan. That nearly three-year gap is itself a sign of how frozen things have been. So a face-to-face meeting, even one that produces no signed agreement, is being watched closely simply because it is happening at all.

The visit didn't come out of nowhere. In the weeks before it, the US Treasury Secretary — the official in charge of America's finances and economic policy — met with China's Vice Premier, one of the country's most senior officials, ranking just below the premier and typically responsible for major economic decisions. Those talks were the groundwork: lower-level officials testing what might be possible before the two presidents sit down together.

Three subjects are expected to dominate the room. The first is Taiwan's status — the decades-old, unresolved question of whether the self-governing island is part of China, as Beijing insists, or effectively independent, a position the US has never officially endorsed but has quietly supported by selling Taiwan weapons and treating it as a de facto ally. The second is tariffs: taxes that governments place on imported goods, which both countries have used against each other, raising costs for businesses and, eventually, for shoppers. The third is artificial intelligence — both nations are racing to build the most capable AI systems, partly for economic advantage and partly because military planners increasingly see AI as a future battlefield tool.

The two governments are already describing the same meeting differently, which is worth noticing. Beijing's Foreign Ministry is calling it a chance to stabilise the relationship — language aimed at reassuring its own public and businesses that things won't spiral further. Washington is emphasising toughness: firm trade demands and unresolved worries about Taiwan's security. Neither framing is false exactly, but neither is neutral either — both governments are talking to their own audiences as much as to each other.

The timing adds friction of its own. The meeting arrives alongside separate disagreements over Iran policy and ongoing tariff disputes that have nothing directly to do with Taiwan or AI, meaning the two leaders will be negotiating against a noisy backdrop rather than a clean slate.

Analysts caution against expecting much on paper. The more realistic outcome is tonal: a lowering of temperature, an agreement to keep talking, perhaps a narrow trade concession — rather than any breakthrough on Taiwan, which neither side has shown willingness to budge on in years.

  • Vice Premier — One of China's most senior government officials, ranking just below the premier and often responsible for major economic policy.
  • Taiwan's status — The long-running, unsettled question of whether the self-governing island of Taiwan belongs to China, as Beijing claims, or is effectively independent, a position the US does not officially recognise but quietly supports.

Borrowing Money in America Just Got More Expensive

On Wednesday, the cost of borrowing money in the United States jumped to its highest point since 2007 — a year before many of today's parents even had smartphones. It happened right after new reports showed American businesses growing busier and faster than they have in over four years.

Why this mattersIf your family ever wants to buy a house, a car, or even use a credit card, this news matters — borrowing money for those things could now cost more. It's a bit like the price of 'renting' money going up, even though nothing you buy directly has changed price yet.
Read the whole story

Imagine you want to borrow money to buy a bike, and you promise to pay it back a little at a time, plus some extra as a thank-you for the loan. That extra bit is called interest. This week, the interest rate the U.S. government pays to borrow money shot up to its highest level in 18 years.

This happened because a report came out saying American businesses are busier than they've been since the summer of 2021. That sounds like good news, but it also worried a lot of grown-ups. When the economy speeds up a lot, prices for everyday things — food, gas, toys — often rise faster too. Rising gas prices this week added to that worry.

There's a group in America called the Federal Reserve. Its job is to help decide how expensive or cheap it is to borrow money across the whole country. People had been hoping it would make borrowing cheaper soon. Now, many think it might do the opposite and make borrowing pricier instead.

Some experts say this feels like the 1970s, a time when prices kept climbing while the economy struggled — a mix nicknamed 'stagflation.' Other experts say that comparison doesn't really fit, because today's numbers look quite different. Nobody knows for certain yet which way things will go.

Words worth knowing

  • inflation — Inflation is when the prices of everyday things, like snacks or bus tickets, slowly go up over time.

US Government Borrowing Costs Hit Highest Level Since 2007

On Wednesday, the interest rate the US government pays to borrow money for ten years jumped to about 5.1%, the highest it's been since 2007. New data showing the economy is growing faster than expected, plus rising oil prices, spooked investors into thinking inflation will stay high for longer than hoped.

Why this mattersIf you or your family ever take out a mortgage, a car loan, or a student loan, the interest rate you pay is connected to exactly these numbers — when Treasury yields rise, borrowing gets more expensive for everyone, not just the government. It's also a reminder that good economic news isn't always simple: a booming economy can trigger fears that make life more expensive in other ways.
Read the whole story

When the US government needs money, it borrows it by selling bonds — basically IOUs — and promises to pay interest, called a yield, until it pays the money back. On Wednesday, the yield on the 10-year US Treasury bond climbed to around 5.1%, and the 30-year bond hit about 5.41%. Both are the highest levels seen since 2007, the year before the global financial crisis. That happened right after a widely watched survey (from S&P Global) showed American businesses growing at their fastest pace since July 2021.

Here's the chain reaction: investors only want to lend the government money at a rate that beats inflation, because otherwise they lose purchasing power on the deal. When a strong economic report and rising oil prices suggest inflation might stick around, investors demand a higher yield to compensate. The government has to offer that higher rate to attract buyers, and yields rise across the board. This isn't confined to government debt — mortgage rates, car loan rates, and the interest rates businesses pay to expand or hire all move up in step with Treasury yields, because they're all priced off the same baseline.

The bigger shock came from what investors think the Federal Reserve — the US central bank, which sets a key interest rate that influences all the others — will do next. Just a day earlier, markets (tracked through data from the exchange CME) had priced in a 55% chance the Fed would raise rates again in October. After Wednesday's data, that jumped to 71%. That's a notable swing, because until recently many expected the Fed to start cutting rates, not raising them, as growth cooled. A strong economy sounds like good news, but if it means inflation refuses to fall, the Fed may feel forced to keep rates high — or push them higher — to slow things down.

Some analysts have started using the word 'stagflation' — a mash-up of stagnation and inflation, describing a nasty combination of high prices and weak growth that plagued the US through much of the 1970s, with oil shocks and job losses hitting at the same time as prices spiraled. It's a loaded comparison, because stagflation was hard to fix and took years of painful high interest rates to unwind. But it's disputed: the current data actually shows growth accelerating, not stagnating, so critics argue the comparison is premature or misleading, and reflects fear more than fact.

What's not in dispute is the immediate effect on ordinary borrowing. Anyone shopping for a mortgage, a car loan, or a small business loan in the coming months is likely to face steeper costs than they would have a few weeks ago. For a government already paying enormous sums in interest on its own debt, higher yields mean an even bigger bill — money that could otherwise go toward other spending.

Words worth knowing
  • Treasury yield — The interest rate the US government promises to pay when it borrows money by selling bonds, which serves as a benchmark for many other loan rates in the economy.
  • Federal Reserve rate hike — A decision by the US central bank to raise its key interest rate, usually done to slow inflation by making borrowing and spending more expensive.
  • Stagflation — A rare and painful economic situation where prices keep rising quickly even as economic growth slows down and jobs become harder to find, first seen widely in the 1970s.

  1. With borrowing about to get more expensive for millions of people, should the Federal Reserve raise interest rates further to fight inflation?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Raise rates to fight inflation

    • Letting inflation take hold, as it did in the 1970s, requires much more painful and prolonged rate hikes to fix later.

    Keep the Fed independent

    • Elected officials face pressure to keep rates low before elections, even when that fuels inflation that hurts everyone later.
    • Independent experts making decisions based on economic data, rather than political timing, tends to produce more stable long-term outcomes.
    • Nearly every major economy insulates its central bank from direct political control for this same reason, and the arrangement has generally worked.

    Give elected officials more say

    • Interest rate decisions affect millions of people's mortgages and jobs, and those people have no direct vote over who makes them.
    • A body accountable to voters might weigh the pain of high rates on ordinary families more heavily than one focused mainly on inflation numbers.
    • Concentrating major economic power in a small, unelected group raises legitimate questions about democratic accountability.

China's Biggest Online Store Says It Built a Super-Fast Computer Chip

Alibaba, the company behind China's largest online shopping website, just showed off a brand new computer chip. They say it's their most powerful one ever, built to power artificial intelligence, or AI, the kind of computer smarts that can chat, write, or recognize pictures. Alibaba also announced huge new computer warehouses to run that AI. The news made Alibaba's stock price jump way up.

Why this mattersAI chips like this one power things you might already use, like voice assistants, video game graphics, or apps that write stories and draw pictures. If China builds its own powerful chips instead of buying them from the U.S., it could change which countries lead in AI technology for years to come, the same way it matters who builds the best rockets or vaccines.
Read the whole story

Alibaba is a giant Chinese company, a bit like if Amazon and Google were mixed together. This week they revealed a new chip called the Zhenwu V900. A chip is a tiny piece of computer hardware, smaller than your thumbnail, that does all the thinking inside a computer.

Alibaba says this chip will help power AI in China without needing chips from American companies like Nvidia. Nvidia makes some of the best AI chips in the world, but the U.S. government has made rules stopping companies from selling their most advanced chips to China.

Alongside the chip, Alibaba announced plans to build enormous data centers, buildings packed with thousands of computers that run AI programs. They said these new centers could use about 20 gigawatts of power. That's roughly enough electricity to power several large countries' worth of homes at once.

Nobody outside Alibaba has actually tested the new chip yet to see if it really works as well as Nvidia's chips. So for now, we only have Alibaba's word for how good it is.

Alibaba Says It Built an AI Chip to Rival Nvidia — But No One Outside the Company Has Proven It Works

Alibaba, one of China's biggest tech companies, announced a new AI chip called the Zhenwu V900 this week, along with plans to build roughly 20 gigawatts of data-center capacity to run it. The company says the chip will help China rely less on American semiconductor makers like Nvidia. Investors reacted immediately, sending Alibaba's stock price sharply upward — even though no outside lab has tested whether the chip actually performs as claimed.

Why this mattersThe chips inside your phone, your school's computers, and the servers running apps like ChatGPT all come from this same global supply chain that the U.S. and China are now fighting over. If China succeeds in building a fully independent chip industry, it reshapes who controls the technology behind AI for decades — and if American restrictions instead just slow China down at great cost to both sides, that's a different world than one where the two countries kept trading freely. Either way, this rivalry will likely determine which country's AI systems, and which country's rules for them, end up shaping the tools you use.
Read the whole story

Here's what actually happened: Alibaba Cloud, the company's cloud-computing division, unveiled a new AI processor it's calling its most powerful chip yet, and paired the announcement with plans for a huge buildout of data centers — the warehouse-sized buildings full of servers that train and run AI systems. The company framed all of this explicitly as part of China's effort to catch up with American chipmakers, especially Nvidia, whose chips currently dominate the AI industry worldwide.

To understand why this matters, you need to know about export controls — U.S. government rules that block American companies from selling their most advanced chips to China. Washington introduced these restrictions starting in 2022, arguing that cutting-edge AI chips could help China's military and surveillance capabilities, and that keeping China a step behind serves American national security. The effect has been to starve Chinese AI companies of the best hardware money can buy, at least the American kind.

Beijing's response has been to push its major tech companies — Alibaba, Huawei, and others — to build a homegrown alternative: chips designed and manufactured without relying on U.S. technology at any point in the supply chain. This is expensive and technically difficult, since the equipment needed to manufacture advanced chips is itself dominated by a handful of companies, several of them American or allied with the U.S. But the strategic logic is straightforward: if China can't buy the best foreign chips, it wants to stop needing them at all.

The scale of the data-center plan is worth pausing on. Twenty gigawatts is an enormous amount of electrical capacity — for comparison, that's roughly equivalent to the power output of about twenty large nuclear reactors, all dedicated to running computer servers rather than homes or factories. Building infrastructure at that scale, if it actually happens as described, would represent one of the largest bets any single company has made on AI's future.

Here's the catch: nobody outside Alibaba has independently verified how the Zhenwu V900 actually performs against Nvidia's chips in real-world tasks. Benchmark testing — running standardized tasks on a chip to measure its speed and efficiency against competitors — hasn't been published by any neutral lab. That didn't stop investors from bidding up Alibaba's share price on the announcement alone, which tells you something about how much the market is currently willing to reward a good AI story, whether or not the substance behind it has been proven yet.

Chinese state-friendly coverage, like that from Caixin Global, has emphasized the chip as a milestone in China's self-sufficiency drive — a framing worth noting as a perspective rather than a settled fact, since Caixin is a Chinese-owned outlet reporting largely from that angle. Independent reporting from outlets like CNBC and Nikkei Asia has covered the announcement more cautiously, flagging the same gap: an impressive claim, a stock price that moved on it, and no outside proof yet.

Words worth knowing
  • export controls — Government rules that block companies from selling certain advanced technology, like top-tier computer chips, to specific other countries.
  • gigawatt — A huge unit of electrical power, roughly equal to the output of a large nuclear power plant, often used to describe how much energy a massive data center needs.
  • semiconductor — A material, usually silicon, used to make computer chips — the tiny electronic circuits that power everything from phones to AI systems.

  1. Should the U.S. government keep restricting the sale of advanced chips to China, even though it's pushing China to build its own supply and possibly succeed faster?

    • Self-sufficiency means a country's economy and security don't depend on the political decisions of another government.

    No, prioritize efficient trade

    • Building duplicate supply chains from scratch wastes enormous resources that could go toward actual innovation instead of reinventing what already exists elsewhere.
    • Global trade lets each country specialize in what it does best, which historically produces better and cheaper technology for everyone.
    • Chasing self-sufficiency can slow down real progress, since going it alone is usually less efficient than building on the world's combined expertise.
