America's Navy Buys Part of a Submarine-Parts Company

The United States government just did something unusual with submarines. Instead of only paying a company to build parts, it bought an actual share of the company. The deal will help build a brand-new factory near Baltimore, a city in the state of Maryland. The plan is about building more submarines faster, because China is also building up its navy.

Why this mattersThe money for deals like this comes from taxes, the money the government collects from working adults, including maybe your own parents. It shows how decisions about ships and submarines built far away can still change how that tax money gets spent, and how many jobs exist near a shipyard.
Read the whole story

Imagine your school paid an ice cream truck driver to bring treats every Friday. That's a normal deal, like a contract. Now imagine your school decided to buy a part of the ice cream truck business instead. That's closer to what just happened with a company called Anduril, which makes weapons and military equipment.

The US Navy, the part of the military that uses ships and submarines, agreed to help build a new shipyard near Baltimore. A shipyard is a place where workers build and fix big ships and submarines. This one will make parts for submarines.

News reports gave different numbers for how much money is involved. Some said $2.9 billion, others said $3.7 billion, and one report added everything up to about $6.6 billion. These numbers may be describing different pieces of the same deal rather than disagreeing, but no single clear total has been published yet.

The US government says it needs stronger submarine-building power to keep up with China, whose navy has been growing quickly. This isn't the only time the government has bought a piece of a weapons company lately. It's becoming a pattern.

The US Navy Just Bought Into a Submarine Startup — Instead of Just Buying From It

The US Navy and a defense-tech company called Anduril Industries announced a deal to build submarine parts at a new shipyard near Baltimore, Maryland. The unusual part isn't the money — it's that the federal government is taking an ownership stake in the venture, rather than simply paying for equipment the normal way.

Why this mattersDecisions like this shape what kind of jobs exist in manufacturing towns, how much of your future tax money is tied up in private companies, and how directly the government can influence industries it has a financial stake in rather than just a buyer's relationship with. It's also a preview of a bigger question: as the US and China compete more openly, how much should government and private business blend together to win that race?
Read the whole story

For most of modern history, when the US military needed something built, it signed a contract: here is the design, here is the price, deliver it by this date. The new arrangement between the Navy and Anduril works differently. Instead of just paying for submarine components, the government is putting money in and taking a piece of ownership in the shipyard itself — something closer to what a venture capital investor does when backing a startup than what a government agency usually does when buying hardware.

The exact size of the deal is hard to pin down, because different outlets are describing different slices of it. CNBC reported a $2.9 billion Navy contract. The Hill and the Washington Post described a $3.7 billion investment in the shipyard. Another report put the whole package at around $6.6 billion. These numbers likely aren't contradicting each other — a contract for specific parts, an investment in the facility that makes them, and a total figure covering both would naturally look different — but no single outlet has laid out a reconciled total, so the full shape of the deal remains somewhat unclear even now.

The stated reason for all this is competition with China, whose navy now has more ships than the US Navy, even though American vessels are generally larger and more advanced. Submarines and their components are expensive and slow to build, and American shipyards have struggled for years with backlogs and workforce shortages. By building a new facility focused specifically on undersea manufacturing — and by taking a financial stake rather than just placing an order — the government is betting that direct involvement will get submarines built faster than contracts alone have managed.

This fits a broader pattern under the Trump administration: rather than only funding private defense and manufacturing companies through contracts, the government has been taking direct ownership positions in some of them. That's a meaningful shift. A contract ends when the work is delivered. An ownership stake means the government has a continuing financial interest in whether the company succeeds, fails, or is sold — and potentially more say in how it's run.

Anduril itself is worth a second look. It was founded by Palmer Luckey, a young tech entrepreneur best known before this for building virtual-reality headsets. Separately, according to Al Jazeera's reporting, Luckey has joined an informal 'war study' group alongside Elon Musk — a detail that underlines how a small circle of tech founders now sits close to both the business of American defense and the people making decisions about it.

Words worth knowing
  • Equity stake — Partial ownership of a company, meaning you share in its profits, losses, and decisions rather than just buying something from it.
  • Shipyard — A facility where ships and submarines are built, repaired, or assembled, often employing thousands of skilled workers.
  • Defense contractor — A private company that builds weapons, vehicles, or other military equipment for the government, usually under a paid contract rather than government ownership.

  1. Should the government own part of a weapons company instead of just buying things from it?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes, owning a share makes sense

    • If the government owns part of the company, it can make sure submarines get built on time.
    • Any profits from owning a share can come back to help pay for the military, not just the company's owners.
    • Owning a stake gives the government more say in how factories and shipyards are run.

    No, government shouldn't own private companies

    • Private companies often work best when they compete freely, without government as a partner.
    • If government owns part of one company, it might treat that company better than its fair competitors.
    • Mixing government money with private ownership makes it harder to know who really decides what gets built.

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes, build more submarines

    • Having strong defenses can make other countries think twice before starting trouble.
    • Falling behind in technology could leave sailors with older, less safe equipment.
    • New shipyards create many new jobs for workers and engineers.

    No, don't race to build more weapons

    • Building more weapons on both sides can turn into an expensive race that doesn't make anyone safer.
    • Money spent on submarines could instead go to schools, hospitals, or scientific research.
    • Competing to build more military equipment can make tensions between countries worse instead of better.

  1. Should the government become a part-owner of private companies instead of just buying what it needs from them?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Government ownership stakes are a good idea

    • It gives taxpayers a direct share in the value and profit created with public money, not just a bill to pay.
    • It lets the government steer production toward urgent national priorities more directly than a one-time contract allows.
    • It can reduce financial risk for companies working in slow, expensive industries like shipbuilding, encouraging investment that might not otherwise happen.

    Government ownership stakes are a bad idea

    • Government picking which companies to invest in can crowd out competitors and reduce the pressure to innovate.
    • It blurs the line between public power and private business, creating conflicts of interest that are hard to monitor.
    • Taxpayers go from being customers, insulated from a company's losses, to part-owners exposed to them if the venture struggles.

  2. Is framing manufacturing policy mainly around competing with China the right way to rebuild American industry?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes, the framing is useful

    • A clear rival focuses political attention and funding that might otherwise stall in Congress for years.
    • China's naval expansion is real and well documented, so preparing for it is a reasonable use of government resources.
    • National security spending tends to survive political changes in a way that purely economic arguments for manufacturing often don't.

    No, the framing is risky

    • Framing every industrial decision as a race against China can be used to justify spending that isn't properly scrutinized.
    • Leaning on rivalry to drive policy risks escalating tensions in ways that make conflict more likely, not less.
    • America's underlying manufacturing problems — skills shortages, high costs, slow permitting — won't be solved just by invoking a rival.

A Newspaper Asks Hard Questions About How ICE Arrests People

The Washington Post, a big American newspaper, dug into how ICE works. ICE is the U.S. agency that finds and arrests people who are in the country without legal permission. The newspaper found three things that worry a lot of people. So far, only this one newspaper has reported them, so nobody else has checked the details yet.

Why this mattersWhen any group has the power to arrest people, grown-ups want to make sure it is used carefully and fairly, not just quickly. These stories are about ICE, but the questions are ones you'll meet again: should the people in charge follow clear rules, and what happens when someone thinks those rules were broken? You might even know a family that has dealt with ICE, since it operates in neighborhoods across the country.

One Newsroom's Reports Raise Hard Questions About How ICE Treats People It Detains

The Washington Post published a set of reports about U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE), the federal agency that arrests and deports people living in the country without legal permission. The reports describe detainees being asked whether they voted illegally, internal emails showing the agency shortened training for local police officers helping with immigration arrests, and a man who says he plans to sue ICE after being hospitalized during his arrest. All three claims come from a single newsroom, and no other outlet has independently confirmed them yet, so they should be read as one organization's reporting rather than settled fact.

Why this mattersHow ICE questions people, trains the officers who detain them, and handles use of force affects anyone who interacts with the immigration system, including green card holders, visa holders, and even U.S. citizens occasionally caught up in enforcement by mistake. It also shapes a bigger question every democracy has to answer: how much power to give enforcement agencies, and how carefully to check that power is used fairly.
Read the whole story

Immigration enforcement in the United States has sped up this year, and ICE, the agency responsible for finding, detaining, and deporting people without legal status, has been at the center of it. Ahead of the November midterm elections, when voters pick members of Congress and many state and local officials, the Washington Post published several findings about how ICE operates day to day. Taken together, they paint a picture of an agency moving fast and, according to the Post's sources, cutting some corners along the way.

The first finding involves detainees being asked whether they voted illegally while in federal custody. Voting by non-citizens is already a crime, but the timing and context of these questions, reportedly happening inside detention facilities as part of broader processing, has raised eyebrows among legal observers the Post spoke with. Critics worry the questions could be used to build voter-fraud narratives ahead of an election, while ICE would likely argue it is simply checking for a separate category of lawbreaking it is required to flag.

The second finding concerns training. ICE doesn't just rely on its own federal agents; it also deputizes local police officers through a program that lets them carry out immigration enforcement duties under federal authority. Internal emails reviewed by the Post reportedly show that required training for these local officers was shortened. Training matters here because deputized officers are given new legal powers, including the authority to detain people for immigration violations, a job that normally requires specific knowledge of federal law that local police don't otherwise need.

The third finding is a single case: a man hospitalized during an ICE arrest who is reportedly preparing a lawsuit against the agency. Lawsuits like this, when they happen, often turn into the clearest test of whether an agency's use of force during an arrest was reasonable, because a court ends up examining exactly what happened, in detail, under oath.

It's worth being clear about where this information comes from. All three stories trace back to one newsroom, the Washington Post, and no competing outlet has independently verified any of them individually yet. That doesn't mean the claims are false, reporters often break stories alone and get confirmed later, but it does mean readers should treat this as one account currently, not as multi-sourced, independently checked fact.

The backdrop matters too. Immigration enforcement tends to intensify before elections, and ICE's practices, including who gets deputized to help and how they're trained, have become a genuinely contested political issue rather than a purely technical one. Supporters of tougher enforcement see faster, broader action as necessary; civil liberties advocates see thinner training and aggressive tactics as corners cut for speed, with real people bearing the cost if something goes wrong.

Words worth knowing
  • ICE (Immigration and Customs Enforcement) — The U.S. federal agency responsible for finding, detaining, and deporting people living in the country without legal immigration status.
  • Deputized officer — A local police officer given special federal authority, in this case to carry out immigration enforcement duties normally handled by federal agents.
  • Due process — The legal principle that the government must follow fair, established procedures before it can detain, punish, or deport someone.

  1. Is it fair for ICE to ask someone in custody whether they voted illegally, before any judge has decided if that person broke a law?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes, it's fair

    • Officers are allowed to ask questions while they investigate, just like police ask many kinds of questions.
    • If someone really did vote illegally, finding that out quickly can matter for the election.
    • Asking a question isn't the same as punishing someone; the person can still explain themselves.

    No, it's not fair

    • People in custody often feel scared and pressured, so they might answer in a way that hurts themselves without meaning to.
    • The question seems to connect being an immigrant with breaking election rules, which could be unfair to people who did nothing wrong.
    • Everyone is supposed to be treated as not guilty until proven otherwise, and this question seems to skip that step.

  2. If local police officers want to help with immigration arrests quickly, is a shorter training course a reasonable trade-off?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes, shorter training is reasonable

    • More officers helping sooner could mean faster responses in communities that want more enforcement.
    • Local police already have basic training from their own police jobs, so they aren't starting from zero.
    • Rules and extra training can always be added later once officers are already out working.

    No, shorter training is risky

    • Immigration arrests involve specific rules that regular police training doesn't cover, so gaps could lead to mistakes.
    • If officers aren't fully trained, people could get hurt or have their rights ignored during an arrest.
    • Rushing training to arrest more people faster suggests speed matters more than doing the job carefully.

  1. Should local police officers be deputized to carry out federal immigration enforcement, even with shortened training?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes, deputize them

    • Federal immigration agents are limited in number, and local police deputization lets enforcement scale up quickly where it's needed most.
    • Local officers already know their communities and can act faster than federal agents sent in from elsewhere.
    • Some training reduction may reflect streamlining repetitive material rather than cutting substance, and agencies routinely adjust training length without compromising core competence.

    No, don't deputize them

    • Immigration law is complex and federal in nature; giving new powers to officers without adequate training risks wrongful detentions.
    • Local police were hired and trained for community policing, not federal immigration enforcement, and blending the two roles can damage trust between police and immigrant communities they're supposed to protect.
    • When training is cut for speed, the people who bear the risk of mistakes are the detainees, not the officers or the agency.

  2. Should the public treat single-newsroom investigative reporting as reliable before other outlets independently confirm it?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes, take it seriously now

    • Investigative journalists often have exclusive documents and sources, and waiting for confirmation can mean waiting indefinitely while real harm continues.
    • Reputable outlets have strong incentives to get facts right, since their credibility is on the line with every major story.
    • Many true and consequential stories in history broke from a single newsroom before anyone else had the sourcing to confirm them.

    No, wait for confirmation

    • Every newsroom has blind spots or institutional leanings, and relying on one account means relying on one set of editorial choices about what to include and how to frame it.
    • Politically charged timing, like right before an election, creates extra incentive to publish quickly, which can outpace verification.
    • Treating unconfirmed claims as settled fact can shape public opinion and policy debates before the full picture is actually known.

Now, according to the news outlet The Hill, some Republican senators are privately worried this plan is costing their party support before important elections next year. They think the White House has handled the issue badly, even though no one has said this publicly by name.

Why this mattersDecisions about who can stay in the country affect real families, neighbors, and classmates, some of whom may know people worried about being sent away. Now some Republican senators are quietly telling reporters they are nervous. They worry the public is turning against how harshly this is being done, right before elections where many seats in Congress are up for grabs.

Republican Senators Reportedly Worried That Trump's Deportation Push Could Cost Votes

According to the political news outlet The Hill, some Republican senators have privately told the Trump administration that its aggressive push to deport undocumented immigrants is turning into a political problem ahead of next year's midterm elections. The report says unnamed sources describe the White House as having 'fumbled' an issue that was one of Trump's biggest 2024 campaign promises. No senator is named on the record, and no other outlet has independently confirmed the account.

Why this mattersImmigration enforcement touches real neighborhoods — classmates, coworkers, and families — and how aggressively it's carried out shapes daily life for millions of people, regardless of how it plays politically. It's also a useful case study in reading the news carefully: a dramatic claim from unnamed sources is not the same thing as a confirmed fact, and knowing the difference matters as much as knowing the story itself.
Read the whole story

When Donald Trump ran for president in 2024, mass deportation — removing large numbers of people living in the country without legal permission — was one of his signature promises. Since taking office, his administration has carried it out aggressively, including expanded raids and detentions by Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE), the federal agency responsible for finding and removing undocumented immigrants.

Now, according to The Hill, some Republican senators are privately worried that the way this has been handled could hurt their party in the 2026 midterm elections — the nationwide vote held partway through a president's term that decides control of Congress. The concern, as described in the report, is that enforcement has gone far enough to alarm voters in competitive districts without necessarily satisfying the base that demanded it in the first place.

It's worth pausing on how solid this claim actually is. The Hill's report rests entirely on anonymous sources from within the Republican Party, and no senator has put their name to the criticism. No other news organization has been able to confirm that this is a widespread view, rather than the frustration of a handful of staffers or lawmakers. That doesn't mean it's false — but it means readers should treat it as one outlet's reporting, not an established fact about a party in revolt.

The timing matters, because the warning arrives alongside separate and more concrete controversies about how ICE operates. Reports have surfaced that detainees are being questioned about whether they voted illegally in U.S. elections — a serious accusation that, if mishandled, could sweep up people who made honest mistakes or were never involved at all. There are also reports that training was cut short for 'deputized' local officers — police or sheriff's deputies given federal authority to help with immigration enforcement — raising questions about whether they're prepared to use that power carefully.

Strip away the political noise and there's a real strategic argument underneath: does hardline enforcement win elections or lose them? One view holds that visible, aggressive deportation keeps the base energized and proves Trump is delivering on a core promise. The opposing view holds that swing voters — the people who actually decide close elections — are more persuadable than the base, and stories about wrongful detentions or heavy-handed tactics could push them away. Nobody has decided this question yet; it will be tested at the ballot box, not in a memo.

As of this report, none of this has produced any change in law or policy. The administration hasn't softened its approach, and Congress hasn't passed anything in response to the senators' reported concerns. It's a story about private anxiety inside one political party, not about a government decision — at least not yet.

Words worth knowing
  • Mass deportation — A large-scale government effort to find and remove people living in a country without legal permission.
  • Midterm elections — U.S. elections held two years into a president's four-year term, deciding control of Congress without electing a new president.
  • Deputized officer — A local police officer or sheriff's deputy who has been granted federal authority, in this case to help enforce immigration law.
