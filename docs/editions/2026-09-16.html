Lawmakers Tell the President: Ask Us Before More Fighting With Iran

On Tuesday, the House of Representatives voted to say President Trump needs their permission before taking more military action against Iran. It was the third time this year they've voted on this same idea. The very next day, the head of the House sent everyone home for seven weeks, which means a different vote — about the Secretary of Defense — won't happen before they leave.

Why this mattersImagine if your class had a rule that nobody could start a big project without the whole class agreeing first — that's kind of what Congress is arguing about here. This fight is about who gets to decide when America goes to war: one person, or a big group of elected representatives. It also shows how leaders sometimes use scheduling and timing to control which votes actually happen.

The House of Representatives is one part of Congress, the group of lawmakers who make America's rules. Think of it as 435 people whose job is to speak for everyone in the country. On Tuesday, 220 of them voted yes and 204 voted no on something called a war-powers resolution. It says President Trump cannot send more soldiers or weapons against Iran without asking Congress first.

This was the third time since June that Congress voted on this exact question. Seven Republicans, who usually support President Trump, joined the Democrats to vote yes. That's a small but noticeable group breaking from their own team.

On the same day, a Republican lawmaker named Thomas Massie filed papers accusing Pete Hegseth, the person in charge of running the military, of handling the Iran conflict badly. These papers could lead to Hegseth losing his job — but only if the House votes on them.

The next day, the leader of the House, Mike Johnson, sent everyone home for seven weeks so they can prepare for elections. That means the vote on Hegseth won't happen for a long while. Some people think Johnson planned it that way on purpose. Others say the trip home was already scheduled and has nothing to do with dodging that vote.

Congress Votes to Limit Trump's War Powers on Iran — Then Goes Home Before a Bigger Fight

The House of Representatives voted 220-204 on Tuesday to say President Trump should not take further military action against Iran without asking Congress first. Seven Republicans crossed party lines to join every Democrat in voting yes. The next day, a Republican congressman filed formal charges against the Secretary of Defense over how the Iran conflict was handled — and the Speaker of the House sent everyone home for seven weeks before a vote on those charges could happen.

Why this mattersThis is a live argument about who actually gets to decide when the United States goes to war — the elected Congress, or the president acting alone — and it will keep resurfacing any time a future president, of either party, uses military force without asking permission first. It also shows how the tools Congress has for holding officials accountable, like impeachment, can be slowed or avoided through ordinary scheduling decisions rather than open votes.
Read the whole story

Under the U.S. Constitution, only Congress can formally declare war. But presidents have ordered military strikes without that formal declaration for decades, arguing they have the authority to act quickly to protect American interests or forces. Congress pushed back on this in 1973 with something called the War Powers Resolution, a law meant to make presidents check in with lawmakers before military action drags on. Tuesday's vote was the third time since June that the House has used that law to formally object to Trump's actions toward Iran — a sign that a meaningful bloc of Republicans, not just Democrats, think this war was never properly authorized.

The vote itself does not stop anything. It is what's called a resolution, which is closer to a formal statement of opinion than a binding order — the president is not legally required to obey it. The White House and House Republican leadership have said as much, dismissing the vote as symbolic and pointing out that Trump can keep acting militarily regardless of what the resolution says. Supporters of the resolution counter that symbolism has weight too: it puts on the record, three separate times, that a majority of the House — including members of the president's own party — think this conflict lacks public and legal support.

The same day the resolution passed, Kentucky Republican Thomas Massie filed articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth. Impeachment is the constitutional process for formally accusing a government official of serious wrongdoing, with the House acting like a grand jury deciding whether to bring charges, and the Senate holding something like a trial afterward. Massie's articles accuse Hegseth of mishandling how the Iran war was actually carried out — a much sharper and more personal charge than a general resolution about presidential power.

On Wednesday, Speaker Mike Johnson announced the House would break early for a seven-week recess ahead of this year's midterm election campaigns — the elections that will decide control of Congress. One practical effect of that decision is that it makes it impossible for the House to hold a floor vote on Massie's impeachment articles before lawmakers scatter across the country to campaign. Johnson's office says the recess was planned well in advance for campaign purposes and has nothing to do with Hegseth. Critics, including some within Massie's own party, suspect the timing is not a coincidence, since an early break conveniently buries a vote that would force every Republican to publicly choose a side on the defense secretary.

Nobody disputes the vote count on the war-powers resolution or the fact that Massie filed his impeachment articles. What's contested is what any of it actually changes. Does a non-binding resolution that a president can ignore still function as meaningful political pressure, or is it closer to venting? And was sending Congress home really about the election calendar, or about avoiding an uncomfortable vote? Both questions turn less on the facts, which are public record, than on how much good faith you extend to the people making the decisions.

Words worth knowing
  • War Powers Resolution — A 1973 law meant to make U.S. presidents consult Congress before starting or continuing military action, though presidents have often argued they can act first and explain later.
  • Articles of impeachment — A formal written list of accusations against a government official, filed as the first step toward possibly removing that person from office.
  • Congressional recess — A scheduled break when lawmakers leave Washington, often to campaign or work in their home districts, during which the House and Senate do not hold votes.

America's Money Referee Just Made Borrowing Cost More

A group called the Federal Reserve decides how expensive it is to borrow money in America. This week, they made it a little more expensive. It's the first time they've done this since 2023, and it changes bills for almost every family in the country.

Why this mattersIf your family has a car loan, a house payment, or a credit card, this change could mean paying a bit more each month. If your family has money saved in a bank account, you might now earn a little extra. Either way, a decision made by a small group of adults in Washington just reached into kitchens and mailboxes across the whole country.
Read the whole story

Imagine you want to borrow ten dollars from a friend, and pay back eleven dollars later. That extra dollar is like an 'interest rate.' The Federal Reserve, sometimes just called 'the Fed,' sets a kind of interest rate that affects almost every loan in America.

This week, the Fed raised that rate by a small amount, called 25 basis points. That's like turning a dial up just a notch. Now the 'cost' of borrowing money is a little higher than before.

This matters for grown-ups buying houses, buying cars, or using credit cards. Their monthly payments could get bigger. But people who save money in the bank might now earn a little more on their savings.

The Fed says they did this because prices for things like groceries and gas have stayed high, called 'inflation,' even though people are still finding jobs and businesses are doing okay. Some experts worry this could make it harder for companies to hire new workers. Others think it was the right move to stop prices from climbing even higher.

Words worth knowing
  • interest rate — The extra amount you pay back when you borrow money, or the extra amount you earn when you save it.
  • inflation — When prices for things like food and toys keep going up over time, so the same dollar buys less than it used to.

The Fed Raises Interest Rates Again, Betting Against a Cooling Job Market

The Federal Reserve, the institution that sets the cost of borrowing money across the entire U.S. economy, raised its key interest rate by a quarter of a percentage point this week. That reverses a plan it had signaled earlier this year to start cutting rates, and it means loans of almost every kind — mortgages, car payments, credit cards — are about to get a little more expensive. Fed officials say inflation is still too high to let up. Not everyone agrees that's the right call, especially with some warning signs already showing in hiring.

Why this mattersIf your family has a mortgage, a car loan, or credit card debt, this rate hike will likely show up as a slightly bigger bill in the months ahead, since many loan rates are tied to what the Fed does. It also matters for anyone hoping to get a new job soon or a raise at an existing one — how the Fed juggles inflation against hiring shapes how easy or hard it is to find work almost everywhere in the country.
Read the whole story

The Federal Reserve is the United States' central bank — not a bank you can open an account at, but the institution that manages the overall supply and cost of money in the economy. Its main tool is the 'benchmark interest rate,' a target for what banks charge each other to borrow money overnight. That number ripples outward: raise it, and banks raise the rates they charge you for a mortgage, a car loan, or a credit card balance. Lower it, and borrowing gets cheaper, which tends to push people and businesses to spend and invest more.

This week the Fed raised that target range by 25 basis points — a basis point is one-hundredth of a percent, so 25 of them equal a quarter of a percentage point — to a range of 3.75% to 4%. That's a small move in absolute terms, but a significant one in direction. Earlier this year, the Fed had signaled it expected to start cutting rates, on the theory that inflation was cooling enough to ease up. Instead, it just did the opposite for the first time since 2023.

The Fed's stated reason is that inflation — the rate at which prices for everyday things rise — has stayed stubbornly higher than the 2% level the Fed treats as its target, even as the broader economy and job market have kept humming along. When people have jobs and money to spend, businesses can raise prices without losing customers, which can keep inflation elevated. Raising interest rates is meant to cool that off: pricier loans mean people and companies borrow and spend less, which in turn takes some pressure off prices.

But that same cooling effect is exactly what worries critics of the move. Slower spending doesn't just tame prices — it can also mean businesses hire less, delay expansion, or lay people off. Some economists point to recent data showing hiring has already started to slow in parts of the economy, and argue that raising rates now risks tipping a gently cooling job market into a much colder one, right as it was already showing strain.

Other economists counter that backing off too early has its own cost: if inflation is allowed to reaccelerate, the Fed may be forced into even bigger, more painful rate hikes later to bring it back under control. That's roughly what happened in the early 1980s, when the Fed let inflation run for years before crushing it with interest rates near 20%, triggering a deep recession. Today's Fed officials are, in effect, betting that a small hike now prevents a much larger one down the road — though how many policymakers on the Fed's committee agreed with that bet, and whether more hikes are coming this year, hasn't been made fully clear yet.

Words worth knowing
  • Federal Reserve — The United States' central bank, which controls the cost of borrowing money across the whole economy rather than serving individual customers.
  • Basis point — A unit equal to one-hundredth of a percent, used because interest rate changes are often too small to describe cleanly in whole percentages.
  • Inflation — The rate at which prices for goods and services rise over time, which reduces how much the same amount of money can buy.

  1. When inflation and job security pull in opposite directions, should the Fed prioritize keeping prices stable even if it costs some people their jobs?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Prioritize price stability

    • Runaway inflation eventually hurts everyone, including the people it's meant to protect, by eroding wages and savings across the board.
    • Delaying action tends to make the eventual fix worse, requiring even harsher rate hikes and a deeper economic downturn later.
    • Price stability is what lets businesses and families plan confidently, which supports healthy job creation over the long run.

    • Unemployment falls hardest and fastest on people who are already financially vulnerable, unlike moderate inflation which is a slower, more diffuse burden.
    • Economic forecasts about inflation are uncertain, so risking real jobs today to prevent a possible future problem is a heavy trade to make on a guess.

  2. Should a group of unelected officials have this much power to shape the economy for everyone?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes, keep it independent

    • Elected politicians face pressure to keep rates low before elections regardless of what the economy actually needs, which can make inflation worse over time.
    • Interest rate decisions require technical expertise and long time horizons that don't fit neatly into short political election cycles.
    • Central bank independence in many countries has historically correlated with more stable, predictable inflation over the decades.

    No, add more accountability

    • Decisions that raise borrowing costs for nearly every household are deeply political in effect, even if the process is technical, and deserve more democratic input.
    • Unelected officials aren't directly answerable to the people who bear the consequences of their choices, like workers who lose jobs during a slowdown.
    • Concentrating this much economic power in one small committee makes the whole system dependent on the judgment of a few individuals.

OpenAI in Talks for a $1.2 Trillion Valuation — Even Though It's Losing Money

OpenAI, the company behind ChatGPT, is reportedly in early talks with investors that could value it at more than $1.2 trillion — making it the most valuable private company ever, if the deal happens. Nothing is signed yet, and OpenAI is still burning through cash faster than it earns it. The number is being watched closely because it says a lot about how far investors think artificial intelligence can go — or how far their optimism has already run ahead of the evidence.

Why this mattersEven if you've never used ChatGPT, decisions made inside a $1.2 trillion company ripple outward — into what jobs look like, what software you're required to use at school or work, and how much power a handful of AI firms end up holding over information and daily tools. It's also a live lesson in how financial hype works: a company can be worth more, on paper, than most countries' entire economies while still losing money every single day.
Read the whole story

OpenAI, the company that makes ChatGPT, is in early talks with investors about raising money at a valuation of more than $1.2 trillion, according to reporting from Bloomberg that was echoed by Fortune. A valuation is simply what investors believe a company is worth, calculated by what they're willing to pay for a slice of it. If this round happens at that price, OpenAI would become the single most valuable private company — meaning a company that doesn't sell shares on a public stock exchange — in history, beating every company that came before it.

To put $1.2 trillion in perspective: that's roughly the size of the entire economy of a mid-sized country, or more than the combined value of most of the world's airlines, car makers, and supermarket chains put together. And OpenAI would be reaching that number as a company that, by most reports, is still losing large amounts of money every year. It has millions of users and enormous revenue growth, but running ChatGPT and its other AI tools costs a huge amount — training the underlying models and running the computers that answer every query is extremely expensive, and OpenAI hasn't yet turned that activity into profit.

The talks are described as preliminary, which means no lead investor has been locked in, no closing date has been set, and the final number could shift. But the reporting suggests this round is part of a longer path toward an eventual initial public offering, or IPO — the process by which a private company sells shares to the general public for the first time, usually years after early investors have already piled in privately. Positioning for an IPO tends to mean a company wants to look as strong and as valuable as possible before outside investors, including ordinary savers and pension funds, get their turn to buy in.

Why would investors pay such a staggering price for a company that isn't yet profitable? The bet isn't on OpenAI's finances today — it's on a belief that AI will reshape huge parts of the economy, from how offices work to how software gets built, and that OpenAI, having built the product that made the whole AI boom visible to the public, will keep leading it. Skeptics counter that a lot of previous tech bubbles were inflated by exactly this kind of story, and that valuations detached from actual profit have a way of correcting hard and fast when investor mood changes.

This isn't happening in isolation. Money is pouring into AI companies across the board right now, and OpenAI's number is being treated by analysts as a kind of bellwether — an early signal — for whether that whole wave of investment is grounded in something real or is running on hype. If a $1.2 trillion price tag for a lossmaking company becomes normal, it says investors are extremely confident about AI's future. If the deal stalls, shrinks, or the valuation gets challenged publicly, it could cool enthusiasm across the sector, not just for OpenAI.

Words worth knowing

  • Private company — A company whose shares are not bought and sold on a public stock exchange, so only chosen investors can buy in.
  • IPO (Initial Public Offering) — The first time a company sells its shares to the general public on a stock exchange.
