Apple makes the iPhone, one of the most popular phones in the world. Every September, Apple shows off its newest phones at a big event. This year's event happened at Apple's headquarters in California, called Apple Park.

The star of the show was the iPhone Duo. Unlike every iPhone before it, this one folds in half, like a wallet. You can carry it small in your pocket, then open it up into a bigger screen, almost like a mini tablet, when you want to watch a video or read something.

Apple's product launches usually follow a predictable script: a new iPhone, slightly faster, slightly different camera, same basic shape it's had since 2017. This one broke that pattern. The iPhone Duo folds in half like a small book, turning a phone into something closer to a mini tablet when opened. It's Apple's answer to foldable phones that Samsung and other companies have sold for years — except Apple, notoriously, waits until it thinks a technology is ready before it touches it.

The price makes clear this isn't aimed at everyone. The Duo starts at $1,999 for the smallest storage option, roughly double what Apple charges for its standard flagship phone, the top-tier model most people think of as "the new iPhone." That's a deliberate signal: Apple is launching this as a premium, almost luxury product first, the way it once did with earlier innovations, rather than trying to replace the regular iPhone lineup overnight.

The event mattered for a second reason that has nothing to do with hinges or screens. It was the first time John Ternus stood on stage as Apple's chief executive, having taken over from Tim Cook, who had run the company for 14 years, stepping down earlier in September 2026. Ternus was previously in charge of hardware engineering, meaning he was already deeply involved in projects like the Duo before he became the person announcing them. How a new leader's first major event lands — confidently or shakily — tends to shape how investors, employees, and rivals read the transition.

One thing outlets could not yet confirm at the time of the launch: how well the folding mechanism actually holds up. Foldable phones from other companies have had real problems in the past — screens creasing, hinges wearing out, dust getting into the fold line. Independent teardown reviews, where technicians take a device apart to examine its build quality, and durability tests, which simulate months of folding and unfolding, take weeks to complete and hadn't been published yet. So while the price and specs are confirmed, whether the Duo is actually built to last is still an open question.

Coverage of the pricing, specifications, and leadership change lines up consistently across independent outlets, which is a reasonable sign the basic facts are solid. What's genuinely uncertain — durability, whether people will pay double for a folding screen, whether this changes what "a phone" is expected to do — will only become clear over the coming months, as real owners use these devices and reviewers finish their long-term testing.

Three Countries Say No to Buying Things Made in Certain Israeli Towns

On September 8, 2026, three countries — Britain, France, and Canada — announced they will stop buying goods made in certain Israeli towns built on land called the West Bank. They also said they will punish companies connected to building more of those towns. It's a sign that some of Israel's oldest friends are pulling away from it.

Why this mattersImagine three of your close friends suddenly saying they won't trade stickers with you anymore because they think you're doing something wrong — that's a bit like what's happening between these countries and Israel. It matters because when countries stop trading with each other, it can change how much money moves around and how people in charge feel pressure to act differently. It's also a reminder that grown-up countries can disagree deeply about what counts as fair, even when they've been friends for a long time.
Read the whole story

The West Bank is a piece of land where both Israelis and Palestinians live and both say it belongs to them. Since Israel took control of it many years ago, Israeli settlers have built towns there. Most countries in the world say building these towns breaks international rules — like building on land that isn't yours to build on. Israel disagrees and says it has a right to be there.

This week, more of those towns have been getting built, faster than before. Britain said it will not import — meaning buy and bring in — any goods made in these settlements. It will also punish companies connected to the building. France and Canada announced similar rules on the very same day.

Britain's foreign minister, a person in charge of how the country deals with other countries, used very strong words in front of lawmakers. He said settlers had committed 'ethnic cleansing' — a term for forcing a group of people out of an area because of who they are — against Palestinians. Israeli officials strongly reject this description and say it is unfair.

This isn't just angry words. Three powerful countries changing their trade rules over this is a real, lasting shift — not something that will just blow over.

  • sanctions — Punishments a country uses instead of fighting, like refusing to trade with or do business with another country or its companies.

UK, France and Canada Hit Israeli Settlements With Sanctions and Trade Bans

On September 8, 2026, Britain, France and Canada each announced sanctions and trade restrictions aimed at Jewish settlements in the occupied West Bank. Britain said it would stop importing goods made in the settlements and sanction companies linked to a rapid wave of new construction there. Announcing the move in Parliament, the UK's Foreign Secretary accused "settler terrorists" of carrying out "ethnic cleansing" against Palestinians — language Israel rejects outright.

Why this mattersThis is a story about how far governments will go to punish an ally's policies they consider illegal — a question that comes up whenever countries you're friendly with do something you think is wrong, whether it's trade, human rights, or war. It also shows how language itself can become part of the story: a word like "ethnic cleansing," spoken in Parliament, carries diplomatic weight far beyond what it describes, and that weight lands on ordinary trade, travel and business decisions for years afterward.
Read the whole story

The West Bank is a stretch of land east of Israel that Israel has controlled militarily since a war in 1967. Roughly three million Palestinians live there, alongside several hundred thousand Israeli settlers — Israeli civilians who have moved into communities built on that land, often with government support. Most of the world's governments, along with the United Nations and the International Court of Justice, consider these settlements illegal under international law, the body of rules and treaties countries have agreed govern how states may treat territory and people during conflict. Israel disputes this, arguing it has historical and security claims to the land that predate the 1967 war.

What changed on September 8 wasn't the legal argument — that's decades old — but the willingness of three G7 countries to back it with money and trade. Britain announced it would ban the import of goods physically made in West Bank settlements, meaning products like wine or produce grown there could no longer be sold in British shops. It also placed sanctions — legal penalties that freeze assets and block business dealings — on specific companies tied to what officials described as an accelerating construction boom in the settlements. France and Canada announced their own, separate measures the same day, coordinated in timing though not identical in detail.

The sharpest moment came in the language itself. Ed Miliband, the UK's Foreign Secretary — the minister responsible for Britain's relationships with other countries — told Parliament that "settler terrorists" had carried out "ethnic cleansing" against Palestinians in the West Bank. Ethnic cleansing is a specific and heavily loaded term, generally used to describe the forced removal of a population from an area because of who they are; it sits close to, but is legally distinct from, genocide. Israeli officials rejected the framing entirely, and it's the kind of accusation that, once made by a G7 foreign minister on the floor of Parliament, is very hard to walk back quietly.

What makes this significant isn't just the sanctions themselves — trade bans on settlement goods have existed in smaller forms before, from the EU and others — but who is doing it and how. The G7 is an informal club of seven of the world's largest economies (the US, UK, France, Germany, Italy, Japan and Canada) that meet regularly to coordinate on major world issues. Three of its members moving together against a country they've treated as a close ally for decades is a structural break, not just a war of words. It signals that patience in Western capitals for the pace and scale of settlement expansion has run out, at least among some governments, even as the United States — Israel's most important backer — has not joined them.

Israel's government has dismissed the sanctions as one-sided and accused Britain, France and Canada of rewarding violence rather than addressing it, pointing to attacks against Israeli civilians as the reason security measures in the West Bank exist at all. Supporters of the sanctions argue that settlement expansion has continued regardless of negotiations for peace, making any two-state solution — the long-standing idea of an independent Palestinian state existing alongside Israel — increasingly difficult to imagine on the ground. Both readings of the same set of facts are argued seriously by people who follow the conflict closely, which is part of why this dispute has lasted so long.

Words worth knowing
  • Settlements — Communities built by Israeli civilians on land in the occupied West Bank, considered illegal under international law by most countries but defended by Israel's government.
  • Sanctions — Legal penalties — like freezing money or banning business deals — that one government imposes on people, companies, or another country to pressure a change in behavior.
  • International law — The shared set of rules and treaties that countries have agreed should govern how states treat territory and people, especially during conflict or occupation.

Oil, War and Debt: Why the Stock Market Just Had Its Roughest Week Since Summer

US stock prices fell for two days straight in early September as fighting in the Middle East pushed oil prices up and a surprise government announcement sent a key interest rate to its highest level in three years. On Wednesday alone, the Dow Jones Industrial Average — one of the main scoreboards for the American stock market — dropped over 400 points. Behind the numbers is a chain reaction that reaches well past Wall Street, into the cost of mortgages, credit cards and the price of filling up a car.

Why this mattersNone of this stays confined to a trading screen: if borrowing costs keep rising, a family shopping for a mortgage or a business loan will likely pay more for it, and if oil keeps climbing, so will the price of gas and everyday goods that depend on shipping. It's also a useful reminder that a war on the other side of the world and a bureaucratic announcement about government debt can combine to shape what your family pays at the pump or the bank within the same week.
Read the whole story

On Tuesday, September 8, 2026, US stocks slipped as fighting in the ongoing conflict between the United States and Iran pushed oil prices higher, right at the start of a holiday-shortened trading week. By Wednesday the slide had turned into something sharper: the Dow Jones Industrial Average, an index that tracks 30 large American companies and is one of the most-watched measures of the stock market's health, dropped 405 points — a 0.77% fall — to close at 52,380.66.

The oil piece of the story is fairly direct. When fighting disrupts oil-producing regions or the routes tankers use to move crude around the world, buyers get nervous that supply will shrink, and prices rise even before anything actually stops flowing. Higher oil prices ripple outward fast: they raise the cost of gasoline, of shipping goods, and of manufacturing almost anything that uses plastic or fuel in its production. That makes oil one of the more reliable early warning signs of inflation, the general rise in prices that makes the same amount of money buy less than it used to.

The bond market piece is stranger, and worth slowing down for. On Wednesday, the US Treasury Department — the government agency that manages America's finances and borrows money by selling bonds, essentially IOUs that pay interest over time — announced it would triple a program to buy back some of its own longer-term debt. Normally, a government buying back its own bonds is meant to be a stabilising move: it takes some of that debt out of the market, and less supply usually means the price of what remains goes up, which pushes the yield — the effective interest rate investors earn — down. Instead, yields jumped, with the 10-year Treasury yield hitting its highest point since 2023. One reading is that tripling the buyback so abruptly made investors wonder whether the Treasury was quietly responding to strain it saw building in its own debt market — the kind of strain that would make lenders demand a higher return to keep lending.

Whatever the exact cause, rising yields matter well beyond the trading floor. The interest rate the US government pays to borrow acts as a kind of baseline for interest rates across the whole economy — banks use it as a reference point when setting rates on mortgages, credit cards, and loans to businesses. When that baseline climbs, borrowing gets more expensive for almost everyone, from a family buying a house to a company trying to expand a factory.

Put the two forces together and you get a week where stocks fell for reasons that don't sit neatly in one category: a war pushing up the cost of energy, and a debt-management decision pushing up the cost of borrowing, both landing in the same few days. Investors dislike uncertainty more than they dislike bad news on its own, and having two separate sources of pressure arrive together — one from geopolitics, one from fiscal policy — made the sell-off sharper than either might have caused alone.

Words worth knowing
  • Bond yield — The effective interest rate a government or company pays to borrow money by selling bonds, which tends to rise when investors see more risk or demand higher returns.
  • Buyback operation — When a government or company repurchases some of the debt or shares it previously sold, usually to influence prices or reduce the amount in circulation.
  • Inflation — The rate at which prices for everyday goods and services rise over time, meaning the same amount of money buys less than it used to.

  1. When a war abroad pushes oil prices up sharply at home, should the government step in to control fuel prices, or should the market be left to adjust on its own?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Government should intervene

    • Sudden price spikes hit low-income families hardest, since fuel and heating take up a bigger share of tight budgets.
    • Stepping in early with subsidies or caps can stop a short-term war shock from turning into a longer inflation spiral that's much harder to reverse.
    • Governments already shape markets in other ways, such as setting interest rates, so guiding fuel prices in a crisis is a difference of degree, not of principle.

    • Price caps often cause shortages, because sellers have less reason to supply fuel at a price that doesn't cover their costs.
    • Rising prices push people to drive less and use energy more carefully, which is exactly the adjustment a real shortage requires.
    • Once a government starts controlling prices, it's politically very hard to stop, and subsidies can become expensive and difficult to unwind.

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    It reflects real danger

    • Oil price shocks have historically come before recessions, so a fast market reaction can be an early, useful warning sign.
    • Rising bond yields raise real borrowing costs within weeks, not months, so the market reaction tracks a genuine and immediate change.
    • Investors and traders often have access to the same information as officials, and are pricing in supply and trade disruptions that haven't hit the news yet.

    • A lot of day-to-day market movement comes from automated trading and short-term sentiment rather than careful long-term analysis.
