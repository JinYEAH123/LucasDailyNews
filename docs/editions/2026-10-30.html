Google's Parent Company Just Had Its Worst Stock Market Day Ever

On Thursday, the value of Alphabet — the company that owns Google — dropped by 13% in a single day. That is the biggest one-day drop since the company first sold pieces of itself to the public more than 20 years ago. It happened right after Alphabet told investors it was spending far more money than expected on artificial intelligence, and getting less cash back than hoped.

Why this mattersLots of families — maybe even yours — have money saved for the future in accounts that include pieces of companies like Alphabet, Amazon, and Intel. When a giant company has a bad day like this, that saved money can shrink too, even for people who never bought Google stock directly. It also shows that even the richest, most successful companies can make expensive bets that do not work out right away.
Read the whole story

Companies like Alphabet are owned by millions of people through something called stock. You can buy a tiny slice of a company, and if the company does well, your slice becomes worth more. If it does badly, your slice shrinks.

On Thursday, Alphabet's slices shrank a lot. The company had been spending huge amounts of money building computers and data centers for artificial intelligence, hoping it would pay off. But its latest report showed it was burning through far more cash than expected, with little proof yet that the AI spending is earning money back.

Investors got nervous and started selling their Alphabet stock fast. In one day, the company lost about $500 billion in value. If you split that money evenly among every person in the United States, each person would get about $1,500.

Other big tech companies had a rough day too. Intel warned that fewer people want to buy its computer chips, and Amazon's earnings disappointed investors. Together, this dragged down the whole stock market, including retirement savings accounts held by regular people all over the world.

  • artificial intelligence (AI) — Artificial intelligence is computer software built to do things that normally need human thinking, like recognizing pictures or writing sentences.

Google's Parent Company Just Had Its Worst Day on the Stock Market Ever

On Thursday, shares in Alphabet, the company that owns Google, fell 13% in a single day. That one drop wiped out roughly $500 billion in value — more than the entire economy of countries like Poland or Belgium — and it happened because investors are suddenly worried that Alphabet is spending too much money chasing artificial intelligence without enough to show for it yet.

Why this mattersEven if you've never bought a stock, a day like this touches you indirectly — through a parent's retirement account, a pension fund, or a college savings plan that holds a slice of companies like Alphabet. It's also a preview of a bigger question hanging over the economy: whether the trillion-dollar bet on AI infrastructure turns into real, lasting value, or whether it's a bubble that investors are only now starting to question.
Read the whole story

Here is what triggered it. Every three months, big public companies report their earnings — basically a scorecard showing how much money came in, how much went out, and how the business is doing. Alphabet's report, released Wednesday, contained one number that spooked investors badly: free cash flow, which is the actual cash left over after a company pays for its operations and investments, came in at negative $9.07 billion. Analysts who study the company had expected it to be a positive $2.7 billion. That gap — nearly $12 billion off expectations — told investors that Alphabet is burning through cash far faster than anyone predicted.

Why is Alphabet burning cash? The short answer is artificial intelligence. Building the data centers, chips, and computing power needed to run AI systems like Google's Gemini costs an enormous amount of money, and Alphabet has been spending aggressively to keep pace with rivals like OpenAI and Microsoft. Spending big on AI isn't necessarily a mistake — companies often invest heavily before a payoff arrives — but investors had assumed Alphabet could do this while still generating strong cash flow. When that assumption broke, confidence broke with it.

The scale of the reaction was historic. A 13% single-day drop is the steepest one Alphabet has ever had, going back to its 2004 stock market debut, and it beat the previous worst day, which happened in March 2020 during the early panic of the COVID-19 pandemic. Alphabet didn't fall alone. The Nasdaq, an index that tracks mostly technology companies, had its worst day since June, partly because chipmaker Intel gave weak guidance about future demand and Amazon posted earnings that missed expectations too. When several giant tech companies stumble in the same week, it raises a broader question: is the AI spending boom that has driven the stock market for the past two years starting to look shakier than investors assumed?

This isn't just a story about rich investors losing money on paper. Alphabet is one of the most widely owned stocks on earth, not because everyone buys it directly, but because it sits inside index funds — big baskets of stocks that many retirement plans and pension funds automatically invest in. If you, a parent, or a grandparent has money in a retirement account, there's a decent chance a slice of it is tied to Alphabet's stock price, even without anyone choosing Google specifically. That's how a 13% drop in one company's share price ends up rippling through ordinary people's savings around the world.

Analysts are genuinely split on what this means. Some think it's a healthy correction — a reminder that stock prices had climbed too fast on AI excitement, and that a sharp pullback now is better than a bigger crash later. Others think it's an early warning sign that the AI spending race, which has pumped hundreds of billions of dollars into data centers and chips across the industry, might not generate profits as quickly as companies have been promising investors. Nobody will know which reading is correct for months or years — Alphabet's own executives insist the AI investment will pay off, and plenty of past technology booms looked wobbly before they eventually succeeded.

Words worth knowing
  • Free cash flow — The actual cash a company has left after paying for its day-to-day operations and its investments, a number investors watch to judge financial health.
  • Market capitalization — The total value of all of a company's shares added together, calculated by multiplying the share price by the number of shares that exist.
  • Index fund — An investment that holds a large basket of different companies' stocks at once, often used in retirement accounts because it spreads risk across many businesses.

China Removes 33 Top Officials — and Promotes Its Corruption-Hunter

China's ruling Communist Party just confirmed it threw out 33 of its most senior leaders, including generals and former ministers, accusing them of corruption. At the same big meeting, the general in charge of catching cheats inside the army was surprised with a promotion into the party's most powerful inner circle.

Why this mattersLots of toys, phones, and clothes you use are made in China, so what happens inside its government can ripple out to shops everywhere. It's a bit like if your school suddenly removed a third of its teachers overnight — you'd wonder why, and who's really in charge now. When one country this big has an uncertain plan for its next leader, the whole world pays attention.
Read the whole story

Picture a huge team running a country of 1.4 billion people — more people than live in all of North and South America put together. That team is called the Communist Party, and it runs China. This week, the party said it had removed 33 of its top members.

The officials were accused of corruption. That means using power unfairly — taking money or favors you're not supposed to have. Two of the people removed used to sit on the Politburo, a tiny group of about 24 leaders who make China's biggest decisions.

At the same meeting, something unexpected happened. General Zhang Shengmin, the man whose job is hunting corruption inside China's army, was promoted into that same powerful Politburo. Many experts think this shows China's leader, Xi Jinping, is tightening his grip on the army after kicking out several top generals.

More leaders stayed away from this meeting than any meeting since the 1970s. That's a big deal — it shows how many senior people have been swept up in this cleanup. And right now, there's no clear sign of who might lead China next, even though the next major party meeting is expected in 2027.

China Purges 33 Top Officials and Promotes Its Chief Military Enforcer

China's Communist Party has confirmed that 33 senior officials, including two former Politburo members, a former top diplomat, and a former government minister, have been expelled from the party. At the same meeting, the general in charge of rooting out corruption inside the military was promoted into the party's most powerful inner circle.

Why this mattersChina is one of the two countries, along with the United States, whose internal politics shape the rest of the world's economy, trade rules, and security decisions. Who controls the Chinese military and how power is concentrated at the top affects everything from how China handles tensions over Taiwan to how reliable its supply chains and business environment feel to the rest of the world — including the companies and products your family interacts with daily.
Read the whole story

China's ruling Communist Party held one of its periodic closed-door meetings of top leaders, called a plenary session, and afterward confirmed a sweeping set of expulsions: 33 senior officials stripped of their party membership, including former Politburo members Ma Xingrui and Zhang Youxia, former top diplomat Liu Jianchao, and former industry minister Jin Zhuanglong. The Politburo is the roughly two-dozen-person group that sits just below the very top of China's political system and effectively runs the country day to day. Losing a seat on it, let alone being expelled from the party entirely, is as close to political execution as the system allows.

The development that surprised outside observers most wasn't the expulsions themselves — purges have become almost routine under President Xi Jinping — but who got promoted instead. General Zhang Shengmin, the military's top anti-corruption enforcer, was elevated into the Politburo. His job for years has been investigating and removing corrupt officers inside the People's Liberation Army, China's armed forces. Putting the chief internal watchdog into the party's elite leadership group is widely read as Xi tightening his personal grip on the military, especially after several senior generals were purged in the past two years.

The meeting itself told its own story. Reports describe the highest rate of member absences from a Central Committee gathering recorded since Mao Zedong's death in 1976. The Central Committee is a body of a few hundred senior officials, one tier below the Politburo, that formally approves major party decisions. A high absence rate at this level usually means officials have been removed, are under investigation, or have simply vanished from public life — a blunt signal of how deep the anti-corruption campaign has cut into the ranks of people who were, until recently, considered untouchable.

What makes this reshuffle unusual is what it leaves unresolved: there is still no one clearly positioned to succeed Xi ahead of the next party congress, expected in 2027. In most political systems, a leader eventually grooms or at least tolerates a visible heir. Here, the pattern looks more like consolidation around one person rather than preparation for a handover — those being elevated are loyalists and enforcers, not obvious successors.

Much of the detailed reporting on these internal party moves comes from China-based or China-focused outlets, which have the best access to leadership politics but also face real constraints on what they can report and how. Independent verification from outside that circle remains limited, which is worth keeping in mind: the official confirmations are real, but the fuller picture of motives and internal rivalries is harder to check from outside.

Words worth knowing
  • Politburo — A group of roughly two dozen of the most powerful officials in China's Communist Party, who effectively run the country beneath the very top leadership.
  • Central Committee — A body of several hundred senior Chinese officials, one level below the Politburo, that formally approves the party's major decisions.
  • Anti-corruption campaign — A long-running effort by China's leadership to investigate and remove officials accused of bribery or abuse of power, which critics say is also used to remove political rivals.

Israel's Election Leaves No One Fully in Charge

Israel, a country in the Middle East, just held its first big election since a group called Hamas attacked it in October 2023 and started a war in Gaza. The results came out and Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's team won far fewer seats than before. Now no single group has enough seats to run the government by itself.

Why this mattersThis election could change how the war in Gaza ends, since many voters were unhappy with how Netanyahu handled it. It could also change how Israel gets along with countries like Iran and the United States. It's a bit like when your class picks new leaders — new people in charge can mean new rules and new decisions.
Read the whole story

Israel's government works a bit like a giant classroom vote. People choose representatives to sit in a parliament called the Knesset. This was the first time Israelis voted since the October 7, 2023 attack that led to the war in Gaza.

Imagine the Knesset as a room with 120 chairs. Benjamin Netanyahu's group used to sit in 68 of those chairs at its strongest. Now it only won 53.

The group that disagrees with Netanyahu won 48 chairs. Parties led by Arab citizens of Israel won the other 19. To be fully in charge, a group needs 61 chairs, so nobody has enough on their own. Leaders now have to team up to form a government.

Netanyahu's party said, without showing any proof, that the Arab-led parties had 'stolen' the election. Election officials and outside observers say there is no evidence this happened. Right now, nobody knows for sure if Netanyahu will stay in charge or if a different leader will take over.

Israel's First Wartime Election Leaves No One in Charge

Israel just held its first national election since the Hamas attack of October 7, 2023, and the results, released Thursday, gave no side a clear path to power. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's governing bloc dropped from 68 seats to 53, well short of the 61 needed to control parliament. His party is now publicly claiming, without evidence, that the vote was stolen.

Why this mattersThis isn't a faraway vote with no echo — Israel sits at the center of a war and a ceasefire that affect oil markets, US foreign policy, and the stability of an entire region, so who ends up governing it ripples outward. It's also a live example of something that shows up in democracies everywhere: what happens when a leader loses ground at the polls but refuses to accept that the public has turned.
Read the whole story

Israel's parliament, called the Knesset, has 120 seats, and a government needs the backing of at least 61 of them to function. In Thursday's results, Netanyahu's bloc of right-wing and religious parties won 53 seats, the main opposition bloc won 48, and Arab-led parties took the remaining 19. No single side cleared the 61-seat line, which means weeks of negotiation now lie ahead as parties try to stitch together a coalition — a working alliance of separate parties that agree to govern together, since Israel's voting system almost never hands one party a majority on its own.

This was the first time Israelis had voted since the October 7, 2023 attack, when Hamas gunmen crossed from Gaza and killed roughly 1,200 people, triggering the war that has defined Israeli politics for the two years since. Many analysts are reading Netanyahu's drop from 68 seats to 53 as a verdict on how he handled that war — including the security failures that let the attack happen, the slow and costly campaign in Gaza, and the fate of hostages still held there. A 15-seat swing sounds small in a 120-seat chamber, but it is the difference between comfortably running the country and having to beg smaller parties for support.

Rather than accept the result as a setback, Netanyahu's party has been pushing claims that Arab-led parties 'stole' the election. Independent election officials and outside observers have found no evidence to support this — the count was conducted through Israel's normal, audited process. Spreading unproven fraud claims after a loss is not unique to Israel; it is a tactic seen in elections around the world, and it tends to deepen public distrust in the vote itself even when officials insist the process was clean.

What happens next matters well beyond Israel's borders. A fragile ceasefire in Gaza, already strained, could tilt either toward holding or collapsing depending on who ends up running the government. Israel's approach to Iran, which backs several of its regional adversaries, and its relationship with the United States, its most important ally and arms supplier, could shift depending on whether Netanyahu survives or a different coalition takes shape. For now, the country has a parliament but no confirmed government, and the bargaining that decides who leads it is only beginning.

Words worth knowing
  • Knesset — Israel's national parliament, made up of 120 seats, where a group needs support from at least 61 members to form a government.
  • Coalition — A group of separate political parties that agree to govern together because no single party won enough seats to rule alone.
  • Bloc — A cluster of parties that usually vote the same way and are counted together when deciding who can form a coalition, even though each party keeps its own name and leader.
