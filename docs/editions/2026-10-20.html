Report: Putin Wants to Settle Ukraine War With Trump and Xi — Without Ukraine in the Room

A single unnamed source has told the Wall Street Journal that Russia's president proposed a three-way summit with the US and China to negotiate an end to the war in Ukraine — leaving Ukraine's own president out entirely. No government has confirmed this happened, so right now it's an unverified claim, not a fact. But the idea itself, that the war's outcome could be settled by three other countries, has alarmed Ukraine and its European allies.

Why this mattersWhen wars end, the people who get a seat at the negotiating table usually get more say in the outcome — and the people left out often live with terms they didn't choose. This story is a reminder that in international conflicts, being physically present at a negotiation can matter as much as being right or being the victim, which is a pattern that shows up far beyond this one war.

Imagine two neighbours in a years-long property dispute, and then a third neighbour proposes that the matter get settled over coffee — between himself, one of the disputing neighbours, and a powerful mutual friend, with the other disputing neighbour not invited. That's roughly the shape of what the Wall Street Journal reported: Russian President Vladimir Putin allegedly suggested, in an October 9 phone call, that he, US President Donald Trump, and Chinese President Xi Jinping meet to hash out terms for ending the war in Ukraine — without Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy present.

The catch is that this entire story rests on one unnamed source, described only as a former Russian intelligence official. That's a thin foundation. No government — not Russia's, not America's, not China's — has confirmed a three-way summit was proposed or that this call took place as described. US Secretary of State Marco Rubio, when asked, said only that 'there's been talks of a meeting,' which is vague enough to mean almost anything. Until more sources or an official confirmation emerge, this should be treated as an unverified report rather than established fact — something that might be true, might be exaggerated, or might be entirely wrong.

Still, the report matters even as a possibility, because of what it would mean if true. One floated location is the APEC summit (a yearly meeting of leaders from Pacific-rim economies, including the US and China) scheduled for November in Shenzhen, China. If Putin, Trump, and Xi did meet there to discuss ending the war, it would represent a stunning shift in how the conflict is handled: a war being fought on Ukrainian soil, killing and displacing millions of Ukrainians, would have its terms discussed by three powers — two of which aren't directly fighting it — while the country actually under invasion watches from outside the room.

That's precisely why Ukraine and European governments have reacted with alarm to the report. Their fear isn't just about etiquette; it's about leverage. Wars are usually ended through negotiation, and whoever sits at the table shapes the outcome — what land changes hands, what security guarantees are given, what sanctions lift or stay. A country excluded from those talks has far less power to protect its own interests, even if the deal is technically about its territory and its people.

There's also a geopolitical logic some analysts point to: pairing the US and China together as dealmakers on Ukraine would be unusual, since the two countries are frequently rivals on trade, technology, and military posture. If Putin genuinely proposed this, one reading is that he's trying to position Russia as dealing with the world's two biggest powers as equals, sidelining both Ukraine and the European allies who've backed it financially and militarily throughout the war. Another reading is that this is simply a trial balloon — an idea floated by one anonymous source, possibly exaggerated or invented, to test reactions or sow division among Western allies before anything concrete is agreed.

For now, what's solid is this: a war that has lasted since Russia's full invasion in February 2022 might be approaching a new phase of diplomacy, and the shape of that diplomacy — who gets a seat, who doesn't — is itself becoming a point of serious contention before any actual negotiation has been confirmed to exist.

  • APEC summit — An annual meeting of leaders from countries around the Pacific Ocean, including the US and China, held to discuss trade and economic cooperation.

  1. Should a country at war ever be left out of talks that decide how that war ends?

China's Economy Grows Faster Than Expected — But Not Everywhere

China, a huge country in Asia with more than a billion people, just announced how much its economy grew over the summer. The number was better than expected — but dig a little deeper, and you find some parts booming while others are struggling badly.

Why this mattersA lot of the toys, phones, and gadgets in your house were probably made in China — so when its factories are busy exporting, that connects directly to shelves at your local store. But if regular families in China aren't spending much and fewer houses are being built, that can be a warning sign about how healthy the economy really is underneath the surface, which can eventually affect prices and jobs everywhere, including where you live.
Read the whole story

China's government said its economy grew 4.5% between July and September, compared to the same months last year. That's a bit faster than the 4.3% growth from the spring — and faster than most experts had guessed.

Here's what's driving it: China is selling huge amounts of tech goods to other countries, things like phones, computers, and electronics. Think of it like a kid who makes money mowing neighbors' lawns rather than spending their own allowance — China is earning from outside, not from people inside the country shopping more.

Inside China, things look shakier. Shopping at home barely grew at all — just 1.1%, like getting one extra dollar when you expected five. And house-building? That dropped more than 20% compared to last year. Imagine a neighborhood where one in five construction sites just... stopped.

So China's report card has a strange mix: an A in selling stuff abroad, but a much weaker grade in building houses and getting its own people to spend money. Some experts even say China's official numbers might paint a rosier picture than what's really happening underneath.

China's Economy Grew Faster Than Expected — But Not Because Shoppers Were Buying

China's economy grew 4.5% in the third quarter of this year compared to the same period last year, beating economists' predictions. But the growth came almost entirely from selling tech products abroad — not from Chinese people spending money at home, where shopping barely budged and the housing market kept sinking.

Why this mattersChina makes a huge share of the phones, laptops, toys, and components people around the world buy, so how its economy performs shapes prices and product availability well beyond its borders. It also matters because an economy leaning this heavily on exports, rather than on its own consumers feeling confident enough to spend, is a warning sign that economists watch closely when judging how stable a country's growth really is.
Read the whole story

China's National Bureau of Statistics announced that the country's gross domestic product — the total value of everything produced in the economy — grew 4.5% year-on-year in the third quarter. That's an improvement from the 4.3% growth recorded in the second quarter, which had been the slowest pace in three years, and it beat the 4.4% figure economists had forecast.

On the surface, that sounds like good news: an economy accelerating, beating expectations. But the number hides a lopsided story. The growth was driven largely by exports of technology goods — Chinese factories selling chips, electronics, and other tech products to buyers overseas. Meanwhile, the signs of domestic health were weak. Retail sales, a measure of how much people are spending in shops and online, rose just 1.1%, barely keeping pace with inflation. Industrial output, which measures how much factories are producing, rose a more respectable 5.5%, but that's still modest for an economy this size.

The real warning sign is real estate. Investment in property — building new apartments, offices, and other developments — fell 20.6% compared to a year earlier. For context, real estate and construction have historically been one of the biggest engines of Chinese growth, employing millions of workers and absorbing huge amounts of household savings, since many Chinese families hold most of their wealth in property rather than stocks. A fifth less investment in that sector is a significant drag, and it's been dragging for years now, ever since a wave of debt problems hit major property developers.

So the picture is of an economy held up by one leg while the others wobble. Export strength, especially in high-tech manufacturing, is masking weakness in the two areas that normally signal a healthy, self-sustaining economy: people spending their own money, and a stable property market. An economy that depends heavily on selling to other countries is vulnerable if those countries buy less — through tariffs, a slowdown of their own, or shifting their purchases elsewhere.

It's worth adding a caveat economists often raise: China's official GDP figures come from a government statistical agency, and some independent analysts have long argued that official numbers can diverge from what's actually happening on the ground, tending to smooth out volatility or align with political targets. That doesn't mean the figures are fabricated, but it means outside observers often treat them as one data point among several, rather than the final word.

Words worth knowing
  • GDP (Gross Domestic Product) — The total value of all goods and services a country produces in a given period, used as the main scorecard for how an economy is doing.
  • Retail sales — A measure of how much money people are spending on goods in stores and online, used as a signal of consumer confidence and spending power.
  • Real estate investment — Money spent on building and developing property, such as new homes and offices, which in China has historically been a major driver of overall economic growth.

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

A Rocket Just Launched to Grab a Piece of Mars's Moon

Japan sent a spacecraft flying toward Mars this week. It isn't going to land on Mars itself. It's aiming for one of Mars's two tiny moons, to scoop up dirt and bring it all the way back to Earth.

Why this mattersNobody has ever brought back a piece of a Martian moon before, so whatever MMX finds will be completely new information. Figuring out how Phobos formed helps scientists understand how all rocky planets, including the one you're standing on right now, came together billions of years ago.
Read the whole story

Japan's space agency, called JAXA, launched a spacecraft named MMX on a huge rocket from a space center on a Japanese island.

The spacecraft is heading toward Phobos, one of Mars's two small moons. The other moon is called Deimos. Phobos is tiny and lumpy, only about as wide as a mid-sized city, nothing like our round, smooth Moon.

Once it arrives, MMX will try to land on Phobos and scoop up at least 10 grams of rock and dust, about as much as two teaspoons of sugar. Then it has to fly all the way back to Earth, a round trip expected to take until 2031.

Scientists from the United States, Europe, France, and Germany all built instruments for the spacecraft. They're trying to answer a mystery: was Phobos once part of Mars, blasted off long ago by a giant crash, or was it a wandering space rock that got pulled in and trapped by Mars's gravity?

Japan Launches Spacecraft to Steal a Piece of One of Mars's Moons

Japan's space agency, JAXA, launched a spacecraft on a rocket bound for Phobos, one of Mars's two small moons. The plan is to land on it, scoop up at least 10 grams of rock and dust — about two teaspoons — and fly it back to Earth. If it works, it will be the first time anyone has ever landed on and returned a sample from a moon other than our own, and the whole trip won't finish until 2031.

Why this mattersThe rocky planets in our solar system, Earth included, were all shaped by collisions and gravitational capture billions of years ago, and we still don't fully understand how that process worked. A clear answer about Phobos's origin would fill in a real piece of that story — not just a curiosity about one small moon, but evidence about the violent, chaotic process that built the planet you're standing on.
Read the whole story

The spacecraft, called MMX (Martian Moons eXploration), lifted off aboard an H3 rocket from Japan's Tanegashima Space Center. Its destination is the Mars system — not the planet itself, but its two moons, Phobos and Deimos, both of which are oddly small and lumpy, more like asteroids than proper moons. MMX will spend time studying both from orbit, then attempt something much harder: landing on Phobos, grabbing surface material, and launching back off it to begin the long journey home.

The reason scientists care so much about Phobos comes down to a question nobody has been able to settle just by looking at it through telescopes: where did it come from? One idea is that Phobos and Deimos are asteroids that wandered too close to Mars and got trapped by its gravity, the way a marble can get caught circling the rim of a bowl. The other idea is that they're debris — chunks of Mars itself, blasted into orbit by a massive impact billions of years ago, similar to how scientists think Earth's own Moon formed when something the size of Mars slammed into young Earth. These two stories would mean the moons are made of completely different stuff, and only by holding real material in a lab on Earth — not just photographing it from a distance — can scientists actually tell which one is true.

Landing on Phobos is a strange engineering problem precisely because the moon is so small. It's only about 22 kilometres across, roughly the length of a decent city, which means its gravity is extremely weak — an astronaut standing on it could probably jump clear off into space. MMX has to approach slowly, settle onto the surface without bouncing away, fire a mechanism to collect loose material, and then push itself back off using very little force, since too much thrust would simply send it flying past the moon instead of away from it cleanly.

This isn't a solo Japanese effort. NASA, Europe's space agency ESA, and the French and German space agencies (CNES and DLR) all contributed scientific instruments riding on MMX — cameras, spectrometers that analyze what light bouncing off a surface reveals about its chemistry, and in one case a small rover built by the French and German teams that will actually drive around on Phobos before the sample is collected. Pooling instruments this way lets each country specialize rather than every space agency trying to build everything itself.

Sample-return missions are considered the gold standard of space science because instruments on a distant spacecraft can only measure so much. A rock in a laboratory on Earth can be sliced open, studied under far more powerful microscopes than anything that could survive a rocket launch, and re-examined years or decades later as new techniques are invented — which is exactly what's still happening with Moon rocks brought back by Apollo astronauts more than fifty years ago.

MMX won't actually reach the Mars system for a couple of years, and the full round trip — travel out, months of study, the landing and sample grab, and the long cruise back — isn't expected to wrap up until 2031. That's a long time for a mission to stay on schedule and in working order, and plenty of past sample-return attempts by various countries have run into trouble along the way, from equipment failures to landings that didn't go as planned.

Words worth knowing
  • Sample-return mission — A space mission designed to physically bring material back to Earth, rather than just studying it remotely with cameras and sensors.
  • Captured asteroid — An asteroid that gets pulled into orbit around a planet by its gravity, becoming a moon without ever having been part of that planet.
  • Giant-impact theory — The idea that a moon formed from debris thrown into orbit after a massive collision with its planet, the same process many scientists think created Earth's Moon.
