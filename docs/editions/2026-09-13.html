The Man Building Powerful AI Says: Maybe We Should Slow Down

Dario Amodei runs Anthropic, one of the companies racing to build the most advanced artificial intelligence systems on Earth. On Saturday, he published an essay warning that his own industry has been lying to itself about how dangerous that technology could be. What's stranger still: his fiercest rivals, including OpenAI's Sam Altman, quickly agreed with him.

Why this mattersThe AI systems these companies build already influence what you see online, how schools think about homework, and which jobs will exist in ten years — decisions currently being made by a handful of executives with no binding rules forcing caution. Whether this becomes a real turning point or just a headline depends on something you can actually watch for: whether any government passes actual laws, or whether it's just words.
Read the whole story

Imagine the person selling you a car suddenly holding a press conference to say the brakes might fail. That's roughly what happened when Dario Amodei, CEO of Anthropic — a company valued in the tens of billions of dollars for building 'frontier' AI models, meaning the most advanced systems that exist at any given moment — published an essay arguing the entire AI industry has spent years downplaying just how risky its own product could become. "I won't lie to you," he wrote, "there are real dangers." He named four specifically: AI systems slipping outside human control entirely, AI being used to design cyberattacks or biological weapons, and AI causing economic upheaval on a scale society isn't prepared for.

What makes this newsworthy isn't just that Amodei said it — insiders have warned about AI risk before — it's who agreed with him. Sam Altman, CEO of OpenAI and Amodei's most direct competitor, publicly backed the call to slow down the pace of frontier development. These two men run companies locked in a race worth hundreds of billions of dollars, competing for the same investors, the same engineers, and the same claim to building the future. They rarely agree on anything in public. Elon Musk, who runs his own AI company and has feuded with both of them, was also reported echoing similar worries. For three rivals who normally spend their time undercutting each other, this counts as a genuinely unusual moment.

But here's the catch: nobody promised anything. No company announced it would actually pause a product launch, delay a model release, or accept new rules limiting what it can build. What happened was a set of warnings — public statements about risk, not binding commitments. That gap matters enormously. It's the difference between three rival factory owners saying "pollution is bad" and any one of them actually shutting down a profitable production line while their competitors keep running.

This is exactly why some observers are skeptical the moment means much. Each of these companies is under enormous pressure to keep releasing bigger, more capable models faster than its rivals — that's how they attract the investment and talent needed to survive. If Anthropic actually slowed down while OpenAI kept racing ahead, Anthropic would likely lose ground, funding, and relevance. Warning about danger costs nothing; actually stepping back from a race worth your company's survival costs everything. Critics also note that dramatic warnings about how powerful and dangerous your own technology is can double as marketing — a way of saying "our product is so advanced it's genuinely scary," which is also a sales pitch.

Others take the warnings more seriously, pointing out that the people making them are the ones with the most detailed, inside knowledge of what these systems can actually do — information the public and regulators mostly lack. Amodei has made this kind of argument before and has, at times, backed it with actual company policies on safety testing. The fact that lifelong rivals are converging on the same public message, even without matching actions, could be read as an early signal that concern is spreading faster than the technology itself — and that governments, which have so far written very little binding law about advanced AI, might be the actual missing piece needed to make any slowdown real.

Words worth knowing
  • Frontier model — The most advanced AI system a company has built at a given time — the current cutting edge, not an older or smaller version.
  • AI-enabled cyberattack — A hacking attack made faster, cheaper, or more effective because an AI system helped design or carry it out.
  • Loss of control — A scenario where an AI system's actions or decisions can no longer be reliably predicted, corrected, or stopped by the humans who built it.

  1. When a company's own CEO warns that its product is dangerous, should the public trust the warning or suspect it's a marketing move?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Trust it

    • The CEO has far more detailed knowledge of the technology's real capabilities than any outsider, journalist, or regulator.
    • Warning about your own product's danger carries real reputational risk if it turns out to be false or overblown, so saying it anyway suggests genuine concern.
    • Multiple rival CEOs converging on the same warning independently makes coordinated marketing spin a less likely explanation.

    Be skeptical

    • A dramatic warning that your technology could reshape or endanger the world is also a powerful way to make it sound impressive to investors and buyers.
    • Warnings cost nothing if they don't come with any actual change in the company's own behavior or product timeline.
    • Executives have a long history of raising alarms about their industry while continuing to expand it exactly as before.

  2. If AI companies genuinely believe their technology poses serious risks, should they be free to keep competing at full speed, or should governments force them to slow down together?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Let companies compete, don't force a slowdown

    • If one country's companies slow down while another's don't, the slower ones simply lose the technology's benefits and influence to competitors abroad, especially in China.
    • Companies understand their own systems better than lawmakers do, and heavy-handed rules risk being clumsy, outdated, or wrong about where the actual danger lies.
    • Rapid development also produces real, immediate benefits — in medicine, education, and productivity — that a mandated slowdown would delay for everyone.

    Governments should force a coordinated slowdown

    • Individual companies have every incentive to keep racing even if they privately believe the risks are serious, because pausing alone just means losing to a rival who doesn't.
    • Voluntary promises without enforcement have historically failed to change corporate behavior once profit and competition are on the line.
    • Some risks — like AI-assisted bioweapons design or systems acting outside human control — could be irreversible, which is exactly the kind of danger societies usually decide is too important to leave to competitive markets alone.

Leaders From Five Powerful Countries Meet in India

This week, the leaders of some of the world's biggest countries gathered in New Delhi, the capital of India. China's leader Xi Jinping and Russia's leader Vladimir Putin both showed up in person, standing next to India's leader Narendra Modi. They came together to talk about trade, money, and the wars happening around the world.

Why this mattersWhen powerful countries decide whether to work together or stay apart, it can affect prices, jobs, and even whether wars get worse or better — things that reach into ordinary homes everywhere, including yours. It's a bit like when kids in a class decide to team up or split into separate groups: the decision can change who gets picked for things and how fights get solved, even for kids who weren't in the room.
Read the whole story

Five years ago, a group of countries called BRICS started meeting to talk about working together. The name comes from the first countries who joined: Brazil, Russia, India, China, and South Africa. More countries have joined since then, and together these nations have more people living in them than any other group on Earth.

This meeting felt special. Russia has been mostly avoided by many world leaders since it invaded Ukraine. China has been in a long argument with the United States about trade and taxes on goods called tariffs. Seeing Xi, Putin, and Modi stand together sent a message: these countries still want to talk to each other, even when the United States and Europe keep their distance from some of them.

But talking is not the same as agreeing. By the end of the meeting, the leaders had not signed any big new deal or promised anything specific. They mostly talked about wanting fairer trade rules and a say in world problems, like the war involving Iran.

People who study world politics disagree about what this all means. Some think BRICS is slowly becoming a real alternative to the groups that Western countries like the US usually lead. Others think it was mostly a photo opportunity — leaders standing together to look strong, without doing much that actually changes anything yet.

Words worth knowing
  • BRICS — BRICS is a group of large countries — including Brazil, Russia, India, China, and South Africa — that meet to talk about working together on trade and world problems.
  • Sanctions — Sanctions are punishments one country puts on another, like refusing to trade with them or blocking their access to money, usually because of something that country did.

Modi, Xi and Putin Share a Stage in New Delhi as BRICS Tests Its Unity

India's Prime Minister Narendra Modi welcomed the leaders of China, Russia, and other major developing economies to New Delhi for a summit of the BRICS group. The most striking image was Chinese President Xi Jinping and Russian President Vladimir Putin appearing together in person, at a moment when both countries face heavy pressure from the United States over trade and sanctions. No major deals were signed, and analysts are still arguing about whether the meeting changed anything real.

Why this mattersIf BRICS countries do build real alternatives to dollar-based trade or Western financial systems, it could eventually change how goods you buy get priced and how easily countries can be punished with sanctions — including the ones your own country trades with. Even short of that, a more visibly united China-Russia-India bloc changes the calculations for U.S. foreign policy, and those calculations shape trade rules, tariffs, and alliances that reach into ordinary economic life.
Read the whole story

BRICS is a loose club of major developing economies — it started with Brazil, Russia, India, China and South Africa, and has since added more countries. It has no headquarters, no army, and no single rulebook the way the European Union does. What it has is size: together, its members hold a huge share of the world's population and, increasingly, its economic output. For years, BRICS meetings were mostly polite photo opportunities. This one was watched more closely, because the countries involved are under real pressure from the same source at the same time.

That pressure comes from Washington. The U.S. has raised tariffs — taxes on imported goods — that hit Chinese exports particularly hard, squeezing companies that depend on selling to American consumers. Russia has been cut off from much of the global financial system through sanctions since its invasion of Ukraine, meaning it can no longer easily trade, borrow, or move money through banks that most of the world relies on. India, meanwhile, has its own tense trade relationship with Washington. All three countries have reasons to want other options besides doing business on America's terms.

That's what made Putin and Xi standing next to each other in New Delhi more than a photograph. Putin has been largely frozen out of Western capitals since 2022; showing up somewhere he's welcomed, next to the leader of the world's second-largest economy, is itself a message — that isolation has limits. Xi's presence carried its own signal: even as the U.S. and China argue over tariffs and technology, China is also investing in relationships that don't run through Washington at all.

But image is not the same as agreement. Reporters covering the summit noted that no binding deals came out of it — no new trade pact, no joint currency, no shared response to the Iran war that's also unsettling global markets. India, in particular, has its own reasons to be cautious about drawing too close to China, given a long-running border dispute between the two countries and India's separate, closer ties to the U.S. and its allies. A summit can produce warm statements about cooperation while the countries in the room still compete, and sometimes distrust each other, on almost everything else.

This is the central disagreement among people who study these things. Some see BRICS gradually building the scaffolding of an alternative to the Western-led economic order — its own trade arrangements, its own way of settling payments without U.S. dollars, its own diplomatic voice on wars the West is involved in. Others see a group whose members have too little in common — different governments, different economies, sometimes open rivalries — to act as one for anything beyond a group photo and a shared complaint about the United States.

Words worth knowing
  • BRICS — A group of major developing economies — originally Brazil, Russia, India, China and South Africa — that meets to coordinate on trade and diplomacy, though it has no formal headquarters or shared rulebook.
  • Sanctions — Penalties, often financial, that one country or group of countries imposes on another to restrict its trade, banking, or access to the global economy, usually as punishment or pressure over its actions.
  • Tariffs — Extra taxes a government charges on goods imported from another country, making those goods more expensive and often used as a tool of pressure in trade disputes.

Trump Asks Ukraine to Stop Bombing Russia's Fuel Factories

On Sunday, President Trump said he had told Ukraine's leader to stop hitting Russian oil refineries with drones. He said the attacks were pushing up the price of diesel fuel all over the world. Ukraine has been using flying robot planes to strike deep inside Russia, and those strikes have been hurting Russia's ability to make fuel.

Why this mattersDiesel fuel powers the trucks that bring food to your grocery store and the machines that farmers use to grow crops. When diesel gets more expensive, lots of everyday things can cost more too, even for families who live far from any war. This story shows how fighting in one part of the world can still reach into prices back home.
Read the whole story

Ukraine and Russia have been fighting a war for years. Ukraine has been sending drones — planes that fly with no pilot inside — deep into Russia. Many of these drones have hit oil refineries, which are giant factories that turn crude oil into fuel like diesel.

These strikes have knocked out a big chunk of Russia's fuel-making power. That has helped push diesel prices up around the whole world. Diesel is the fuel used by trucks, buses, tractors, and cargo ships.

President Trump talked about this on Sunday, at a golf tournament in Ireland. He said Ukraine's president, Volodymyr Zelenskyy, needs to stop this particular kind of attack, because it was making fuel too expensive for everyone, everywhere.

At the same time, Russia keeps attacking Ukraine too. Russian strikes have hit cargo ships, steel factories, and other buildings. Some people were killed, and more than 20 were hurt in recent attacks — about as many kids as in a full school class.

Trump Tells Ukraine to Stop Bombing Russian Oil Refineries — Because It's Making Diesel More Expensive

President Trump said Sunday that he had personally warned Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy to halt drone strikes on Russian oil refineries, blaming the attacks for pushing global diesel prices to record highs. It is a striking moment: a U.S. president publicly pressuring a wartime ally to stand down from a tactic that has been hurting the economy of the country invading it. The remarks came even as Russian strikes on Ukraine continued, reportedly killing several civilians and wounding more than twenty in a single night.

Why this mattersDiesel prices touch almost everything — the cost of shipping, farming, and trucking — so a war fought partly through oil refineries can raise prices you pay even if you live nowhere near the fighting. This story is also a small window into a bigger tension: how far a country's ally can push it to change its own military strategy when that strategy has side effects the ally doesn't like.
Read the whole story

Since the start of the war, Ukraine has increasingly targeted Russia's oil refineries — the industrial plants that turn crude oil into usable fuels like diesel and gasoline — with long-range drones. The logic is straightforward: Russia funds its war largely through oil exports, and refineries are a chokepoint. Knock enough of them offline, and you don't just dent Russia's finances, you also strain the fuel supply that keeps its military trucks, tanks, and equipment running.

The strategy has worked well enough to cause real damage. Russian diesel refining capacity has dropped sharply because of the strikes, and that has had a side effect nobody in Kyiv particularly wanted headlines about: diesel prices have climbed worldwide, hitting record levels in some markets. Diesel isn't just what goes in trucks — it powers shipping, farm equipment, and much of the machinery that moves goods around the global economy, so a shortage anywhere tends to be felt everywhere.

That global price effect is what Trump seized on. Speaking on the sidelines of a golf tournament in Ireland, he said Zelenskyy 'has to do one thing: He has to stop knocking out diesel fuel in Russia.' It's a notable reversal of the usual framing — rather than praising an ally's strike campaign against an invader's war economy (the industries and exports that keep a country's military funded), Trump is asking that ally to pull back, effectively prioritizing global fuel prices over one of Ukraine's few tools for damaging Russia from a distance.

Critics were quick to point out the awkwardness of the position: it asks the country being invaded to absorb more strategic risk so that fuel stays cheaper for drivers and shippers elsewhere in the world, including in the United States. Supporters of Trump's comment counter that a war fought partly through global commodity markets has costs too — costs paid by ordinary people far from the front line who never signed up for higher prices at the pump.

Meanwhile, the war itself showed no sign of slowing on the ground. Russian strikes over the same period hit Ukrainian cargo ships, a steel plant, and other industrial sites, with reports — the exact numbers vary by outlet and reporting cutoff — of several civilians killed and more than twenty wounded. The contrast is stark: while diplomatic attention focused on a fuel-price dispute, the physical war of missiles and drones on both sides continued without pause.

Words worth knowing
  • oil refinery — A large industrial plant that processes crude oil into usable fuels like diesel and gasoline.
  • war economy — The industries, exports, and financial systems a country relies on to fund and supply its military during a war.
  • strike campaign — A sustained series of military attacks aimed at a particular type of target, rather than a single one-off strike.
