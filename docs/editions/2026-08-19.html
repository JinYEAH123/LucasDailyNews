Back in the spring, the US and Iran signed a memorandum of understanding — a kind of informal agreement, weaker than a treaty, meant to buy time while the two sides figured out a longer-term deal. It was set to last 60 days. That window closed on August 18-19, and nothing replaced it. Instead of new talks, the world got new threats.

President Trump posted an image marking the Strait of Hormuz — a narrow strip of water between Iran and Oman that ships use to move oil out of the Persian Gulf — as "New US Territory." There is no legal basis for that claim; most people who study international law see it as a message, not a real territorial move. Trump also said he would bomb Oman, a country that has stayed neutral in the conflict, if it "gets in the way" of reopening the strait, and promised the "most crushing economic operation ever" against Iran through new, harsher sanctions — penalties that cut a country off from trade, banking, or other resources to pressure it into changing behavior.

The UAE, which is Iran's second-biggest trading partner, got pulled directly into the crisis. It said its air defenses intercepted two ballistic missiles fired from Iran, with one landing in its own coastal waters. In response, the UAE suspended all trade with Iran — a major economic and political statement from a country that had tried to stay out of the fighting.

Here's why the Strait of Hormuz matters so much: about one-fifth of the world's oil shipped by sea passes through it. That is like one out of every five trucks delivering gasoline to every gas station on Earth going through a single, narrow road. If that road gets blocked or fought over, oil prices can spike everywhere, not just in the countries at war.

There are also reports of a naval blockade connected to the conflict — warships stopping other ships from entering or leaving — that has reportedly intercepted 85 vessels and seized 3, costing Iran an estimated $500 million a day. That number comes from a secondary source and hasn't been independently confirmed, which is common in fast-moving conflicts where accurate numbers are hard to verify.

Six months in, neither side seems close to backing down. Trump's rhetoric — the territorial claim, the threat against Oman, the promise of even harsher sanctions — suggests the US is trying to raise the pressure rather than restart talks. Whether that pressure brings Iran back to the table or pushes the conflict further into new countries and new waters is the open question hanging over the region right now.

The agreement that lapsed was a memorandum of understanding — a written arrangement short of a formal treaty, often used when two governments want to signal intent without binding themselves legally. It had held for 60 days, apparently keeping a lid on direct US-Iran confrontation even as fighting between Israel and Iran continued elsewhere. When it expired on August 18-19 with no replacement, Trump said flatly that no talks were underway, then escalated in a way few expected: a social media post claiming the Strait of Hormuz, a 33-kilometer channel between Iran and Oman that virtually all Gulf oil exports must pass through, as American territory.

There is no legal basis for that claim. The strait is an international waterway; ownership isn't in dispute among lawyers or diplomats, only in the president's rhetoric. Most analysts read the post as a pressure tactic rather than a policy proposal — a way of signaling that the US considers free passage through the strait a red line it is willing to enforce by force, without actually asserting sovereignty anyone expects to be honored. Trump paired the claim with a direct threat to bomb Oman, a US partner that has historically played mediator in the region, if it 'gets in the way' of reopening shipping lanes.

The economic pressure track escalated alongside the military rhetoric. Trump promised what he called the 'most crushing economic operation ever' and new 'draconian' sanctions — economic penalties, like freezing assets or blocking trade, designed to cut off a country's access to money and goods until it changes behavior. Separately, a naval blockade connected to the wider conflict has reportedly intercepted 85 vessels and seized three ships attempting to move oil or goods linked to Iran, at an estimated cost to Iran of $500 million a day. That figure comes from a secondary summary of the situation rather than an independently verified source, so it should be treated as a claim under the fog of an active conflict, not a confirmed number.

The UAE's response signals how seriously regional governments are taking the danger of spillover. The UAE is Iran's second-largest trading partner, meaning the two economies are deeply intertwined despite decades of political friction. It reported intercepting two ballistic missiles fired from Iranian territory, with one landing in its own territorial waters, and responded by suspending all trade with Tehran — a rare and costly step for a country that has generally tried to keep commercial channels open even during periods of tension.

The reason any of this matters beyond the region is simple geography. Roughly one-fifth of the oil the world consumes each day physically has to pass through the Strait of Hormuz on tankers. There is no easy detour; pipelines exist as partial alternatives but can't handle anywhere close to that volume. A serious, sustained disruption there — whether from military action, mines, insurance companies refusing to cover ships, or Iran attempting to close the strait outright — would ripple immediately into fuel prices worldwide, the kind of shock that shows up at gas pumps and in shipping costs on everyday goods within weeks.

Earlier, smaller trials of this combination had already shown promise, so a positive update was not a total surprise to people following the field closely.

Here is the uncomfortable part: the news that moved billions of dollars in stock value traveled through markets before it traveled through the normal channels science uses to check itself. A personalized cancer vaccine that meaningfully cuts recurrence would be a genuine medical advance, not just a financial one — it could change how high-risk melanoma is treated after surgery.     • Highly personalized treatments are hard to scale, meaning most patients worldwide will never realistically benefit from them.
    • Health systems have limited budgets, and prioritizing expensive novel treatments can crowd out funding for prevention and basic care that helps far more people.
    • 把资源投入到只有少数富裕地区患者能获得的治疗上,或许不如将其分散用于更便宜、已被证实有效的疗法上更有益。
    • 高度个性化的治疗难以规模化,这意味着全球大多数患者实际上永远无法从中受益。
    • 医疗系统预算有限,优先资助昂贵的新型疗法可能会挤占本可惠及更多人的预防和基础医疗的资金。
