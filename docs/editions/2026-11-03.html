America Votes: Millions of Ballots, One Big Court Fight

On Tuesday, November 3, people across the United States vote for hundreds of leaders at once — in Congress, in governors' offices, and on special questions called ballot measures. Right before voting day, the Supreme Court — the highest court in the country — said no to a request from the Trump administration that would have thrown out about 20,000 mail-in ballots in California. The court decided so quickly that it did not even wait to hear California explain its side first, which judges almost never do.

Why this mattersWhoever wins these elections will decide the laws that affect things like school funding, taxes, and healthcare where you live. The fight over those 20,000 ballots is really a fight about trust — about whether voting rules are being followed fairly, and about how much power courts should use to step into elections while people are still voting.

Every two years, Americans choose new members of Congress, the group of lawmakers who write the country's rules. This time, all 435 seats in one part of Congress, called the House, are up for grabs — that's like filling every seat in ten school gyms with a different person. Over a third of the Senate, the other part of Congress, is being decided too.

Right now, the party called Republicans control the Senate by a slim margin — 53 seats to 47. Eleven close races will decide if they keep that lead or if the Democrats take over instead. Whoever wins gets more power to pass laws for the next two years.

Just one day before voting, the Supreme Court stepped into a fight. The federal government wanted California to throw out about 20,000 ballots, because officials worried some might have been mailed in by people who are not US citizens. California said it's already against the law for noncitizens to vote there, and doubted the government's numbers. The nine Supreme Court judges unanimously said no to tossing the ballots — and they moved so fast that they skipped the normal step of listening to California argue back first.

Voters in California will also decide on a special question called a ballot measure: should the state tax its billionaires more? Other states are deciding their own questions, including ones about abortion rights and how voting districts get drawn.

America Votes Tomorrow — And One Court Fight Already Made News

On Tuesday, every seat in the US House of Representatives, a third of the Senate, and 36 governorships are up for grabs. A day before voting even started, the Supreme Court unanimously shut down a Trump administration attempt to throw out roughly 20,000 mail-in ballots in California — and did it so fast that California's own lawyers never got to argue their side first.

Why this mattersWhoever wins the House, Senate, and governorships tomorrow will shape the laws, courts, and budgets that affect daily life for the next two years — from taxes to abortion access to who draws the voting maps you'll vote under next. The Supreme Court fight also shows how election disputes about voter eligibility and fairness can land at the country's highest court within days, not years, right as votes are being cast.

Tomorrow is Election Day across the United States, and almost every layer of government is on the ballot at once. All 435 seats in the House of Representatives are being decided, along with 35 of the 100 Senate seats and 36 state governorships. Voters in several states will also decide ballot measures — proposals that go straight to the public instead of through elected lawmakers — covering how voting districts get drawn, abortion rights, and in California, a proposed new tax aimed specifically at billionaires.

The Senate math is the headline number to watch. Republicans currently hold a narrow 53-47 majority, and both parties agree that 11 specific Senate races are close enough to flip the chamber either way. Whoever controls the Senate controls which judges get confirmed, which laws get voted on at all, and whether a president's agenda moves or stalls — so a handful of races in a handful of states will decide the balance of power in Washington for the next two years.

One day before voting, the Supreme Court stepped into an unusually tense fight. The Trump administration had asked the court to force California to set aside about 20,000 mail-in ballots, arguing that federal data suggested some of them could have been cast by people who are not US citizens — who are not legally allowed to vote in American elections. The Supreme Court rejected the request unanimously, meaning every justice agreed, regardless of which president had appointed them.

What made the ruling notable wasn't just the outcome — it was the speed. The court acted so quickly that it didn't wait to hear California's response before deciding. Courts almost always let both sides make their case before ruling; skipping that step is rare enough that legal observers flagged it as unusual, even though the result favored no particular party ideologically.

California's position is that noncitizen voting is already illegal under state law, so there was nothing new to enforce, and state officials have publicly questioned how reliable the federal government's underlying data actually is — without a public breakdown of that data, there's no independent way to check the claim. The dispute over the data itself remains unresolved; the court's ruling settled only whether those 20,000 ballots would be set aside before the election, not whether the underlying allegation holds up.

None of this changes what's actually at stake tomorrow: control of Congress, control of dozens of state governments, and ballot measures that could reshape voting districts, abortion access, and tax policy for years to come. The court fight was a preview of how contested this election already is — and a reminder that even unanimous, fast-moving legal decisions can leave the bigger argument unsettled.

Words worth knowing

  • Ballot measure — A proposed law or policy that voters decide on directly, instead of leaving the decision to elected lawmakers.
  • Emergency ruling — A fast decision from a court, issued outside its usual slower process, sometimes before every side has had a chance to argue its case.

One AI Company Promised Almost $900 Billion — More Than Any Company Has Promised Before

Anthropic makes a chatbot called Claude, kind of like a very smart computer helper. Before selling shares of itself to the public for the first time, the company had to tell everyone a huge secret: it has promised to spend about $895 billion on computers and buildings that power its AI. That is one of the biggest spending promises any company has ever made.

Why this mattersWhen one company makes a giant promise like this, it affects way more than just that company. Banks, workers, chip factories, and even people's retirement savings can be tied to whether these AI bets pay off — so if Anthropic can't keep growing fast enough, the ripple could reach a lot of people, even ones who never use a chatbot.
Read the whole story

Anthropic wants to sell pieces of itself to investors, called shares, in something called an IPO. To do that, by law it has to show everyone its finances in a big document.

That document revealed something enormous. Anthropic has promised, through contracts, to spend roughly $895 billion over time — almost all of it on powerful computers and the giant buildings, called data centers, that run its AI programs. If you spent one million dollars every single day, it would take you about 2,450 years to spend that much.

The company is growing fast. Its sales jumped about 1,200% in a year, which means it is making roughly 13 times more money than before. But only a quarter of that giant $895 billion promise can be canceled if things go wrong — the rest has to be paid no matter what, as long as Anthropic keeps growing just as fast.

This news made investors nervous about the whole AI industry. In the weeks since, companies that make computer chips lost about $3.7 trillion in value on the stock market — except for Nvidia, the biggest chip maker, which barely moved.

Anthropic Just Told Investors It Owes Nearly $900 Billion for Computers

Anthropic, the company behind the AI chatbot Claude, is preparing to sell shares to the public for the first time. In the legal paperwork for that sale, it revealed something staggering: it has promised to spend roughly $895.4 billion on computing power over the coming years — almost all of it on the chips and data centers needed to run AI. Most of that money cannot legally be clawed back if things go wrong.

Why this mattersIf you use AI tools at school or expect to work with them in whatever job you eventually get, the companies behind them are making financial bets this large to keep the technology running and improving. If those bets go bad, it could mean fewer AI companies, pricier AI tools, or a shakeup in an industry a lot of future jobs may depend on — similar to how the 2008 financial crisis reshaped banking for a generation.
Read the whole story

Before a company can sell shares to the public for the first time — an event called an IPO, short for Initial Public Offering — it has to file a document with US regulators called an S-1, which lays out its finances in detail so potential investors know what they're buying into. Anthropic filed an amended version of this document on November 2, and buried inside it was a number nobody had seen before: roughly $895.4 billion in total spending commitments, almost entirely tied to buying and leasing computing power and building data centers — the warehouses full of specialized computer chips that train and run AI models like Anthropic's Claude.

To put that number in perspective, Anthropic's actual revenue — the money it has taken in from customers — was $33.24 billion for the first nine months of 2026, with one report putting a single recent quarter at around $17 billion. That's genuinely explosive growth, up roughly 1,200% from the same period a year before. But $895 billion is more than twenty-five times that nine-month revenue figure. It's a bet that the company's income will keep multiplying fast enough to cover commitments made years in advance, the way someone might sign a decade-long gym lease assuming their business will have grown tenfold by the time the bill comes due.

The filing included a crucial detail: only about $213 billion of the roughly $895 billion can legally be cancelled if Anthropic needs to back out. The rest — well over $600 billion — is locked in through binding purchase agreements and leases, regardless of whether the money materializes. Anthropic itself warned investors that meeting these obligations depends on sustaining very high revenue growth for years to come. If growth slows even somewhat, the company could find itself owing far more than it can pay.

The disclosure landed at an uneasy moment. Since October 6, stocks in companies that make the chips and storage hardware AI depends on have lost about $3.7 trillion in combined value. Companies like Marvell, Intel, Seagate, Western Digital, and AMD took the heaviest hits, while Nvidia — the company whose chips currently dominate AI training — barely moved. That split matters: investors aren't fleeing AI hardware broadly, they're questioning whether the companies supplying the broader chip ecosystem can really absorb the demand everyone has been promising.

Anthropic's number is now the largest publicly known AI-infrastructure spending commitment on record, and it has sharpened a debate that was already simmering: are AI companies locking themselves into bets they can't actually afford if the technology's growth slows down even slightly, or is this simply what it costs to build the infrastructure for a technology that's reshaping entire industries? Nobody — not the companies, not the investors, not the analysts — knows yet which answer is correct.

Words worth knowing
  • IPO — An Initial Public Offering is the first time a company sells shares of itself to the public, which is how it raises money and lets ordinary investors buy a piece of it.
  • S-1 — An S-1 is a legal document a US company must file with regulators before an IPO, disclosing its finances, risks, and business details so investors know what they're buying into.
  • Compute — Compute is shorthand for the computing power — specialized chips, servers, and data centers — needed to train and run AI systems, and it is extremely expensive to build and maintain.

U.S. Strikes Iranian Sites Near a Chokepoint That Moves a Fifth of the World's Oil — One Day Before the Midterms

On November 2, the United States carried out what an anonymous U.S. official called 'defensive' strikes on Iranian targets near Qeshm Island, in the Strait of Hormuz — the narrow sea passage through which a huge share of the world's oil travels by tanker. The strikes were described as limited, not a full return to open conflict, and came the day before the U.S. midterm elections. Diplomatic talks between the U.S. and Iran were reportedly still happening even as the strikes occurred, with Iran's negotiator saying 'the ball is now in America's court to prevent a war.'

Why this mattersIf tensions around the Strait of Hormuz rise further, it can push up the price of oil and gasoline worldwide, which shows up in the cost of filling a car's tank or in the price tag on almost anything shipped by sea or truck. It also matters because decisions about using military force — even 'limited' ones — can quietly expand into longer conflicts that affect military families, international stability, and how much trust other countries place in U.S. diplomacy going forward.
Read the whole story

Qeshm Island sits at the mouth of the Strait of Hormuz, a stretch of water so narrow that at its tightest point it's only about 21 miles wide — roughly the distance a slow highway drive might cover. Yet somewhere around a fifth of the world's oil, loaded onto tankers in the Persian Gulf, has to pass through it to reach the rest of the world. That makes it one of the most important pieces of geography on Earth for the global economy, and also one of the most fought-over: a country that controls or threatens that passage has leverage far beyond its size.

That is the backdrop against which the U.S. struck Iranian targets on November 2. A U.S. official, speaking without being named, described the action as 'defensive' — meaning intended to stop or deter an Iranian threat rather than to open a new war. Reporting also described the strikes as limited in scope, distinguishing them from what a full resumption of hostilities between the two countries would look like: sustained bombing campaigns, mobilized forces, or declared war. Whether 'limited' and 'defensive' are accurate descriptions, or simply the most favorable way to frame a military strike, is something that will only become clear with time and independent verification.

That verification is part of the story here. At the time of reporting, nearly every account of what happened — the targets hit, the scale of the operation, whether anyone was killed — traced back to the same single anonymous U.S. official. No independent confirmation of casualties or damage had emerged. That doesn't make the reporting false, but it does mean the specific details should be treated as provisional until other sources, including ones not relying on a government's own telling of its own strike, can confirm or correct them.

What makes the moment stranger is that diplomacy was reportedly still active while the bombs fell. Talks between U.S. and Iranian officials were said to be continuing, and Iran's own negotiator responded not with threats but with a pointed warning: 'the ball is now in America's court to prevent a war.' That is itself a message — it suggests Iran wants to be seen as the side still willing to talk, while placing responsibility for what happens next on Washington.

The timing is hard to ignore. The strikes landed on the eve of the U.S. midterm elections, when control of Congress was being decided. Whether that timing was coincidental, driven by military necessity, or shaped by political considerations is something reasonable people read differently — and it's exactly the kind of question where the honest answer is that we don't fully know.

Words worth knowing
  • Strait of Hormuz — A narrow sea passage between Iran and the Arabian Peninsula that a large share of the world's oil-carrying ships must pass through.
  • Chokepoint — A narrow, strategically important passage — like a strait or canal — where controlling or blocking it gives one side outsized power over trade or military movement.
  • Escalation — When a conflict grows more intense or widens in scope, often through a series of actions and responses that are each more serious than the last.
