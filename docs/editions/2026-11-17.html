Three of the World's Most Powerful Leaders Will Sit Down Together

On Thursday, something that has never happened before will happen. The leaders of the United States, China, and Russia will all sit at the same table. President Trump, President Xi Jinping, and President Vladimir Putin will meet in a Chinese city called Shenzhen.

Why this mattersWhen the leaders of the world's biggest countries talk about weapons and trade, it can change prices in stores, how safe the world feels, and whether wars get bigger or smaller. Even though this meeting is happening far away, decisions made there can ripple all the way to your own town.
Read the whole story

These three countries have the biggest armies, the most nuclear weapons, and some of the largest economies on Earth. Their leaders have never all sat down together before, even though each has met the others separately.

The meeting happens alongside a bigger event called APEC, where leaders from countries around the Pacific Ocean gather once a year to talk about trade. Shenzhen is a large city in southern China, a bit like a giant workshop town full of factories and skyscrapers.

Nobody knows exactly what the three leaders will talk about. Guesses include keeping dangerous weapons under control, the war happening in the Middle East, rules for buying and selling goods between countries, and maybe even the war in Ukraine — though it's unclear if that last one will get much real attention.

This meeting comes right after President Trump's political party lost several elections at home, which some people who study politics think could make it harder for him to get what he wants from the other two leaders. No written plan has been shared publicly, so grown-ups who follow this closely still disagree about what each side is really hoping to get out of it.

For the First Time Ever, the Leaders of the US, China and Russia Will Sit Down Together

China's government confirmed that President Xi Jinping will meet with Russia's Vladimir Putin and America's Donald Trump in the Chinese city of Shenzhen this Thursday. No sitting leaders of these three countries have ever shared a table like this before. The meeting is happening alongside a bigger economic summit, but the real story is the three of them in one room.

Why this mattersThese three countries together hold most of the world's nuclear weapons and account for a huge share of global trade, so decisions made quietly in a room like this can eventually show up in prices, in wars that do or don't happen, and in how safe the world feels from catastrophic weapons. Even a meeting with no clear agenda can shift how these governments deal with each other for months afterward.
Read the whole story

China's Foreign Ministry announced on Monday that Xi, Putin and Trump will all be in Shenzhen on Thursday, meeting on the sidelines of APEC — a yearly summit where leaders from countries around the Pacific Ocean, including the US and China, discuss trade and economic policy. The three leaders being in the same place isn't unusual on its own; what's unprecedented is that the leaders of the United States, China and Russia have never actually sat down together at once. Trump is already in Asia for the trip — his second visit to China this year — and this stop was added to the schedule.

Officials from China have floated a list of topics that might come up: stopping the spread of nuclear weapons, the ongoing war in the Middle East, and trade policy between the three countries. Ukraine — the country Russia invaded in 2022 — is on the list too, but with far less certainty. Trump has previously claimed he could end that war quickly; Putin is the one who started it and has shown no sign of withdrawing; and China has avoided taking a clear side, continuing to trade with Russia while calling for peace talks in general terms. Whether the three of them will actually dig into Ukraine, or just mention it and move on, is something nobody outside the meeting currently knows.

That uncertainty matters because no written agenda or joint statement has been released ahead of time. Normally, major diplomatic meetings are preceded by documents that signal what each side wants to get out of it. Here, there's nothing — which means analysts are left guessing at motives. One reading is that this is a serious, if early, step toward managing real tensions between three nuclear-armed powers. Another reading is that it's mostly symbolic: a photograph and a handshake that lets each leader show voters and allies back home that they're taken seriously on the world stage, without committing to anything.

The timing adds another layer. Trump and his party are dealing with a rough stretch after recent midterm elections — the votes held partway through a presidential term that often serve as a public verdict on how things are going. Some analysts think arriving in Asia right after losses like that could put Trump in a weaker position at the table, since Putin and Xi may calculate he has less room to make bold demands while managing problems at home. Other analysts think this reads too much into domestic politics; foreign leaders negotiate with whoever is in the room, not with poll numbers.

What actually gets said on Thursday will likely only become clear afterward, through statements, leaks, or the absence of any agreement at all. For now, the meeting itself — three leaders who rarely appear together, in the same place, at the same time — is the news.

The US Says: Ask First Before Sending Super-Computer Chips to These 5 Countries

The United States government just made a new rule. Companies now need special permission before sending powerful computer chips to data centers in five countries: Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, and Brazil. The US worries that computer chips sent there might secretly end up helping China, even without the chips ever crossing into China.

Why this mattersAI chips are the kind of technology that helps build voice assistants, video game graphics, and the computer programs grown-ups use at work. When powerful countries fight over who gets to use the best chips, it can change what kinds of technology get built, how fast, and who gets to use it first — including things that might end up in your games or apps someday.
Read the whole story

Imagine you own an amazing, super-fast gaming console. You lend it to a friend in another town. But what if your friend then lets someone you don't trust play on it over the internet, from far away? You never meant for that to happen, but it still did.

That is kind of what the US government is worried about. Companies like Nvidia make incredibly powerful computer chips. These chips are used in giant warehouses full of computers, called data centers, to run artificial intelligence, or AI — computer programs that can learn and think in chip-sized brains.

The US is worried that some data centers in those five countries could let Chinese companies use the chips' computing power over the internet, even if the actual chips never leave the building. This is sometimes called 'diversion' — when something ends up somewhere it wasn't supposed to go.

This new rule came right after Nvidia's boss, Jensen Huang, resigned during a different chip-smuggling scandal. The rule is called 'interim,' which means it's not final yet — people can still argue about it and it might change.

Words worth knowing
  • AI chip — A small but extremely powerful computer part that helps machines learn, recognize patterns, and make quick decisions, almost like a tiny artificial brain.
  • export license — Special permission from a government that a company must get before it's allowed to send certain products, like powerful chips, to another country.

The US Just Closed a Backdoor That Let China Rent American AI Power From Abroad

On Monday, the US Commerce Department ordered data centers in five countries — Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil — to get a special government license before sending advanced AI chips there. The real target isn't those countries; it's a workaround that let Chinese companies access powerful American chips without the chips ever crossing into China. The rule follows a chip-smuggling scandal that forced out Nvidia's chief executive, Jensen Huang.

Why this mattersThis fight over chips is really a fight over who gets to build the most powerful AI first, and the rules that come out of it will shape which companies and countries have access to that power for years. If you've used an AI chatbot, image generator, or voice assistant, it almost certainly ran on chips like the ones at the centre of this dispute — so decisions about who can buy or rent them affect which products keep improving and which get left behind.
Read the whole story

For years, the US has tried to stop China from getting its hands on the most advanced AI chips — the specialised processors, mostly made by Nvidia, that train systems like chatbots and image generators. The logic is straightforward: these chips are also useful for military planning, surveillance, and other applications Washington doesn't want to help a geopolitical rival build. So the US restricted direct sales of top-tier chips to China.

But a direct sale isn't the only way to use a chip. Companies can build huge warehouses full of chips — data centers — in a third country, then let customers anywhere in the world rent computing time over the internet, the same way you might rent a server to run an app. The chip itself never leaves, say, Malaysia. But a buyer in China can still use its processing power remotely, as if it owned the hardware. Monday's rule tries to shut that door by requiring a US export license before chips can even be installed in data centers in five countries that Washington says carry an 'unacceptable risk of diversion' — official language for 'we think chips sent here could end up serving Chinese buyers.'

The timing isn't random. It follows a scandal that forced Jensen Huang, Nvidia's co-founder and chief executive, to resign amid an investigation into chip smuggling — reports say he sought help from Taiwan's government during the affair. Nvidia didn't necessarily do anything illegal itself, but the case exposed how porous the existing rules were: chips, or the computing power they generate, were apparently finding ways to reach China regardless of the restrictions meant to stop that. This new rule is widely read as Washington's attempt to patch that specific hole.

Not every outlet covering this agrees on what it means. Hong Kong's South China Morning Post describes the US as 'closing China's overseas data centre loophole' — a description that sounds neutral but is worth reading carefully, since the paper is owned by Alibaba, a major Chinese technology company with its own stake in how chip restrictions are portrayed. That doesn't make the framing wrong, but it's a reminder that even factual-sounding headlines can carry a point of view depending on who owns the newsroom.

What's less clear is how any of this actually gets enforced. The rule is 'interim,' meaning it's a first version that can still be changed after public comment — companies, governments and lawyers will get to argue over the details. And the US hasn't fully explained how it would verify, in practice, where computing power genuinely ends up once it leaves a data center and travels through the internet. Tracking a physical chip at a border is one thing; tracking who's secretly logging into a cloud server from another country is a much harder problem, and critics say the rule may be easier to announce than to enforce.

Words worth knowing
  • interim rule — A government regulation that takes effect right away but is still open to public comment and could be changed later.
  • export license — Official government permission required before a company can sell or ship certain restricted goods, like advanced chips, to another country.
  • diversion risk — The danger that something sold to one buyer or country secretly ends up being used by someone else it wasn't meant for.

Americans Barely Spent More on Shopping Last Month

Every month, the U.S. government counts up how much money people spent shopping — on cars, clothes, furniture, even groceries. In October, that total grew by only a tiny bit, far less than the month before. People bought fewer cars, fewer couches, fewer sneakers, and even fewer groceries than expected.

Why this mattersWhen people across the whole country buy less, stores sell less, and that can mean fewer hours for workers or fewer new things on shelves. It also happens right before the holidays, when families often buy gifts, so a slowdown now could be a hint about what shopping will look like this December.
Read the whole story

Imagine you get an allowance, and last month you spent almost all of it buying things. This month, you kept most of your money in your piggy bank instead. That is roughly what happened across the whole country in October.

The government measured how much money all Americans together spent in stores and online. It only grew by 0.2%, which is a tiny sliver — like going from 500 pennies to 501. The month before, spending had grown four times faster, by 0.8%, but that was mostly because gas got more expensive, not because people bought more stuff.

This time, people pulled back almost everywhere. They bought fewer cars. They spent less on furniture, sports gear, and clothes. They even bought less food at the grocery store than usual.

Grown-ups who study money for a living, called economists, think this might mean shoppers are getting cautious right before the holiday season — the busiest shopping time of the year. But the numbers alone do not say why people are holding back. It could be because prices are too high, because borrowing money costs more right now, or for some other reason nobody has pinned down yet.

U.S. Shoppers Suddenly Got Cautious — Right Before the Holidays

Americans spent a lot less eagerly in October than they did the month before. Government data released Tuesday showed retail sales grew just 0.2%, down sharply from September's 0.8% jump. People cut back not just on cars, but on furniture, clothes, sporting goods, and even groceries — a sign that the pullback is widespread, not just one industry having a bad month.

Why this mattersThis report is one of the clearest early signals of whether the U.S. economy is cooling down, and it lands right before the biggest shopping season of the year — a season that shapes how many seasonal jobs get created and how confident businesses feel going into the next year. Even if you're not the one doing the spending, slower retail sales can mean fewer job openings, more cautious hiring, and a different mood in the economy your family and community operate in.
Read the whole story

Every month, the U.S. Commerce Department — the government agency that tracks how the economy is doing — surveys thousands of stores and adds up how much money shoppers spent. That number is called retail sales, and it's one of the most closely watched signs of economic health, because consumer spending makes up roughly two-thirds of all economic activity in the United States. When people buy less, businesses sell less, order less from factories, and eventually hire fewer workers. The reverse is true too. So a slowdown in this number gets attention well beyond the shopping aisle.

October's number was weak. A 0.2% increase sounds like growth, but it's barely above zero once you account for the fact that prices on many goods were also rising — meaning people weren't actually buying much more stuff, just paying slightly more for roughly the same amount. Compare that to September's 0.8% gain, which looked much stronger on paper but was mostly driven by one thing: rising gas prices. When gas costs more, the dollar amount spent at gas stations goes up even if nobody is driving any further, which inflates the overall retail figure without reflecting real growth in spending power.

What makes October's report more worrying to economists isn't just the headline number — it's which categories slowed down. Car sales fell, which was expected. But spending also dropped on furniture, sporting goods, clothing, and groceries. Groceries are the key detail here: people don't usually cut back on food unless they're being genuinely careful with money, since groceries are one of the last things most households trim. A broad retreat across unrelated categories — cars, couches, sneakers, and cereal all at once — looks less like a coincidence and more like a pattern.

The report itself doesn't explain why shoppers pulled back, and that's the part economists are arguing about. One read is that high interest rates — the extra cost of borrowing money, set largely by the Federal Reserve, America's central bank — are finally discouraging big purchases, since things like cars and furniture are often bought on credit. Another read is that prices for everyday goods, even if growing more slowly than during the worst of recent inflation, are still high enough that households are feeling squeezed and choosing to hold back rather than spend freely. A third possibility is simpler: people might just be waiting, saving ahead of the holiday season rather than pulling back for good.

Timing matters here. October sits right before the stretch from Thanksgiving through December, when American retailers typically make a large share of their annual sales and hire extra seasonal staff to handle the rush. If shoppers are already tightening their belts beforehand, that's a worrying signal for stores counting on a strong holiday season to carry their year. If instead this turns out to be a one-month blip, the worry fades. Which of those two stories is true won't be clear until the November and December numbers come in.

Words worth knowing
  • Retail sales — The total amount of money people spend buying goods at stores and online in a given month, used as a quick snapshot of how willing consumers are to spend.
  • Commerce Department — The U.S. government agency responsible for collecting and publishing official economic data, including the monthly retail sales report.
  • Interest rates — The cost of borrowing money, set largely by the Federal Reserve (America's central bank); higher rates make loans for things like cars and furniture more expensive, which can discourage big purchases.

  1. When shoppers pull back like this, should the government step in to encourage more spending, or let the slowdown play out on its own?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Step in

    • A broad pullback across unrelated categories like groceries and clothing can signal the start of a deeper slowdown that's easier to prevent than to reverse.

    • Consumer spending makes up most of U.S. economic activity, so when people buy less, businesses earn less and may cut jobs or hours.
    • Confident spending signals that households feel financially secure, which tends to create a positive cycle of hiring and investment.

    Caution is healthy

    • Households that save rather than overspend are better protected against job loss, rising prices, or unexpected expenses.
    • A pullback in spending can cool inflation, which benefits everyone paying higher prices for essentials like food and rent.
    • An economy built on constant high spending, often fueled by debt, can be fragile — pausing to rebuild savings can make the foundation sturdier.
