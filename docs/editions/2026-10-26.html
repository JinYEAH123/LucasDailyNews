A Chatbot Company Wants to Sell Pieces of Itself to Everyone

Anthropic, the company that makes a chatbot called Claude, asked the government on Monday for permission to sell small pieces of itself to anyone who wants one. People might be able to start buying starting November 16. But the same paperwork revealed the company lost an enormous amount of money this year, even while it was also earning more than ever.

Why this mattersYou've probably used a chatbot, or seen a grown-up use one, without thinking much about the company behind it. When a company 'goes public,' regular people — maybe even your family someday — can own a small piece of it and have a say in big decisions. And when a company openly says its own invention could be dangerous, that's something worth thinking about whether or not you ever buy a share.
Read the whole story

Claude is a chatbot. You type a question, and it writes back like a very fast, very well-read friend. The company that built it, Anthropic, decided on Monday to ask the U.S. government for permission to sell shares — tiny pieces of ownership in the company — to anyone who wants to buy them. This is called 'going public.'

If everything is approved, people could start buying and selling those shares as soon as November 16, on a stock market called Nasdaq, under the short code 'ANT.' People who study the stock market think this could be one of the biggest first-day sales of a company's shares ever seen.

The paperwork had a surprise in it. Anthropic lost $80.7 billion in just the first six months of this year. To put that in perspective, if you spent one million dollars every single day, it would take you more than 200 years to spend that much. And yet, at the same time, the company is also earning more money than ever before from people and businesses using Claude. Some people are guessing the company could end up worth more than $2 trillion once trading begins — but that is just a guess. The real number will not be known until the shares actually start selling.

There was one more surprising thing. In the same documents, Anthropic itself warned that artificial intelligence, the kind of technology behind chatbots like Claude, could cause 'catastrophic or existential risks to humanity' — meaning dangers serious enough to badly hurt, or even threaten, human life as we know it. It is unusual for a company to say something like that about its own product while also asking people to invest their money in it.

Words worth knowing
  • Going public — When a company sells small pieces of itself, called shares, so that anyone who wants to can buy them, usually through a stock market.
  • Net loss — The amount of money a company spent that was more than what it earned, once everything is added up.

Anthropic, Maker of the Claude Chatbot, Files to Go Public — While Warning Its Own Technology Could Be 'Catastrophic'

Anthropic, the company behind the Claude AI chatbot, filed paperwork on Monday to sell shares to the public for the first time, making it the first major advanced AI company to do so. Trading could begin as soon as November 16th on the Nasdaq stock exchange, in what analysts expect to be one of the biggest stock debuts ever. The filing revealed a staggering $80.7 billion net loss in just the first half of this year, even as the company's revenue is growing fast. Buried in the same document is a warning from Anthropic itself that artificial intelligence could pose 'catastrophic or existential risks to humanity.'

Why this mattersIf you've used an AI chatbot for homework help, or seen one built into an app on your phone, you're part of the market this IPO is betting billions of dollars on. The outcome also matters beyond the stock price: it will shape how much money flows into building ever more powerful AI systems, and how seriously the industry is forced to take its own warnings about risk.
Read the whole story

To sell shares on a public stock exchange, a company first has to file something called an S-1 with the Securities and Exchange Commission, the U.S. government agency that polices financial markets. Think of it as a mandatory tell-all: the company has to disclose its finances, its risks, and anything a reasonable investor would want to know before buying a piece of it. Anthropic's S-1, filed Monday, is the first time a frontier AI lab — one of the handful of companies building the most powerful AI systems in the world — has taken this step, rather than staying privately owned and funded by investors like venture capital firms or tech giants.

The numbers inside are extreme in both directions. Anthropic lost $80.7 billion in the first six months of the year, an eye-watering figure driven mostly by the staggering cost of the computing power needed to train and run AI models — specialized chips and data centers that cost billions of dollars to build and operate. At the same time, its revenue, the money actually coming in from customers paying to use Claude, has been surging, as businesses and individuals pay for access to AI tools. This combination — huge losses next to fast-growing sales — is the classic shape of a company betting everything on scaling up before worrying about turning a profit.

Nobody yet knows what Anthropic will actually be worth once shares start trading. News reports have floated a valuation above $2 trillion — meaning investors would be pricing the whole company at more than the economic output of most countries — but that number is speculation ahead of the 'roadshow,' the weeks before an IPO (initial public offering, the formal process of a company selling shares to the public for the first time) when company executives pitch big investors to drum up interest and gauge what they're willing to pay. The S-1 filing itself contains no share price and no valuation; those get set later, closer to the actual listing date.

The strangest part of the filing may be the warning itself. Buried in the legally required risk disclosures — the section where companies list everything that could go wrong, partly to protect themselves from future lawsuits — Anthropic states plainly that AI could pose 'catastrophic or existential risks to humanity.' That is not the kind of line companies typically put in documents meant to attract investors. It reflects a tension at the heart of the entire AI industry: the same executives racing to build and profit from these systems are often the ones most publicly worried about what happens if the technology goes wrong, whether through misuse, accidents, or systems that act in ways their creators didn't intend. The filing also flags rising cybersecurity concerns, acknowledging that AI systems and the data behind them are increasingly attractive targets for hackers.

Anthropic's choice to go public now, rather than staying private indefinitely like it could, raises its own set of questions. Public companies face quarterly pressure to show growth and profit to shareholders, pressure that private companies funded by a smaller circle of investors can sometimes avoid. Whether that pressure pushes AI labs to move faster and cut corners, or whether public capital markets are simply the only place left to raise the staggering sums frontier AI now requires, is something investors, regulators, and competitors will be watching closely once trading begins.

Words worth knowing
  • S-1 filing — A detailed document a company must file with U.S. regulators before it can sell shares to the public, disclosing its finances and risks.
  • IPO (initial public offering) — The first time a company sells shares of itself to public investors on a stock exchange.
  • Valuation — An estimate of what a whole company is worth, usually calculated by multiplying its share price by the total number of shares.

  1. Should a company be allowed to go public while its own filing warns that its technology could pose 'catastrophic or existential risks to humanity'?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes, let it proceed

    • Requiring honest disclosure of risk, even extreme risk, is exactly what financial regulation is supposed to produce, and punishing honesty would only teach companies to hide their concerns instead.
    • Investors are capable of weighing a stated risk themselves, and plenty of industries — nuclear power, pharmaceuticals, airlines — disclose severe risks in prospectuses without being barred from public markets.
    • Blocking Anthropic specifically wouldn't stop the underlying technology from being built; it would just hand the field to competitors who may be less transparent about the dangers.

    No, it's a troubling signal

    • If a company genuinely believes its product could cause catastrophic harm, taking public money and facing pressure to grow fast creates an incentive to downplay or outrun exactly the risks it just admitted to.
    • Boilerplate legal warnings buried in long filings are often written to limit future lawsuits rather than to genuinely inform most retail investors, who are unlikely to read or weigh them carefully.
    • Going public normalizes treating potentially existential technology as an ordinary growth stock, which may numb both investors and the public to how seriously the warning should be taken.

  2. Does an $80.7 billion loss alongside surging revenue show a smart long-term growth bet, or a dangerous bubble?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    It's a sound growth strategy

    • Many successful tech giants, including Amazon in its early years, ran large losses for years while reinvesting heavily in infrastructure before becoming hugely profitable.
    • Building AI models requires enormous upfront spending on computing power, and front-loading that cost while demand is surging can be a rational bet on capturing market share early.
    • Rapidly growing revenue shows real customer demand exists, which is a stronger foundation than losses driven by pure hype with no paying users.

    It's a warning sign of a bubble

    • A loss of this scale is unprecedented even by the standards of past tech giants, raising the question of whether spending is sustainable at any level of future revenue.
    • Investor enthusiasm for AI has at times outpaced evidence of durable profits, echoing earlier periods, like the dot-com era, when stock prices detached from business fundamentals.
    • If growth slows or competition from rival AI labs intensifies, a company burning this much cash could struggle to raise further funding on favorable terms, leaving public shareholders exposed.

Serbia's Students Help Vote Out a Leader Who Ruled for Over a Decade

In the country of Serbia, in Europe, a roof fell down at a train station and killed 16 people. People got angry and said poor construction and corruption were to blame. Students led huge protests, and on Sunday, those protests turned into votes. The country's long-time leader, Aleksandar Vučić, lost the election and admitted defeat.

Why this mattersThis story shows that when people feel something is deeply unfair — like a building collapsing because of neglect — they can organise and change their country's direction, even against a leader who has been in charge for a very long time. It also matters because Serbia sits between powerful countries like Russia, the United States, and the European Union, so who leads it can shape alliances far beyond its own borders.
Read the whole story

Last November, the roof of a train station in the Serbian city of Novi Sad crashed down. Sixteen people died. Many Serbians believed the building had been built badly because officials took bribes and cut corners instead of doing the job properly.

Students across Serbia were furious. They marched in the streets for months, demanding honesty from their government. Their protest grew into a political group called the Student List.

On Sunday, Serbia held an election. Almost 7 out of every 10 adults who could vote did — the highest number since the country's old union, Yugoslavia, broke apart more than 30 years ago. The Student List won about 53 votes out of every 100 cast, while Vučić's party got about 39.

Vučić had led Serbia for more than ten years, balancing friendships with Russia, China, the United States, and Europe all at once. Now his party has lost its grip on power, and Serbia's next steps could change which of those big countries it leans toward most.

Words worth knowing
  • corruption — When people in power use their position to cheat or take money unfairly instead of doing their job honestly.
  • turnout — The number of people who actually show up to vote in an election.

After a Deadly Roof Collapse, Serbian Students Vote Out a 12-Year Ruler

Serbia's president, Aleksandar Vučić, has conceded defeat after an election triggered by a protest movement that began with a tragedy: the collapse of a train station roof that killed 16 people. The movement, led largely by university students, turned a year of street protests into a parliamentary majority. Nearly 70% of voters turned out, the highest since Yugoslavia broke apart in 1992.

Why this mattersA roof that should never have fallen down became the reason an entire government lost its majority, which is a reminder that infrastructure failures are rarely just bad luck — they are often a receipt for how a country has actually been run. The outcome will also help decide whether Serbia drifts closer to the European Union or keeps playing Russia, China and the West off against each other, a choice that shapes trade, visas and alliances for millions of people in the Balkans for years to come.
Read the whole story

In November 2024, a concrete canopy over the entrance of a train station in Novi Sad, Serbia's second city, collapsed and killed 16 people. Investigators and ordinary Serbians alike pointed to the same explanation: a renovation project rushed through with the kind of contracts and oversight that critics say have defined Vučić's government for years — work awarded to politically connected firms, inspections skipped or faked, corners cut to finish fast and look good on paper. The collapse became a symbol rather than an isolated accident, and students at universities across the country began organising blockades and marches that, over the following months, grew into the largest sustained protest movement Serbia had seen in decades.

Vučić had run Serbia for more than twelve years, first as prime minister and then as president, building a style of rule that balanced competing foreign patrons against each other. He courted European Union membership talks while also maintaining close ties to Russia, a historic ally with deep cultural links to Serbia, and increasingly to China, which has poured money into Serbian mines, factories and infrastructure. This balancing act gave Serbia leverage and investment from multiple directions at once, but critics argued it also let Vučić dodge the kind of scrutiny — on media freedom, judicial independence, corruption — that a government fully committed to EU membership would have had to accept.

Facing sustained protests and plummeting public trust, Vučić called a snap parliamentary election, called early rather than at its scheduled date, likely gambling that he could outlast the movement at the ballot box the way he had outlasted earlier waves of unrest. The gamble failed. The Student List, the political vehicle that grew out of the protest movement, took roughly 53% of the vote to about 39% for Vučić's party, according to Bloomberg's tally — a decisive margin in a country where his party had dominated every election for over a decade. Turnout reached nearly 70%, a figure not seen since the chaotic early 1990s when Yugoslavia was splitting into separate countries.

The precise number of parliamentary seats was still being finalised as results came in — Vučić's own party estimated the Student List would hold somewhere between 135 and 140 of the 250 seats, enough for a working majority on its own. Whatever the final count, the shift is large enough to end Vučić's uncontested grip on Serbian politics, even though as president he is not directly removed by this result and may remain in office for now while a new government forms around the Student List's majority.

Words worth knowing
  • Snap election — An election called earlier than its scheduled date, usually because a leader hopes to win quickly before a problem gets worse.
  • Turnout — The share of eligible voters who actually cast a ballot, used as a rough measure of how much people care about a vote.
  • Balancing act (geopolitical) — A strategy of staying on good terms with rival world powers at once — here, the EU, Russia and China — instead of committing firmly to one side.

Trump Says He's Close to Choosing: Peace Deal or Attack on Iran

President Trump says a big decision is coming soon. Should the United States make a peace deal with Iran, or take military action instead? He said on television that he could 'finish it off' easily, or make a deal. Nothing has been decided yet.

Why this mattersOil is used to make gasoline for cars, and to make electricity and many everyday products like plastic. If the Strait of Hormuz becomes blocked by fighting, oil could get harder to find, and prices could rise for families everywhere — including yours, at the gas pump or in the store.

Trump spoke on a TV show called Fox News on Sunday. He said he has a big decision coming up. He can either make a deal with Iran, or take military action against it.

Iran is a country in the Middle East. The United States and Iran have been rivals for many years. People from four other countries — Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Turkey, and Pakistan — are acting as mediators, trying to help the two sides talk instead of fight.

Near Iran there is a narrow strip of water called the Strait of Hormuz. Many ships carrying oil travel through it to reach the rest of the world. If fighting starts again, ships might not be able to pass safely, and oil could become harder to get everywhere.

So far, nothing has actually happened. No deal has been signed. No attack has begun. This is only what Trump said he is thinking about doing.

Trump Says He's Close to Choosing Between a Deal With Iran or Finishing the Fight

President Trump said he is nearing a decision on Iran: either reach a diplomatic agreement or resume military action that he claims could end the standoff quickly. He made the comments in a Fox News interview, as diplomats from four countries keep trying to broker talks and oil markets watch nervously.

Why this mattersDecisions made in rooms far away can still land close to home — through gas prices at the pump, through what's on the news, through the uneasy feeling of watching two countries decide whether to fight. Whether this ends in a deal or a conflict will shape the next phase of a decades-long standoff between the US and Iran.
Read the whole story

Speaking on Fox News' 'Sunday Morning Futures,' President Trump laid out the choice in blunt terms: 'Do I finish it off, which would be very easy to do, or do I make a deal? That's all.' He didn't say which way he's leaning, or what 'finishing it off' would actually involve — more airstrikes, a broader campaign, or something else. The vagueness is probably the point. Keeping Iran's leaders unsure of what comes next is itself a form of pressure.

This isn't happening in a vacuum. Diplomats from Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Turkey, and Pakistan have been quietly working to find a negotiated path forward, acting as go-betweens since the US and Iran don't have normal diplomatic relations with each other. Their efforts show that plenty of regional powers would rather see a deal than another round of conflict — they have their own economies and security to worry about if a war escalates nearby.

One reason the stakes are so high: the Strait of Hormuz, a narrow waterway between Iran and the Arabian Peninsula that roughly a fifth of the world's oil shipments pass through. If fighting resumed and Iran decided to disrupt shipping there — something it has threatened before — oil prices could spike fast, raising gas prices and shipping costs well beyond the Middle East. That's one reason markets are paying close attention to Trump's words, even though he hasn't announced any actual decision.

It's also worth noting the timing. Trump's comments come as the November 3rd midterm elections approach, when American voters decide who controls Congress. Foreign policy moves — or even just tough talk about them — often get read through a political lens close to an election, since a president's handling of a crisis abroad can shape how voters see his party at home. That doesn't mean the decision itself is political, but it adds a layer worth watching.

For now, nothing has actually changed on the ground. No strike has been ordered, no deal has been signed. What's changed is the sense that a decision is coming soon, which is why both diplomats and oil traders are watching closely for the next move.

Words worth knowing
  • Strait of Hormuz — A narrow sea passage between Iran and the Arabian Peninsula that a huge share of the world's oil shipments must pass through, making it a major flashpoint if tensions rise.
  • mediator — A country or person who works to help two sides that don't talk directly reach an agreement, often by passing messages back and forth.
  • midterm elections — US elections held partway through a president's term that decide who controls Congress, often seen as a check on the president's popularity.
