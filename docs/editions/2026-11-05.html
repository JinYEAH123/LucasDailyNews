Millions of Americans Vote, and One Political Party Wins Almost Everywhere

On November 4, 2026, Americans voted in elections called the midterms. One political party, the Democrats, won almost every close race there was. The other party, the Republicans, did not win a single seat they didn't already have.

Why this mattersWhen one political party controls Congress, it becomes much harder for the president to pass new laws on his own. This means decisions about things like taxes, schools, and prices at the gas station could move more slowly, or go in a different direction, for the next two years.

Imagine two big teams, the Democrats and the Republicans, competing for seats in America's law-making group called Congress. Congress has two parts: the Senate and the House of Representatives. This time, the Democrats won control of both parts.

The Democrats took seven Senate seats and almost 30 House seats away from the Republicans. That's like one soccer team scoring goal after goal and the other team scoring zero. Democrats also won five new state governor jobs. The Republicans did not win a single seat, district, or governor's job away from the Democrats, even though they had worked hard to draw voting maps that were supposed to help them.

Right after the results came in, the top Republican in the House, a man named Mike Johnson, said he would step down from his leadership job when the new Congress starts in January. President Trump said the results surprised him a little. He blamed some of his own party's candidates, and also high gas and oil prices, for the losses.

Now Democrats will have the power to slow down or change many of President Trump's plans for the next two years, because he needs Congress to agree before many new laws can happen.

Democrats Sweep the Midterms — and a Gerrymander Built to Stop Them Failed

On November 4, 2026, American voters handed Democrats control of both the House of Representatives and the Senate, along with five governorships and a wave of state legislatures. Republicans, who had spent months redrawing election maps specifically to protect their seats, didn't flip a single one anywhere in the country. The fallout was immediate: House Speaker Mike Johnson announced he's stepping down, and President Trump now faces two years where Congress can block almost anything he tries to pass.

Why this mattersWhoever controls Congress decides whether new laws on things like healthcare costs, student loans, climate rules, or taxes actually get written and passed — or stall completely for two years. A divided government often means less gets changed quickly, which can feel frustrating if you're waiting on a policy that affects your family, but it also means no single party can push through changes without having to compromise.
Read the whole story

Every two years, roughly midway through a president's four-year term, Americans vote in what are called midterm elections — contests for Congress and many state and local offices, but not for president. Because there's no presidential race to dominate the headlines, midterms often become a referendum on how the sitting president is doing. This year, that referendum went badly for President Trump's Republican Party: Democrats flipped seven Senate seats, nearly 30 House districts, and five governor's mansions, while taking control of as many as a dozen state legislative chambers (the exact count is still being finalized).

What makes the result especially striking is what didn't happen on the Republican side: zero. Not one Democratic-held Senate seat, House seat, governorship, or state legislature flipped to the GOP — despite a aggressive push earlier in the year to redraw congressional district maps in Republican-controlled states. This process, called redistricting, happens because district boundaries get redrawn periodically, and the party in power in a state legislature often draws the lines to favor its own candidates — a practice critics call gerrymandering. Republicans hoped new maps would lock in House seats regardless of how the national mood shifted. Instead, Democrats won anyway, suggesting the political wave was large enough to overwhelm maps built to prevent exactly this outcome.

The most immediate casualty was House Speaker Mike Johnson, the top Republican in the House of Representatives and third in line to the presidency. He announced he won't seek to continue as GOP leader when the new Congress begins in January — a direct consequence of his party's drubbing, even though he himself wasn't up against a serious challenger. His departure opens a contested race among House Republicans to pick a new leader, one who will have to manage a smaller, more demoralized caucus.

President Trump, for his part, said he was "a little bit surprised" by the results. He pinned the losses on individual candidates running weak campaigns and on high gas and oil prices squeezing voters' wallets, rather than on his own policies or popularity. He also said he would veto — formally reject — any Democratic-backed legislation that reaches his desk, a power the president has when Congress passes bills. Still, he indicated he'd accept the election results as legitimate, which matters given his past disputes over election outcomes.

The practical effect of all this is what's often called divided government: one party holds the presidency while the opposing party controls Congress. For the next two years, Trump will struggle to pass new laws without Democratic cooperation, since bills need to clear both the House and Senate before reaching his desk. Democrats, in turn, gain the power to launch investigations, block nominees, and shape the legislative agenda — though they'll also share blame if nothing gets done.

Words worth knowing
  • Midterm elections — U.S. elections held roughly halfway through a president's four-year term, covering Congress and many state offices but not the presidency itself.
  • Redistricting (gerrymandering) — The redrawing of voting district boundaries, which the party in power in a state can shape to favor its own candidates — a practice called gerrymandering when done aggressively.
  • Veto — A president's power to formally reject a bill passed by Congress, preventing it from becoming law unless Congress overrides the veto.

Across America, Towns Vote No to Giant AI Computer Buildings

On November 4, 2026, people in at least 38 places across the United States voted on whether to allow huge AI computer buildings near their homes. Almost every single one said no. The very next day, the stock market had one of its roughest days in years, as investors worried the AI building boom might slow down.

Why this mattersThe electricity that charges your tablet and the water that comes out of your tap could one day be shared with a giant AI building near your town. This story is about regular people deciding they get a say in that, even when it pushes back against huge, powerful companies.
Read the whole story

Picture a building as big as ten football fields, packed wall to wall with computers. That's a data center. These buildings run AI programs like chatbots and image generators. They also use a massive amount of electricity and water, and they hum loudly, day and night.

On November 4, voters in 38 places across the country decided whether to allow new data centers to be built nearby. Out of all those places, only one voted yes. Everywhere else, people said no. Voters also picked governors and senators who promised to put rules on the AI industry, and those candidates won too.

People who study this kind of thing say this is a big deal. It shows regular voters pushing back hard against a powerful industry, something that could slow down how fast AI companies can build.

The pushback rattled the stock market. On November 4, stocks had jumped by a huge amount after election results came in. But by November 5, tech and computer-chip companies lost a giant pile of value, as investors got nervous that fewer data centers would mean less money for those companies. Exact numbers differ depending on which news source you check, because the story kept changing hour by hour.

Voters Just Said No to AI's Biggest Buildings — and Wall Street Panicked

On November 4, 2026, people in at least 38 places across the United States voted on whether to allow or restrict giant AI data centers in their towns. Almost everywhere, they voted to restrict them — only one town voted to welcome them. The next day, tech stocks had their worst drop in years, as investors worried the AI building boom might be slowing down.

Why this mattersIf you've used an AI chatbot, streamed a video, or scrolled social media today, a data center somewhere made that possible — and this vote is about who gets to decide how many more of those buildings get built, and where. The stock market reaction also touches anyone with a retirement account or pension invested in index funds, since those often include the same tech and chip companies that just lost value.
Read the whole story

Picture a windowless building the size of several football fields, humming around the clock, drawing as much electricity as a small city and gallons of water by the thousands to keep its computers cool. That's a data center — the physical infrastructure behind every AI chatbot answer, every recommendation algorithm, every bit of "the cloud." Tech companies have been racing to build thousands of these across the US, because more computing power means more powerful AI. But on November 4, voters in dozens of towns and counties pushed back hard, passing ballot measures — local votes where citizens directly decide a policy question, not just elect a person — that ban or restrict new data centers. Out of dozens of these votes, only one came out in favor of allowing them.

The anger isn't really about AI as an abstract idea. It's about what a data center does to a neighborhood. Residents in these places have complained about electricity bills rising because data centers compete for limited power supply, water tables dropping in dry regions, constant low-frequency noise from cooling systems, and vast plots of land — sometimes former farmland — getting fenced off for server racks instead of houses or parks. Candidates for governor and US Senate who promised to regulate or slow down the data-center industry also won their races that same night, suggesting this wasn't just a handful of angry town meetings but a broader shift in what voters want from their elected officials.

Analysts are calling this a new structural check on the AI industry — meaning not a one-time setback, but a lasting obstacle built into the system, since local governments control zoning and land use, and they're now less willing to say yes. That matters because AI companies had been planning years of expansion assuming they could keep building wherever they needed to. If towns keep voting no, that plan runs into a wall no amount of money can simply buy past.

The financial markets reacted almost immediately, though in a confusing, two-part way. On November 4, once it became clear Democratic candidates — several of whom ran on regulating AI data centers — had won key races, the Dow Jones Industrial Average (an index tracking 30 major US companies, used as a rough gauge of how "the stock market" is doing) jumped by somewhere between 1,500 and 1,600 points, its best day after a midterm election in 44 years. But by the next day, November 5, the mood flipped. The Nasdaq, an index weighted heavily toward tech companies, fell around 5% as investors sold off shares in chip-makers and tech firms, worried that if data centers can't get built, the companies that make and sell the chips inside them will earn less money than expected. Some news outlets estimated the one-day loss in chip-related stock value at roughly $2 trillion — an almost incomprehensible number, equivalent to wiping out an entire country's economy in a single day.

It's worth being honest that nobody has settled on exact numbers for any of this. CNBC, Yahoo Finance, ABC News, and CBS News each reported somewhat different point totals for both the Tuesday rally and the Wednesday crash, because markets move continuously and each outlet filed its numbers at a different moment in a fast-changing story. The broad shape — a huge jump followed by a bigger, more unsettling reversal — is agreed on; the precise figures are not, and probably never will be pinned down to everyone's satisfaction.

Words worth knowing
  • Data center — A large building full of powerful computers that stores information and runs AI systems, using huge amounts of electricity and water to keep running and cool.
  • Ballot measure — A question put directly to voters on an election ballot, letting citizens vote yes or no on a specific law or policy instead of just electing a representative.
  • Structural check — An obstacle built into how a system works — like a law or a level of government with veto power — that creates a lasting limit, rather than a temporary setback.

Two US States Vote on Rules About a Big Medical Choice

On November 4, 2026, people in Virginia and Missouri voted on rules about abortion, which is a medical choice to end a pregnancy. Virginia voters chose to protect that choice for the future. Missouri voters said no to a plan that would have banned it again, just two years after allowing it.

Why this mattersWhen voters get to answer a yes-or-no question directly, instead of just picking a leader, they decide a rule themselves. This matters because rules like these can affect your family, your neighbors, or people you know for a very long time, no matter which state you live in.
Read the whole story

In Virginia, voters added new words to the state's biggest rulebook, called a constitution. The new words say people have a 'fundamental right' to make their own choices about having children. Because it's now part of the constitution, it will be much harder for future lawmakers to take that right away.

In Missouri, something trickier happened. Back in 2024, voters there said abortion should be allowed. This year, some lawmakers asked voters to reverse that and ban it again. The same yes-or-no question also included a ban on certain medical care for transgender kids. Voters said no to the whole thing, so both bans were stopped at once.

These two votes fit a pattern seen across the country. Since 2022, when a major court case called Roe v. Wade was overturned, almost every state that let its people vote directly on abortion chose to keep or expand access to it, even in states that usually elect lawmakers who want strict bans.

The votes happened on the same day as other big elections, when Democrats won more seats in the US Congress. But these abortion questions were separate from that. They were decided straight by voters, not by the politicians they elected.

  • constitution — A place's most important rulebook, which is hard to change and protects people's biggest rights.

Voters in Two States Just Rewrote Their Own Abortion Laws — And Reached Opposite Starting Points

On November 4, 2026, Virginia voters added a right to "reproductive freedom" directly into the state's constitution. The same night, Missouri voters rejected a measure that would have banned abortion again, just two years after they had voted to legalize it. Both results fit a pattern: since the Supreme Court ended the nationwide right to abortion in 2022, voters asked to decide the question directly have mostly chosen to keep or expand access, even in states that lean conservative.

Why this mattersWhether someone can get an abortion, and under what circumstances, now depends heavily on which state they live in — and these votes show that in many places, voters themselves are overriding what their own legislatures tried to do. If you or someone you know ever needs reproductive healthcare, moves states, or has to make decisions about pregnancy, these laws aren't abstract; they determine what's actually possible nearby.
Read the whole story

In Virginia, voters didn't just pass a law — they changed the state's constitution, which is a much bigger deal. An ordinary law can be rewritten by the next legislature that doesn't like it. A constitutional amendment is built to be harder to undo; it usually needs voter approval to change again, not just a vote in the state capitol. By approving the "fundamental right to reproductive freedom" amendment, Virginians made abortion access much more resistant to being reversed by future governors or lawmakers, whatever their party.

Missouri tells a stranger story. In 2024, voters there passed a ballot measure legalizing abortion in a state that had banned it. Just two years later, opponents put a new measure on the ballot trying to reverse that — banning abortion again. But this new measure wasn't only about abortion: it was bundled together with a separate ban on gender-transition medical care for minors, meaning voters had to accept or reject both policies as a single package. They rejected the whole thing, which means both the abortion ban and the care ban for trans minors failed together, even though some voters might have supported one piece and not the other.

This is part of a broader trend. Since the Supreme Court's 2022 decision overturning Roe v. Wade — the 1973 ruling that had guaranteed a nationwide constitutional right to abortion — the question of whether abortion should be legal has gone back to individual states. Several of those states have let voters decide directly through ballot measures rather than leaving it entirely to elected legislatures. In nearly every case so far, including in states that usually vote Republican, voters presented with a direct yes-or-no choice have favored preserving or expanding abortion access rather than restricting it further.

These two votes happened on the same night as the broader 2026 midterm elections, in which control of Congress shifted to Democrats — but the ballot measures themselves were separate questions voted on alongside, not because of, that larger race. People could and did vote differently on the measures than they voted for candidates, which is part of why ballot measures are useful to watch: they isolate what voters think about one specific issue, stripped of party loyalty, campaign ads, or feelings about a particular candidate.

Words worth knowing
  • ballot measure — A question put directly to voters on an election ballot, letting ordinary citizens vote yes or no on a proposed law or constitutional change instead of leaving the decision only to elected lawmakers.
  • Roe v. Wade — A 1973 U.S. Supreme Court ruling that established a nationwide constitutional right to abortion; it was overturned by the Court in 2022, sending the decision back to individual states.
  • constitutional amendment — A formal change to a constitution, usually requiring a higher bar to pass and to later undo than an ordinary law, which is why Virginia's vote is harder to reverse than a typical statute.

  1. Should deeply personal rights issues like abortion be decided directly by voters through ballot measures, rather than by elected legislatures or courts?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes, let voters decide directly

    • It reflects the actual will of the people rather than whatever compromise politicians are willing to risk their careers on.
    • It breaks gridlock when legislatures are too divided, scared, or beholden to donors to act on an issue most people have a clear opinion about.
    • People most affected by a law should have a direct say in it rather than trusting it entirely to representatives who may not share their priorities.

    No, leave it to legislatures and courts

    • Complex rights questions deserve careful deliberation and expert input, not a simple yes-or-no choice on a crowded ballot.
    • Ballot measures can be confusing or bundled with unrelated provisions, making it hard for voters to express what they actually believe.
    • Majorities can vote to restrict rights just as easily as expand them, which is exactly why some rights are meant to be protected from shifting popular opinion, not decided by it.

  2. Is it fair for campaigns to bundle two unrelated policies — like an abortion ban and a ban on gender-transition care for minors — into a single ballot measure voters must accept or reject together?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Bundling is a legitimate strategy

    • It lets voters consider and act on a coherent set of related priorities in one vote rather than requiring dozens of separate measures.
    • Campaigns are allowed to combine issues they see as connected, and voters can still choose to reject the whole package if they disagree.
    • It's an efficient use of a single election to address multiple goals a political movement cares about.

    Bundling unfairly limits real choice

    • It forces voters who support one policy but oppose the other to vote against both, erasing their actual preference.
    • It can be used strategically to smuggle an unpopular policy through by attaching it to a more sympathetic one, or vice versa.
    • Each policy deserves its own clear yes-or-no vote so the outcome genuinely reflects what voters think about that specific issue.
