Four Days, Four Big Computer Breakdowns — And Nobody Knows Why

This week, four important American computer systems broke down, one after another, on four days in a row. First a bank's systems glitched. Then flight-tracking computers at the airport agency had trouble. Then Microsoft had an outage. Then, on the fourth day, both the NSA — a secret spy agency — and Apple had problems at the same time. Each company blamed something boring, like 'routine maintenance,' but nobody outside those companies can check if that's really true.

Why this mattersBanks, airports, phones, and government agencies all run on computers now, the same way your school depends on buses showing up on time. When those systems wobble, it can mean delayed flights, slow banking, or confusing phone glitches for regular people. It's also a reminder that even huge, important organizations sometimes have to say 'we're not totally sure why that broke.'

Picture four different kids, each on four different days, all tripping over the same crack in the sidewalk. That's a little like what happened this week in America's computers.

On Tuesday, two big ones broke at once. The NSA, an agency that handles secret government information, said its internal messaging system went down. Apple said its products were glitching too. Both said it was just planned fixing work — nothing scary.

Before that, a bank called JPMorgan Chase had a computer problem. So did the agency that manages airplane traffic. So did Microsoft, a huge software company. Four different systems, four different days, four different explanations.

People who study computer breakdowns say this isn't actually that unusual — there have been stretches with even more problems before. They also say early checks show no sign that someone attacked all four on purpose. Each failure seems to have its own separate cause, like four accidents that happen to land on the same week by chance.

  • routine maintenance — Routine maintenance means a planned check-up or fix-up of a computer system, similar to a regular dentist visit instead of an emergency one.

Four Days, Four Breakdowns: What's Actually Going On With America's Computer Systems?

Over four straight business days, four of the biggest systems in the United States broke down: Microsoft, the agency that controls air traffic, the bank JPMorgan Chase, and then — on the same day — the National Security Agency's internal communications and Apple's products. Officials say each failure had a boring, unrelated explanation. Investigators agree the failures weren't connected to each other, but plenty of people online aren't convinced.

Why this mattersAlmost everything you rely on without thinking about it — your bank card working, planes landing on schedule, your phone doing what you tell it, spies catching the people they're supposed to catch — depends on enormous, mostly invisible computer systems built and maintained by a small number of organisations. When several of those systems stumble in quick succession, it's a reminder of how much modern life quietly depends on code written and run by people you'll never meet, and how little visibility the public actually has into why things break.

On Tuesday, November 17th, something unusual happened twice in one day: the National Security Agency, the US government body responsible for intercepting communications and protecting national security information, said its internal messaging systems had gone down. Within hours, Apple confirmed that some of its own products were malfunctioning too. It was the fourth consecutive working day that a major American institution had suffered a serious tech failure — Microsoft, the Federal Aviation Administration (which manages the country's airspace), and JPMorgan Chase (one of the largest banks in the world) had each gone down on the three days before.

The explanations offered were almost aggressively ordinary. The NSA called its outage 'scheduled routine maintenance' — the kind of planned, temporary shutdown that happens to update or fix systems, usually announced in advance precisely so it doesn't look alarming. Apple described its glitch as the result of an 'internal update' gone wrong. Both are common, unremarkable things that happen to large tech systems all the time. The problem is that neither claim has been independently verified — no outside expert or journalist has been able to confirm, from evidence, that this is really what caused each outage. That doesn't mean the explanations are false. It means nobody outside those organisations can currently prove they're true.

This matters more than it might sound, because when something affecting national security or a trillion-dollar company breaks down, 'trust us' is a lot to ask, especially right after three other major systems failed in a row. Investigators looking into all four incidents have reportedly found early signs that rule out a coordinated cyberattack — meaning one attacker or group deliberately causing multiple failures as part of a single plan. Each outage, they say, appears to have its own separate root cause: the specific technical fault that actually set the problem in motion, as opposed to the more obvious symptom everyone sees. Four different root causes is a very different story from one hidden hand pulling four different levers.

It's also worth putting the numbers in context. Outage trackers — services that monitor internet and tech failures around the world — note that roughly three major incidents in the past 30 days is actually within the normal range; a typical month sees about five. In other words, by the numbers, this stretch of bad luck isn't even statistically unusual. It only feels alarming because of which systems failed and how close together they landed, not because the underlying rate of tech breakdowns has spiked.

Into that gap between 'feels alarming' and 'statistically normal' stepped a rumour: a claim, repeated widely online, that the real cause behind all this is rushed software patches written by artificial intelligence rather than properly checked by human engineers. It's a plausible-sounding story, and it would explain a lot. But it traces back to a single outlet that isn't well known and whose reporting nobody else has been able to independently confirm. That doesn't automatically make it wrong — but it means, right now, it's a claim resting on one unverified source, not an established fact.

Words worth knowing
  • outage — A period when a system, service, or network stops working properly, whether briefly or for a long time.
  • root cause — The actual underlying problem that triggers a failure, as distinct from the more visible symptom everyone notices first.
  • coordinated cyberattack — A deliberate attack where one person or group targets multiple systems at once as part of a single plan, rather than separate problems happening to occur together.

Nvidia's Boss Is Gone, But the Stock Market Keeps Falling Anyway

Nvidia is a company that makes the computer chips inside artificial intelligence machines. Its boss suddenly quit after serious accusations against him. Nvidia just showed everyone its best report card in a while — but the stock market kept dropping anyway.

Why this mattersNvidia's chips end up inside phones, game consoles, and the AI tools many families already use, so what happens to the company can shape what gadgets look like next. When the stock market falls, it can affect grown-ups' jobs and savings, even for families who never think about Nvidia at all.

Nvidia makes special computer chips. These chips are the brains inside artificial intelligence, or AI — the technology that powers chatbots, phone apps, and smart robots. Nvidia's boss, a man named Jensen Huang, suddenly quit his job.

People say he had to go because of worries about national security — things that could put a whole country at risk. There were also reports that he broke promises to his wife and may have secretly talked with Chinese spies. Nobody has proven these things yet. Nvidia's board — the group of people who make the company's biggest decisions — agreed he had to leave, and said he can never come back.

Right after that, Nvidia showed everyone its numbers for the last few months. The company made even more money than experts expected, and said it would grow even faster soon. Normally that kind of good news makes stock prices go up. Instead, the whole stock market fell for the fifth day in a row — the longest losing streak since June — dropping by about one dollar out of every hundred invested.

At the same time, China said it would fight back if America puts new limits on how Chinese companies can use powerful AI chips from far away. This happened just days before a big meeting between the leaders of the US and China. Some American politicians are now using the Huang story to argue for even stricter rules on selling chips to China.

Nvidia's Boss Is Gone Over a Spy Scandal, Profits Just Smashed Records — and Wall Street Still Dumped the Stock

Nvidia, the company that makes the chips powering most of the world's AI boom, just posted its first earnings report since its CEO Jensen Huang abruptly resigned. The board unanimously accepted his exit and ruled out ever bringing him back, after reports — still unconfirmed — that he had multiple affairs and suspected contact with Chinese intelligence. The company's profits beat every expectation, yet the US stock market kept falling for a fifth straight day.

Why this mattersThe chips at the center of this story are the same ones running the AI tools increasingly built into phones, schoolwork, and jobs — so decisions about who can sell them, and to whom, shape what technology looks like for years. It's also a clear example of how markets don't just react to one company's profits; they react to trust, politics, and uncertainty, which is why a record-breaking earnings report can still coincide with a falling stock market.

Jensen Huang didn't just run Nvidia — he built it from a small chip company into the most valuable firm powering artificial intelligence worldwide, the company whose processors sit inside almost every major AI data center on Earth. So when he resigned suddenly, and Nvidia's board this week confirmed it would never bring him back, it was a bigger deal than a normal corporate exit. The board's statement came attached to the company's first earnings report since his departure — a report that, on paper, could not have gone better.

Nvidia beat Wall Street's profit and revenue estimates for the quarter, and went further: it raised its forecast for fiscal year 2028, now expecting revenue to grow 75% compared to the year before. For a company already worth trillions of dollars, that's an enormous number — it means Nvidia expects demand for its AI chips to keep accelerating for years, not slow down. Normally, numbers like that send a stock soaring.

Instead, US markets kept sliding. The Dow Jones Industrial Average, a widely watched measure of major American companies, dropped 561 points to close at 50,198, while the S&P 500 — a broader index covering 500 large US companies — fell to 7,323. It was the fifth losing day in a row, the longest stretch of market decline since June. The disconnect is the real story here: a company can report its best quarter ever and still get dragged down if investors are worried about something bigger than that one company's numbers.

Part of that worry is Huang himself. He stepped down amid reports — not yet confirmed by any investigator — that he had been involved in multiple extramarital affairs and had suspicious contact with Chinese intelligence operatives. Nvidia sells chips that are central to both the US and Chinese AI industries, and also central to US national security concerns, since advanced AI chips can be used for military and surveillance purposes as well as commercial ones. A scandal involving potential foreign intelligence contact at the top of that company is not just a personal story — it touches directly on how much the US government trusts Nvidia with sensitive technology.

That trust question has already spilled into trade policy. China's Commerce Ministry said this week it would retaliate if Washington goes ahead with new restrictions on remote access to advanced AI chips — rules that would make it harder for Chinese companies to rent computing power from Nvidia chips even if the physical chips never leave American soil. Beijing called the move cause for 'strong dissatisfaction and firm opposition.' This is landing just days before a planned summit between President Trump and President Xi Jinping, raising the stakes on what was already a tense negotiation.

Lawmakers from both American political parties are now citing the Huang affair as a reason to tighten chip export restrictions on China even further, arguing that if a company's own CEO could be compromised, the technology itself needs tighter government control. Critics of that reasoning point out that nothing about the allegations has actually been proven, and that using an unconfirmed personal scandal to justify sweeping trade policy risks punishing an entire industry for one unresolved case.

  • Export curbs — Government rules that limit or block the sale of certain products — here, advanced AI chips — to another country, usually for national security reasons.
  • National security concerns — Worries that something — a person, a technology, or a deal — could threaten a country's safety or strategic advantage, often used to justify tighter government oversight.

Alaska Surprise: A Longtime Republican State Picks a Democrat

Alaska, a huge, cold state in the far north of the United States, just did something it almost never does. Voters picked a Democrat named Mary Peltola to be their U.S. senator instead of the Republican who already had the job. She won more than half the votes, so there was no need for extra rounds of counting.

Why this mattersThe Senate helps decide big things that touch your life, like how much money schools get or how much gets spent on building roads and hospitals. When one party holds a much bigger share of the seats, it can pass its plans faster, with less need to work with the other side. This election also shows that even places known for picking the same team every time can surprise everyone.
Read the whole story

Mary Peltola just won a seat in the United States Senate for the state of Alaska. She beat Dan Sullivan, who already held that job. She got more than half of all the votes, so the election was decided right away, with no extra counting needed.

This is a big deal because Alaska almost always votes Republican, like a kid who always roots for the same team every single game. A Democrat winning there is like that kid suddenly cheering for the rival team.

Alaska is the ninth Senate seat that Democrats have flipped from Republican to Democrat this year. Most of those places had voted for Donald Trump, the Republican president, in the last big election. Now Democrats hold 56 out of 100 seats in the Senate, the biggest lead either party has had in 16 years.

News reporters are calling this run of flips historically unusual. They are still trying to figure out exactly why so many voters in Republican areas switched sides this time.

Democrat Mary Peltola beat Republican Senator Dan Sullivan in Alaska, winning outright with more than half the vote so there was no need for a runoff. The win gives Senate Democrats 56 seats, their biggest majority in 16 years, and marks the ninth Senate seat the party has flipped this midterm cycle — nearly all in states that voted for Trump.

Why this mattersDecisions made by a 56-seat Senate majority — over judges, government spending, and major legislation — will shape rules that affect how the country runs for years, not just this election cycle. Watching how a single state like Alaska can break a decades-long pattern is also a reminder that political loyalties people assume are fixed can move faster than expected.

Alaska has voted Republican for Senate in every election most of its residents can remember. So when NBC News projected that Mary Peltola, a Democrat, had defeated incumbent Senator Dan Sullivan — not narrowly, but by clearing 50% of the vote outright — it registered as more than a single seat changing hands. CBS News independently confirmed the result and the national count it produces: Senate Democrats now hold 56 seats to Republicans' 44.

Alaska uses ranked-choice voting, a system where voters list candidates in order of preference rather than picking just one. If nobody wins more than half the first-choice votes, the last-place candidate is eliminated and their votes get redistributed to whichever candidate those voters ranked next, repeating until someone crosses 50%. That process can take weeks and can produce surprising winners. Peltola skipped all of that by winning a majority on the first count — a cleaner, faster, and more decisive victory than the system usually produces.

This is the ninth Senate seat Democrats have flipped this midterm cycle, following earlier wins in North Carolina, South Carolina, Ohio, Texas, Kansas, Iowa, and Maine. Eight of those nine flips happened in states that voted for Donald Trump in the last presidential election — places where Republicans usually have a built-in advantage. Winning a Senate seat in territory like that is like a visiting team not just beating the home team once, but doing it eight times across different cities in the same season. It's the kind of pattern that makes political analysts sit up, because single upsets happen for local reasons, but a pattern this wide usually points to something bigger shifting underneath.

A 56-seat majority matters practically, not just symbolically. It gives Democrats control of Senate committees, which decide which bills even get a vote, and lets them set the chamber's calendar. It does not give them unlimited power — most legislation still needs 60 votes to overcome a procedural hurdle called the filibuster, meaning Republicans can still block most bills. But a majority this size is the largest either party has held in 16 years, which changes the margin for error on things like confirming judges and federal appointees, many of which require only a simple majority.

News outlets are calling the scale of this wave historically unusual, but they are more confident about the fact of it than about why it happened. Was it about the economy, specific Senate candidates, Trump's approval rating, local issues in each state, or something else entirely? That analysis is still being worked out, and reasonable people reading the same results can draw different conclusions about what voters were actually responding to.

Words worth knowing
  • Ranked-choice voting — A voting system where people rank candidates in order of preference, and if no one wins over half the top-choice votes, the weakest candidates are eliminated one by one until someone does.
  • Incumbent — The person who already holds a political office and is running again to keep it.
  • Senate majority — Holding more than half of the 100 Senate seats, which lets a party control committees and the voting schedule, though big bills often still need 60 votes to pass because of a rule called the filibuster.
