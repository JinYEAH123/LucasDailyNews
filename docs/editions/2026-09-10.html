Ships Get Attacked at Sea, and Gas Gets More Expensive

American ships and Iranian ships have been shooting at each other near the ocean where a lot of the world's oil travels. The U.S. military says it hit five huge oil-carrying ships linked to Iran's military after Iran tried to attack a U.S. warship. This is the second round of fighting like this in just a few days, and it made the price of gas jump higher.

Why this mattersWhen oil and gas cost more, it costs more for trucks to deliver food, for buses to run, and for parents to drive to work or school pickup. Even kids who don't think about gas prices feel it when a family trip costs more or grocery prices creep up. Because this is happening close to an election in the United States, it could also change which leaders people choose to vote for.
Read the whole story

Iran is a country in the Middle East, far from the United States. It has a part of its military called the Revolutionary Guard, which is separate from Iran's regular army and answers directly to Iran's leaders.

First, Iran's Revolutionary Guard fired missiles toward two U.S. Navy warships. A warship is a big armed boat built for fighting, not for carrying passengers or cargo. In response, the U.S. military struck three tankers, which are giant ships that carry oil across the ocean, sometimes as much oil as thousands of swimming pools.

Then Iran tried again, attacking another U.S. warship. This time, the U.S. hit five more tankers linked to Iran's Revolutionary Guard. Iran's government says these attacks broke international rules and called them a war crime, though the United States disagrees and says it was defending itself.

All this fighting happened near waters that carry a lot of the world's oil. Because ships were getting attacked there, oil became harder and scarier to move, so its price shot up. In the U.S., the price of diesel fuel, which trucks and some cars use, hit its highest point ever: $5.94 for one gallon, which is about the size of four large milk jugs.

US and Iran Trade Strikes at Sea, and Oil Prices Jump Toward $100

The US military struck five oil tankers tied to Iran's most powerful armed force this week, right after Iran tried to attack a US Navy warship. It was the second round of strikes in just a few days — Iran had fired missiles at two US ships earlier, and the US hit three other tankers in response. Oil prices spiked sharply afterward, and the effects are already reaching gas pumps and, according to analysts, could reach the ballot box.

Why this mattersWhen tensions escalate between countries thousands of miles away, the cost shows up close to home — in what it costs to fill a car, ship a package, or truck food to a store. This is also a case study in how foreign policy and domestic politics collide: decisions made for military or security reasons can end up shaping elections in ways no one intended.
Read the whole story

The US military's Central Command, the branch overseeing American operations across the Middle East, announced it had struck five oil tankers connected to Iran's Revolutionary Guard Corps — a powerful branch of Iran's armed forces, separate from its regular army, that controls much of the country's oil shipping and backs allied militias across the region. The strike came immediately after Iran attempted its own attack on a US Navy warship in the area.

This was not an isolated incident. Days before, the Revolutionary Guard had launched ballistic missiles — long-range weapons that arc high into the sky before dropping onto a target — toward two American warships. The US answered that attack by striking three Iranian oil tankers. When Iran tried again, the US hit five more.

Iran's foreign ministry, speaking through state media, called the American strikes 'a clear violation of Paragraph 4 of Article 2 of the United Nations Charter' — the founding UN rule that forbids one country from using force against another's territory or independence — and described them as a war crime. It's worth being clear about where that description comes from: it is Iran's government's own characterization, delivered through media it controls, and Washington disputes it, framing its strikes as self-defense against unprovoked attacks on its ships.

Oil markets reacted within hours. Brent crude, the international price benchmark that influences fuel costs worldwide, jumped 3.36% to settle at $101.21 a barrel — its first close above $100 since July and its highest price since May. In the US, diesel fuel, which powers trucks, trains, and farm machinery, hit a record $5.94 a gallon.

Fuel prices don't stay contained to gas stations. They ripple into the cost of shipping, groceries, and anything that travels by truck. Analysts now say this spike could become a real factor in the US midterm elections, the votes held partway through a president's term that decide who controls Congress, since voters historically punish whichever party holds power when gas gets expensive — regardless of what caused it.

  • Brent crude — The international benchmark price for oil that many countries use to set the cost of fuel, so when it rises, prices tend to rise worldwide.
  • midterm elections — US elections held two years into a president's term that decide which party controls Congress, often influenced by economic conditions like gas prices.

Apple's Phone Folds in Half — And Costs More Than Some Cars' Down Payments

Apple just showed off its first-ever folding phone, called the iPhone Duo. It can bend in half like a book, and the fanciest version costs $3,199 — more money than some people pay for a used car. This is also the first big product from Apple's brand-new boss, John Ternus.

Why this mattersEven if you'll never own one, this phone shows how companies compete by making things bigger, fancier, and sometimes shockingly expensive. It also shows that even huge companies like Apple sometimes copy an idea from a rival — just years later, and with a much higher price tag.
Read the whole story

Apple makes iPhones, and for years those phones have all been flat rectangles of glass. Now they've made one that folds shut, just like a wallet or a paperback book, so it's small enough to slip in a pocket but opens up into a bigger screen for watching videos or playing games.

The cheapest iPhone Duo costs $1,999. That's like buying about 400 pizzas. The top model costs $3,199 — reports say that's the most any company has ever charged for a phone that regular people can buy in stores, not just a few rich collectors.

This is a big moment for Apple's new CEO, John Ternus. A CEO is the person in charge of running a whole company and deciding what it makes next. Ternus just took over that job, so this folding phone is like his first big test in front of the whole world.

Other companies, like Samsung, have been making folding phones for years already. So Apple is arriving late to this idea — but Apple often waits and then tries to make its version extra polished. People can start ordering the iPhone Duo on October 16, and it will actually arrive in stores on October 23.

Apple's First Folding iPhone Costs More Than a Used Car Down Payment

Apple has finally released a foldable phone — years after competitors like Samsung already had theirs on shelves. Called the iPhone Duo, it starts at $1,999 and climbs to $3,199 for the top model, which reports say is the most expensive price ever set for a phone made for the general public. It's also the first big product launched under Apple's new CEO, John Ternus, making this as much a test of his leadership as it is of the phone itself.

Why this mattersPhone prices affect nearly everyone who owns one, and a $3,199 price tag from a company as influential as Apple can shift what feels 'normal' to charge across the whole industry. It's also a real-time example of how a new leader's first major decision gets scrutinized as a signal of everything that follows.
Read the whole story

Apple unveiled the iPhone Duo at an event called 'Surprise and Shine,' introducing a phone that bends in half like a small book — a design known as a foldable, where a flexible screen lets the device shrink to pocket size and unfold into something closer to a small tablet. The base version, with 256 gigabytes of storage (roughly enough space for tens of thousands of photos or a few hundred movies), costs $1,999. The top-end version costs $3,199. Preorders open October 16, and the phone arrives in stores a week later, on October 23.

That price tag is the headline detail for a reason. $3,199 is described as a record for a mass-market smartphone — meaning a phone designed and sold for ordinary consumers, not a specialty or limited-run device. Most flagship phones from Apple and its rivals sell somewhere between $800 and $1,300. A foldable at triple that price is a bet that enough people will pay a luxury-car-down-payment sum for a phone that does something genuinely new, rather than just a slightly better version of what they already own.

The timing matters too. Samsung and other manufacturers have sold folding phones for roughly six years, working through early problems like screens that crease or hinges that wear out. Apple, known for entering categories late but polishing them, has now made its move — long after the folding phone stopped being a novelty gadget and became a normal option in phone shops worldwide.

This is also the first major product released under John Ternus, who has taken over as Apple's chief executive officer — the person ultimately responsible for the company's decisions and results. New CEOs are watched closely on their first big launch, because it signals what kind of company they intend to run: cautious and incremental, or bold and willing to charge premium prices for premium technology. A phone this expensive, launched this early in his tenure, sends a clear message about the direction he wants to take.

Whether the bet pays off depends on what happens after October 23. If the iPhone Duo sells well despite the price, it could reshape what customers expect to pay for cutting-edge phones — and put pressure on rivals to push their own prices upward too. If it flops, it becomes an early lesson for Ternus and a sign that even Apple's brand loyalty has limits.

US Plans to Take Away the Two-Month Safety Net for Laid-Off Foreign Workers

Right now, if a skilled worker from another country loses their job in the United States, they get 60 days — almost two months — to find a new job or make a plan before they have to leave. A US government agency just said it wants to erase that rule. If that happens, some workers could have to leave the country almost immediately after being let go.

Why this mattersYou might know a classmate whose parent works in the US on a visa like this. If the rule changes, that family could have to move away suddenly, maybe even in the middle of the school year, with almost no warning. It's a reminder that one decision made far away in an office can flip a family's whole life very fast.
Read the whole story

Imagine your family moved to the United States because your mom or dad got a special work permit called an H-1B visa. It lets skilled workers, like engineers or scientists, take jobs at American companies.

If that job ever ended, there's a rule right now giving families 60 days to find a new job, switch their paperwork, or pack up and go home. That's called a grace period — like extra time after a deadline, similar to a few extra days to return an overdue library book.

On Thursday, an agency called USCIS, which handles visas, said it wants to get rid of that grace period. If the change happens, workers could be required to leave right away after losing a job, with no two months to sort things out.

This could affect many thousands of workers and their families. So far, only one newspaper, The Washington Post, has reported the plan, so it isn't confirmed yet by other news outlets.

  • grace period — Extra time allowed after a deadline before you must actually finish something, like a few extra days to return a library book without a fine.

U.S. Considers Ending 60-Day Safety Net for Laid-Off Foreign Tech Workers

The U.S. agency that handles immigration paperwork says it wants to scrap a rule that currently gives skilled foreign workers, including many H-1B visa holders, 60 days to stay in the country after losing a job. Under the proposed change, these workers would have to leave almost immediately instead of having two months to find a new employer willing to sponsor them. The announcement came from U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) on Thursday, and so far only The Washington Post has reported the details, so the exact scope and timing are still unconfirmed elsewhere.

Why this mattersThis is about how much room the government gives people to land on their feet when something outside their control — being laid off — happens to intersect with immigration status. If you know someone on a work visa, or if your own family has ever depended on a job to keep a legal status intact, the gap between 60 days and zero days is the gap between a plan and a crisis.
Read the whole story

An H-1B visa lets American companies hire skilled foreign workers — often software engineers, doctors, or scientists — for jobs that require specialized training. The visa is tied to a specific employer, which means if that company lets you go, your legal right to stay in the U.S. is suddenly in question too. Since 2017, a rule has given these workers a 60-day grace period: enough time to line up a new sponsoring employer, switch to a different visa category, or pack up and leave in an orderly way.

USCIS, the federal agency that processes visas and green cards, announced Thursday that it intends to eliminate that grace period. If the change goes through, a worker who loses their job would be expected to leave the United States almost immediately rather than having two months to sort things out. For someone with a mortgage, a spouse who also works, or kids enrolled in a local school, that is the difference between a manageable transition and an emergency.

This is not law yet. Federal agencies generally have to publish a formal proposal, take public comments, and then finalize a rule before it takes effect — a process that can take months and sometimes gets challenged in court. But the announcement itself signals where the administration wants to go: making it harder for laid-off visa holders to stay and search for another U.S. job, and easier for the government to require quick departures.

The people most affected would be a fairly specific group — skilled visa holders, heavily concentrated in the tech industry, along with their spouses and children who often hold dependent visas tied to the main worker's status. Tech layoffs have been frequent over the past two years, so the population who could be caught by a sudden job loss and a tight new deadline is not small.

It's worth being upfront about the sourcing here: as of this writing, only The Washington Post has reported this story in detail, and no other outlet has independently confirmed the specifics. That doesn't make it untrue, but it means the exact wording of the proposal, its timeline, and any exceptions are worth watching for when other reporting or the official government filing appears.

Words worth knowing
  • H-1B visa — A U.S. visa that lets companies temporarily employ foreign workers in skilled jobs, but ties the worker's legal status to that specific employer.
  • Grace period — A set amount of extra time — here, 60 days — that a rule allows someone to fix a problem, like finding a new job, before facing a stricter consequence.
  • USCIS — U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services, the federal agency that reviews visa applications and writes many of the detailed rules for how immigration works.

  1. Should a person's right to stay in a country depend on keeping a specific job?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes, tie status to employment

    • Work visas exist to fill specific jobs employers say they can't fill locally, so the visa logically should track that job.
    • Without some link to employment, it becomes harder to tell who is actually here to work versus who has overstayed their original purpose.
    • Governments have limited resources to track immigration status, and tying it to a verifiable employer relationship simplifies enforcement.

    No, allow more flexibility

    • A layoff is rarely the worker's fault, and punishing them with an immediate deadline treats an economic event as if it were a legal violation.
    • People build lives — homes, schools, marriages — around long-term jobs, and rigid status rules ignore that reality.
    • Skilled workers who are forced to leave the moment they're laid off may simply take their skills to a competitor country instead, which doesn't obviously serve the original employer's interests either.

  2. Does protecting these workers' transition time help or hurt American workers competing for the same jobs?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    It hurts American workers

    • Every month a foreign worker stays searching for a new sponsor is a month a domestic candidate isn't hired for that role.
    • A shorter grace period pushes companies to prioritize hiring workers who don't require sponsorship at all, which could favor citizens and permanent residents.
    • Removing the grace period matches the stated goal of some immigration policy: reducing the number of foreign workers competing in the labor market at any given time.

    It doesn't meaningfully affect American workers

    • H-1B workers are typically hired for specific technical roles employers already struggled to fill domestically, so removing them doesn't automatically open jobs to others.
    • Forcing skilled workers out quickly can disrupt ongoing projects and teams, which can cost American colleagues their own jobs or bonuses tied to project completion.
    • Companies that lose experienced staff abruptly may respond by moving whole teams or projects overseas, which removes jobs from the U.S. entirely rather than freeing them up for American hires.
