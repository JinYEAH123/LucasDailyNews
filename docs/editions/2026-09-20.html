Ukraine Fires Over 1,000 Drones at Russia in Its Biggest Strike of the War

Overnight into Sunday, Ukraine launched more than 1,000 drones into Russia — the largest single drone attack since the full-scale invasion began. Hundreds were aimed at the Moscow region, hitting oil facilities on the edge of the Russian capital. The timing, on the final day of Russia's regional elections, looked anything but accidental.

Why this mattersA war that has felt frozen in place for a long time just showed it can still escalate sharply, and oil infrastructure near a capital city is the kind of target that affects a country's economy, not just its front line. How Russia responds — and whether other governments read this as Ukraine gaining strength or taking a dangerous risk — will shape how the next phase of the war unfolds.
Read the whole story

For most of this war, Ukraine's drone strikes inside Russia have hit border regions or military targets far from the capital. This attack was different in scale and in address. Officials in Kyiv called it their largest drone operation of the war, and hundreds of the more than 1,000 drones launched were sent specifically toward Moscow and its surrounding region — including its oil infrastructure, the network of refineries, storage depots, and pipelines that keeps fuel flowing and generates export revenue Russia uses to fund the war.

The timing mattered as much as the scale. The strikes landed on the third and final day of Russia's parliamentary elections — the vote that fills seats in the country's legislature. Independent observers and many governments consider Russian elections tightly controlled rather than genuinely competitive, but they still function as a piece of political theatre the Kremlin uses to project stability and public support. Hitting Moscow itself, rather than a distant border town, on the last day of that vote reads to many analysts as a deliberate message: that the war can reach the capital regardless of what the ballot says.

Andrey Vorobiev, the governor of the Moscow region, said three people were killed — two in the strikes themselves and a third who died later in hospital — with twenty others injured, including three children. It's worth being clear about where that information comes from: casualty figures and damage reports are coming from Russian regional officials, and there is no way for outside journalists or investigators to independently verify them inside Russia, where reporting is heavily restricted. Ukraine hasn't challenged these numbers, but it hasn't confirmed them either — which is common in this war, where each side's account of the other's territory is filtered through the reporting government itself.

What makes this attack notable isn't just its size but its direction. For years, this war has largely moved in small increments — villages and front lines shifting by kilometres, with occasional high-profile strikes on military or energy targets. A drone barrage numbering in the thousands, deliberately aimed at the region surrounding the seat of Russian government, is a different order of thing. It signals that Ukraine's drone-manufacturing capacity has grown enough to sustain an attack of this size, and that it's willing to use that capacity to strike where it will be felt most — economically, symbolically, and politically.

Russia has not yet announced a major retaliatory strike in response as of Sunday, though its own drone and missile attacks on Ukrainian cities have been a near-nightly feature of this war for years. Whether this attack changes anything about how the war is fought — whether it invites a matching escalation, or whether it becomes one more strike in a long list — will likely become clearer in the days that follow.

Words worth knowing
  • Drone — An unmanned aircraft, often small and cheap compared to a traditional warplane, that can be flown remotely or guided automatically to strike a target.
  • Oil infrastructure — The physical system of refineries, pipelines, and storage sites that turns crude oil into usable fuel and moves it to where it's sold or used.
  • Independent verification — Confirmation of a claim by someone with no stake in the outcome, such as an outside journalist or investigator — something that is very hard to do inside a country that restricts press access.

Trump Wants a New 'AI Force' to Win the Robot-Brain Race

On Saturday, President Trump said he wants to build a brand-new government group called the 'AI Force.' It would be built like the Space Force, but instead of watching over rockets and satellites, it would watch over artificial intelligence — smart computer programs that can write, talk, and make decisions. He also said he will soon pick one leader, an 'AI czar,' to run it.

Why this mattersAI is already inside things you might use, like games, homework helpers, and voice assistants on phones. Decisions made now about racing ahead or slowing down for safety will shape what kind of AI you grow up using — and how safe or risky it turns out to be.
Read the whole story

Artificial intelligence, or AI, means computer programs that can do things that used to need a human brain — like writing an essay, drawing a picture, or driving a car. Trump wants a whole new part of the government just to handle AI, similar to how the Space Force is the part of the military that handles space.

He said he will pick one person, called a 'czar,' to be in charge of this new AI Force. Right now, there is no clear plan yet for how much money it would cost or whether Congress — the group of lawmakers who make big decisions — even needs to approve it.

Some scientists and lawmakers wanted something different. They have been asking for strict safety rules, sometimes called 'guardrails,' to make sure AI doesn't get built too fast or used in dangerous ways. Trump's plan skips those rules for now and focuses on speed instead.

Trump says the real danger is falling behind China, a country racing to build powerful AI too. Supporters agree that strict rules could slow America down. Critics worry that skipping safety checks while building a military-style AI group could make mistakes more likely and harder to fix.

Words worth knowing
  • AI czar — A single person picked by the government to be in charge of a big project — here, all of America's artificial intelligence plans.
  • guardrails — Rules put in place to stop something powerful, like AI, from being used in unsafe or harmful ways.

Trump Wants an 'AI Force' — But Skips the Safety Rules Many Experts Wanted First

On Saturday, President Trump announced plans to create an 'AI Force,' a new government body modeled on the US Space Force, to oversee artificial intelligence in the United States. He said he will soon appoint an 'AI czar' to run it. The plan focuses on winning the AI race against China rather than putting new safety rules in place — which is exactly what many lawmakers and researchers had been asking for.

Why this mattersAI systems already help decide what ads you see, what content gets recommended to you, and increasingly what jobs exist — decisions about how fast and how carefully that technology grows will shape the internet, the job market, and even how wars are fought during your lifetime. Whether the government treats AI mainly as a race to win or a risk to manage will affect you long before any 'AI Force' officially exists.
Read the whole story

Imagine your school announced a brand-new sports team before deciding who's allowed to play, what the rules are, or who's paying for the uniforms. That's roughly where the 'AI Force' stands right now. Trump described it as a dedicated arm of government focused on artificial intelligence — the technology behind chatbots, image generators, and systems that can write code, plan strategies, or eventually control physical robots. He compared it to the Space Force, the branch of the US military created in 2019 to handle satellites and space operations, suggesting AI deserves the same kind of dedicated, elite status.

There's a catch, though: nobody has said how this would actually work. There's no bill in Congress laying out what powers the AI Force would have, no budget attached to it, and no clarity on whether lawmakers even need to approve it before it exists. An 'AI czar' — a term Washington uses for a single official put in charge of a big, messy problem, without needing a whole new department — hasn't been named yet either. So right now, the AI Force is more of an announcement than an actual plan.

What makes this notable isn't just the announcement — it's what it skips. For the past couple of years, a mix of members of Congress from both parties and AI safety researchers (scientists and engineers who study how advanced AI systems could cause harm, deliberately or by accident) have been pushing for guardrails: binding rules that would require companies to test their AI systems for dangerous capabilities, disclose risks, or slow down before releasing something that could be misused — for spreading disinformation, running cyberattacks, or even helping someone design a weapon. Trump's plan doesn't include any of that. Instead, it frames AI mainly as a race to win, with China cast as the competitor the US can't afford to fall behind.

That framing is the real story here. Two people can look at the exact same technology and reach opposite conclusions about what matters most. One view says AI is like a new industrial revolution — whoever builds the most powerful systems fastest gets enormous economic and military advantages, and hesitating just hands that advantage to a rival country. The other view says AI is being deployed faster than anyone fully understands it, and setting up impressive-sounding government structures without safety requirements is like building a faster car without also building the brakes.

Supporters of Trump's approach argue that regulation is a trap: strict rules might sound responsible, but they mainly slow down American companies while giving competitors elsewhere a freer hand to catch up or overtake. Critics counter that an AI Force with no safety mandate isn't really about caution at all — it's a military-flavored branding exercise that could speed up risky deployment rather than manage it. Both sides agree AI is important enough to need serious government attention. They disagree completely on what 'serious attention' should look like.

Words worth knowing
  • AI czar — A single government official put in charge of handling a big, complicated issue — in this case, AI policy — without necessarily creating a whole new agency.
  • guardrails — Rules or requirements meant to catch dangerous behavior early, such as forcing companies to test AI systems for risks before releasing them.
  • AI safety researchers — Scientists and engineers who study how advanced AI systems might cause harm, whether by mistake, misuse, or unpredictable behavior, and look for ways to prevent it.

In Germany, a Once-Outsider Party Wins Big While the Chancellor's Own Party Shrinks

This weekend, people in one part of Germany voted for new local leaders. A party called AfD, which many people call far-right, won more votes there than ever before. The party of Germany's leader got hardly any votes at all. The leader called the result a 'disaster' but said he would keep going with his plans anyway.

Why this mattersThink about a group project where one kid keeps changing the rules and the others refuse to work with them anymore — that gets harder to manage the bigger the group gets. Germany is one of the biggest makers of cars and machines in the world, so if its government struggles to agree on anything, it can affect jobs and prices far beyond Germany, maybe even things your family buys.
Read the whole story

Germany is a country in Europe that makes huge amounts of cars, machines, and tools that get sold all over the world. This weekend, people in one German region called Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania, a state by the Baltic Sea, voted to choose their local government.

Imagine a class of 100 kids voting. About 37 or 38 of them picked AfD, a party many call far-right because it wants very strict rules about who is allowed to move to Germany. About 35 or 36 picked SPD, a party that focuses on helping workers and people with less money. Only about 5 or 6 picked CDU, the party of Germany's leader, Chancellor Friedrich Merz. A chancellor is like a prime minister, the person in charge of running the whole country.

Merz said the result was a 'disaster' for his party, but he said he would keep pushing ahead with his plans anyway. Other big parties in Germany have said, again and again, that they will not team up with AfD to run governments, even as AfD keeps winning more votes.

This matters beyond just one German state. Germany runs the biggest economy in the European Union, a group of countries that work together, so people in many places watch what happens there closely.

  • Far-right — A word people use for political parties that want very strict, traditional rules, especially about who is allowed to move into a country.

Germany's Far-Right AfD Surges in State Vote as Chancellor's Party Collapses to 5.5%

In a state election in northeastern Germany over the weekend, the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) party won roughly 37 to 38% of the vote, according to exit polls that are still being finalized. Chancellor Friedrich Merz's own conservative party, the CDU, crashed to just 5.5% in the same state. Merz called it a 'disaster' for his party but said he would keep pushing his national reform plans anyway.

Why this mattersGermany runs the EU's biggest economy, so shifts in its politics can move interest rates, trade rules, and immigration policy across all 27 member countries, not just within its own borders. The standoff over whether to ever include a rising far-right party in government is also a live experiment in how democracies handle parties that win real votes but that other parties consider dangerous to democracy itself — a tension playing out in other countries too, including debates closer to home.
Read the whole story

Voters in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania, a mostly rural state on Germany's Baltic coast, went to the polls this weekend, and the early results stunned observers even in a country that has watched the far right rise for years. Exit polls put the AfD's share of the vote at around 37 to 38%, with the center-left SPD close behind at roughly 35.5%. Chancellor Friedrich Merz's CDU, meanwhile, collapsed to just 5.5%, a number so low it barely registers as a serious force in that state at all. Different outlets are reporting slightly different figures as votes continue to be counted and finalized, but the broad shape of the result is not in dispute.

To understand why this matters beyond one state, it helps to know who these players are. Friedrich Merz is Germany's chancellor, the country's head of government, and leader of the CDU, the main center-right party that has governed Germany for most of its post-war history. The SPD is the center-left party that has traditionally been the CDU's biggest rival. The AfD, founded in 2013, started as a party skeptical of the euro currency but has moved sharply toward nationalist and anti-immigration politics since then, and parts of it have been formally classified as extremist by German domestic intelligence services in some states.

That classification is central to why this result is such a shock. Since the AfD's rise began, every other major German party has maintained what is often called a 'firewall' — an informal but firm agreement to never form a coalition government with the AfD, no matter how many votes it wins. A coalition government is simply an alliance between two or more parties that together hold enough seats to govern, something common in Germany's multi-party system, where a single party rarely wins an outright majority. The firewall exists because mainstream politicians view the AfD as a threat to Germany's democratic order, given the country's history with the Nazi regime in the 20th century. But that same firewall means that even as the AfD wins more and more votes, it remains locked out of actually governing anywhere at the state or national level, which is a strange and increasingly tense situation for a democracy to sit in.

The immediate practical result in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania will likely be that the SPD and other smaller parties cobble together a coalition that excludes the AfD entirely, even though the AfD won the most votes. But the deeper story is what this signals for the rest of Germany. Merz leads a national coalition government, and his CDU's near-total collapse in this state — a party that used to be a serious contender nearly everywhere in Germany — raises real questions about how much support his reform agenda, which includes changes to immigration rules and economic policy, actually commands from ordinary voters. Merz insists he will press ahead regardless, but a chancellor whose own party is polling at 5.5% in a state contest is governing from a much weaker position than the numbers alone might suggest.

Because Germany is the largest economy in the European Union, political instability there tends to ripple outward, affecting everything from EU budget negotiations to the currency markets that price the euro. Investors and other European governments watch German elections closely for exactly this reason: a country this large wobbling politically is different from a smaller nation doing the same. Whether this weekend's result is a one-off protest specific to one struggling region or the leading edge of a genuine national shift is something even experienced political analysts in Germany are currently arguing about.

Words worth knowing
  • exit poll — A survey of voters taken just after they leave the voting booth, used to estimate results before the official count is finished — usually close, but not always exactly right.
  • firewall (Brandmauer) — An informal agreement among Germany's mainstream parties to never form a governing alliance with the far-right AfD, no matter how many votes it wins.
  • coalition government — An alliance between two or more political parties that combine their seats to reach a majority and govern together, common in countries where one party rarely wins outright.
