The U.S. Just Made a Fuel Deal With Russia — Even Though Russia Is Still at War

President Trump has agreed to buy diesel fuel, the kind that powers trucks and buses, from Russia. This is a big change, because for years the U.S. had rules in place to punish Russia for invading its neighbor, Ukraine. The very next day, a Russian attack on a Ukrainian city killed 20 people, including three children.

Why this mattersWhen fuel is cheaper, it can cost less to fill up a car or heat a home — something families everywhere notice. But this story shows that the things leaders do to save people money can also affect wars happening far away, and that one choice can mean very different things depending on whose side you're standing on.

Russia is a huge country that invaded its smaller neighbor Ukraine almost four years ago, starting a war that is still going on. To pressure Russia to stop, the United States and other countries put 'sanctions' on it — rules that block trading certain things with Russia.

President Trump just made a deal to break that pattern. He agreed to buy diesel fuel from Russia. The goal is to bring down fuel prices in America before important elections this year. But buying fuel from Russia sends it money, even while the war continues.

One day after the deal was announced, Russia launched an attack on the Ukrainian city of Zaporizhzhia. Twenty people were killed, including three children. Russia's leader, Vladimir Putin, told Trump that restarting peace talks right now is 'unlikely.' He pointed to Ukrainian drone attacks on Moscow that happened during Russia's own elections last month.

Ukraine and Kaja Kallas, a top official who speaks for the European Union, see it differently. They warn that easing the rules on Russia doesn't bring peace closer — it just gives Russia more money to keep fighting.

Words worth knowing
  • Sanctions — Rules one country makes to stop trading certain things with another country, usually as a punishment or a pressure tactic.
  • Peace talks — Meetings where countries that are fighting try to agree on ways to stop the fighting.

Trump Makes a Fuel Deal With Russia — Just as Peace Talks Stall

President Trump has announced a deal to buy diesel fuel from Russia, undoing years of U.S. pressure meant to punish Moscow for invading Ukraine. The announcement landed the same week Russia struck the Ukrainian city of Zaporizhzhia, killing 20 people, and Vladimir Putin told Trump he wasn't ready to restart peace talks.

Why this mattersFuel prices shape everyday costs — bus fares, delivery charges, what it costs to fill up a car — and they're exactly the kind of issue that can swing an election, which is part of why this deal was made now. But it's also a live example of a bigger pattern: governments constantly trade off values against immediate costs, and how the U.S. handles Russia will shape how seriously sanctions are taken the next time any country invades its neighbor.
Read the whole story

For more than three years, the United States has tried to squeeze Russia's economy as punishment for invading Ukraine, mainly through sanctions — laws that block a country, its companies, or its leaders from doing business with the outside world. Oil and fuel sales are Russia's biggest source of income, so cutting off those sales was meant to drain the money funding its war. Trump's new deal does the opposite: it opens the door for the U.S. to buy diesel fuel from Russia again, specifically to bring down fuel prices at home.

The timing matters. The deal comes just before the U.S. midterm elections, when every seat in the House of Representatives and a third of the Senate is up for a vote. High gas and diesel prices are the kind of thing voters notice immediately — at the pump, on receipts, in the cost of everything trucked to a store — and an incumbent president's party is often judged on exactly that kind of daily discomfort. Easing sanctions to bring fuel prices down is, among other things, a political move.

The reversal did not happen in a vacuum. One day after Trump's announcement, a Russian strike hit Zaporizhzhia, a city in southern Ukraine, killing 20 people, including three children. Strikes like this have continued through nearly every stage of ceasefire diplomacy over the past year, which is part of why critics see the fuel deal as poorly timed at best — a reward handed to Russia in the same week it killed civilians.

Putin, for his part, told Trump that restarting three-way peace talks between the U.S., Russia, and Ukraine was 'unlikely' any time soon. His stated reason was Ukrainian drone attacks on Moscow last month, which happened during Russia's parliamentary elections — elections that decide the makeup of Russia's own legislature, though with far less real competition than American elections, since most opposition to Putin is banned or jailed. Ukraine and its allies read Putin's excuse differently: they say Russia simply isn't under enough pressure to negotiate seriously, and now has one less reason to feel pressure at all.

Kaja Kallas, the European Union's top diplomat on foreign affairs, put that argument directly: easing sanctions now, she warned, doesn't bring peace closer — it funds the war that's delaying peace. Ukraine's government has made similar warnings. The disagreement isn't really about the facts of the deal; it's about what kind of signal it sends, and whether engaging with Russia's economy makes Moscow more likely to compromise, or simply removes one more reason for it to.

Words worth knowing
  • Sanctions — Penalties, like blocking trade or banking access, that one country imposes on another to pressure it into changing its behavior.
  • Midterm elections — U.S. elections held halfway through a president's term, deciding every seat in the House of Representatives and about a third of the Senate.
  • Trilateral talks — Negotiations involving three parties at once — in this case, the United States, Russia, and Ukraine, meeting together rather than in pairs.

An 85-Year-Old Judge Wins the World's Biggest Peace Prize

In Oslo, a city in Norway, judges announced the winner of the Nobel Peace Prize, one of the most famous awards on Earth. They gave it to Navi Pillay, an 85-year-old woman from South Africa, for a lifetime spent making international law protect people during wars and terrible crimes.

Why this mattersThis story shows that rules about fairness do not stop at a country's border; courts can step in even when a government will not protect its own people. It also shows that one person who keeps pushing, even when the rules are stacked against them, can change what counts as fair for the entire world.
Read the whole story

Navi Pillay grew up in South Africa at a time when unfair laws, called apartheid, said people with dark skin could not have the same rights as white people. In 1967, she became the very first woman to open her own law office in Natal, a part of South Africa, even though the rules made that extremely hard for her.

Later, she became a judge on special international courts. These are courts that decide whether soldiers or countries broke the biggest rules the whole world agreed to follow during wars. She was a judge for a court dealing with a genocide in Rwanda, a country in Africa, and later a judge on the International Criminal Court, a court for the whole world.

One of her most important rulings helped the world agree that rape used as a weapon during war is a war crime, serious enough for international courts to punish. After that, she led the United Nations' human rights office, speaking up for people treated unfairly in many countries.

Now, at 85 years old, she has won the Nobel Peace Prize for this long life of work.

Words worth knowing
  • war crime — A war crime is one of the worst things a person can do during a war, like hurting people who are not fighting, and it is serious enough for international courts to punish.
  • genocide — Genocide means trying to destroy a whole group of people because of who they are, such as their race or religion, and it is considered one of the worst crimes in the world.

At 85, a South African judge who redefined war crimes wins the Nobel Peace Prize

Navi Pillay, an 85-year-old South African judge, has been awarded the 2026 Nobel Peace Prize in Oslo, Norway. She spent decades building the legal case that rape used in war and genocide is itself a war crime — a principle that did not clearly exist in international law before judges like her argued it into being.

Why this mattersThe idea that sexual violence in war can be formally prosecuted as genocide or a war crime — rather than dismissed as an unfortunate byproduct of conflict — shapes how today's wars, from Ukraine to Sudan, get documented and eventually judged. It's also a reminder that the rules protecting people worldwide, including in places far from any battlefield, were built recently and by name, not handed down from nowhere.
Read the whole story

In 1967, a young Tamil woman named Navi Pillay became the first woman to open her own law practice in Natal, a province of South Africa that was then enforcing apartheid — a legal system that separated and ranked people by race, with white South Africans on top and everyone else, including Black and Indian South Africans, denied basic rights. Opening a law office as a woman of colour in that system was not just unusual; it was an act of defiance against a state built to keep her out of rooms like that one.

She used that law career to keep climbing into rooms most people never reach. She became a judge on South Africa's High Court after apartheid ended, then a judge on the International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda — a United Nations court set up after the 1994 genocide in Rwanda, in which roughly 800,000 people, mostly from the Tutsi ethnic group, were killed in about a hundred days. From there she went on to judge at the International Criminal Court, a permanent court in The Hague, Netherlands, that prosecutes individuals for genocide, war crimes, and crimes against humanity when national courts won't or can't. Later she served as the United Nations High Commissioner for Human Rights, the UN's top official for investigating and speaking out about human rights abuses worldwide.

The Nobel committee's citation points specifically to her role in rulings that recognised rape as a weapon of war and a tool of genocide under international law. Before judges like Pillay pushed this through the courts, mass sexual violence in conflict was often treated by militaries, governments, and even international bodies as a regrettable side effect of war rather than as a deliberate, prosecutable crime — something that happened in war rather than something done as war. Her tribunal's rulings helped establish that when rape is used systematically to terrorise, displace, or destroy a group, it can meet the legal definition of genocide or a war crime, meaning individuals can be charged and imprisoned for ordering or committing it.

That shift mattered beyond the courtroom. Once a legal definition exists, prosecutors elsewhere can use it, survivors can point to it, and governments negotiating peace deals have to reckon with it. International law moves slowly and unevenly — plenty of perpetrators named in such rulings have never been arrested, and powerful countries routinely ignore courts like the ICC when it suits them — but the legal definitions themselves don't disappear once written. They sit there, available, for the next prosecutor or the next generation of survivors.

Pillay, now 85, is one of the last living judges from that first generation of international tribunals that tried to answer a hard question after the Cold War ended: can law actually hold people accountable for mass atrocity, or is it only ever a court for the losers of wars? Her career doesn't settle that question, but it is the strongest answer anyone has offered — a woman barred from practising freely under her own country's laws who ended up writing some of the rules the rest of the world now has to argue about.

Words worth knowing
  • Apartheid — A legal system in South Africa, lasting from 1948 to the early 1990s, that separated people by race and gave white South Africans far greater rights and power than everyone else.
  • International Criminal Court (ICC) — A permanent court based in The Hague, Netherlands, that prosecutes individuals — not countries — for genocide, war crimes, and crimes against humanity when their home country won't do it.
  • Genocide — The deliberate attempt to destroy a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group, in whole or in part, which international law treats as the most severe crime a person can be charged with.

  1. Should the Nobel Peace Prize honour a lifetime of legal work, or go only to people actively stopping violence happening right now?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Honour the legal legacy

    • Laws that outlast any single peace deal protect far more people over time than one ceasefire does.

  2. Should international courts be able to prosecute a country's leaders for war crimes even if that country never agreed to the court's authority?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes, law should override sovereignty here

    • Genocide and mass rape are crimes against humanity as a whole, not just against one nation, so no government should get to shield itself by simply refusing to join a court.
    • Without outside enforcement, leaders who control their own national courts can guarantee themselves impunity.
    • Survivors of atrocities deserve a legal avenue even when their own government is the one responsible.

    No, sovereignty has to come first

    • A court with no vote from a country's people or government is ruling over them without consent, which undercuts the basic idea of self-governance.
    • In practice, only weaker countries end up prosecuted, while powerful states that never joined the ICC act with impunity, which makes the system look selective rather than universal.
    • International courts can be, or appear to be, shaped by the political interests of whichever countries fund and run them, undermining trust in their neutrality.

China Unlocks a Giant Pile of Saved-Up Borrowing Money

China's economy has been growing more slowly than its leaders want. So the government just told local areas across the country: you can now spend money you were allowed to borrow years ago but never used. It adds up to about $82 billion — and it's meant to get builders building and shops selling again.

Why this mattersLots of the things in your house, from sneakers to electronics, are made in factories in China, so how well China's economy is doing can change prices and jobs far beyond its borders. When a government spends money on roads, bridges, and internet lines, it can mean more jobs for construction workers now and better connections for people later. But some worry about what happens when all that borrowed money eventually needs to be paid back.
Read the whole story

China's economy sells things to the whole world, like phones, clothes, and toys. Lately, fewer people inside China are buying things, and fewer countries are buying from China too. House building, which used to be a huge part of the economy, has also slowed way down.

So China's government has a plan. Every year, local governments (like states or provinces) are allowed to borrow a certain amount of money to build things. Some of them never used up their full borrowing allowance in past years. Now the national government is saying: use it. All of it, right away.

That unused allowance adds up to 550 billion yuan, about $82 billion. If you were handed one dollar every single second, day and night, it would take you more than 2,500 years to count that high. On top of that, another $35 billion will go specifically to big building projects, things like roads, power lines, and internet cables, grouped under a plan called the 'Six Networks.'

People who study China's economy say this isn't a huge new surprise package. It's more like the government doing just enough to hit its growth target for the year, rather than a massive new effort.

Words worth knowing
  • stimulus — Stimulus is extra money a government spends or lets people borrow, to wake up an economy that's growing too slowly.
  • bond quota — A bond quota is like a borrowing allowance: the top amount of money a government is allowed to borrow by selling special IOUs called bonds.

China Frees Up $82 Billion in Old Budgets to Keep Its Economy on Target

China's Ministry of Finance just told provincial governments they can spend $82 billion in borrowing room that was approved years ago but never used. It's part of a string of moves meant to keep the country's economy growing at the pace Beijing promised, even as a housing slump and weak spending at home drag on growth.

Why this mattersChina is the world's second-largest economy and a major trading partner for countries everywhere, including the US, so slower Chinese growth can mean fewer orders for factories worldwide and cheaper exports flooding other markets. For a teenager, this might feel distant, but it touches things like the price of electronics, the job market in industries tied to global trade, and even how affordable things like phones or cars are down the line.
Read the whole story

Imagine your local government was given permission years ago to borrow a certain amount of money for roads, bridges, or schools, but never actually used all of it. That unused borrowing power doesn't just disappear — and China's central government has just told its provinces to go ahead and spend it. The total being unlocked is 550 billion yuan, or about $82 billion, a figure roughly the size of Ecuador's entire economy.

On top of that, Beijing is handing out another 250 billion yuan in fresh borrowing permission specifically for the last three months of the year, aimed at regions that can show they have real projects ready to fund. Much of this money is meant to feed into something China calls the 'Six Networks' — a set of major infrastructure programs covering things like transportation, energy, and water systems, built partly to create jobs and partly to give private companies more reason to invest, since big public projects often need parts, materials, and services from private businesses.

Why now? China's property market has been stuck in a slump for years — developers over-borrowed, building boomed past what buyers wanted, and now prices and sales are weak, which matters enormously because property and related industries have historically made up a huge chunk of China's economic activity and of ordinary households' savings, since many Chinese families hold most of their wealth in apartments. At the same time, Chinese consumers are holding back on spending rather than loosening their wallets, worried about job security and falling home values. Together, these two problems have made it harder for China to hit the growth rate its leaders set as an official target for the year.

Analysts quoted in regional financial press describe this move as Beijing doing 'just enough' — not a dramatic rescue package, but a calculated top-up designed to nudge growth numbers toward the target without a bigger, more expensive stimulus that might worry investors about debt levels. It's a bit like a student who's behind on a semester's reading doing just enough extra study to pass the exam, rather than overhauling their entire study habits.

This isn't a one-off. It's one of several targeted measures China has introduced this year as exports face pressure from trade tensions and tariffs abroad, and domestic demand stays soft. The pattern suggests Chinese policymakers are trying to manage the economy carefully and incrementally, rather than letting it either overheat with a flood of new spending or fall short of expectations by doing nothing.

Words worth knowing
  • Bond quota — A limit set by a central government on how much money local or regional governments are allowed to borrow by selling bonds, which are basically IOUs sold to investors.
  • Stimulus — Extra government spending or financial support designed to boost economic activity when growth is slowing down.
  • GDP growth target — An official goal set by a government for how much its economy — measured by the total value of everything produced, called Gross Domestic Product — should grow in a year.

    Give it to people

    • People know best what they personally need, whether that's food, clothes, or school supplies.
    • Money in people's pockets gets spent right away at local shops, helping small businesses directly.

  1. Should governments borrow and spend extra money just to hit a growth target they set for themselves?

    Yes, meet the target

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Small and targeted is smarter

    • Spending only where there's a clear, ready project avoids wasting money on plans that aren't actually prepared to use it.
    • Keeping stimulus modest avoids spooking investors who worry that huge spending means huge future debt problems.
    • It allows policymakers to adjust gradually as they see how the economy responds, rather than committing to one big irreversible move.

    Bigger stimulus works better

    • Small, incremental measures might not be enough to actually turn around deep problems like a years-long property slump.
    • A bigger package can restore consumer and business confidence faster, since people see decisive action rather than a slow trickle.
    • Dragging out a fix with repeated small measures can mean the economy limps along for years instead of recovering firmly.
