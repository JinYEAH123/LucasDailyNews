On September 11, 2001, terrorists flew planes into buildings in the United States. One target was the Pentagon, a huge five-sided building near Washington, D.C. where the military works. Another was the World Trade Center towers in New York City. Thousands of people died that day, and every year since, Americans hold ceremonies to remember them.

Twenty-five years after the September 11 attacks, President Trump marked the anniversary at the Pentagon instead of the traditional Ground Zero ceremony in New York. Every year on September 11, American presidents have gone to Ground Zero — the site in lower Manhattan where the World Trade Center towers fell — to mark the deadliest terrorist attack in U.S. history. Nearly 3,000 people died that day in 2001, when hijacked planes struck the towers, the Pentagon, and a field in Pennsylvania. This year, on the 25th anniversary, President Trump chose a different location: a ceremony at the Pentagon, the U.S. military headquarters just outside Washington, D.C., which was itself one of the targets in 2001.

In his remarks, Trump connected the anniversary to a war the United States is currently fighting with Iran, describing it as part of the same long struggle against terrorism that began with 9/11. That framing caught people's attention because for years, Trump has criticized American wars in the Middle East — including the invasions of Afghanistan and Iraq that followed 9/11 — as costly mistakes that dragged the U.S. into conflicts it should have avoided. Hearing him justify a current war using the same logic he once mocked struck many commentators as a notable shift, even though the White House has not described it that way.

Meanwhile, Vice President Vance attended the Ground Zero ceremony in New York, standing alongside living former presidents and the city's mayor, Zohran Mamdani. Splitting the two ceremonies between the president and vice president was itself unusual — normally the sitting president attends whichever site becomes the focal point, with the vice president elsewhere or absent. The choice meant that the country's two ceremonies delivered two different messages: one about ongoing war, the other about remembrance and unity.

On the same day, the CIA — the U.S. agency responsible for gathering foreign intelligence — declassified 71 documents related to the 9/11 attacks, making them public for the first time. Declassifying means removing the official secrecy that had kept these records hidden, often for national security reasons that eventually expire or get reconsidered. These documents add fresh detail to a long-running criticism of the CIA: that it had scattered pieces of information before 2001 suggesting an attack might be coming, but failed to connect them in time to stop it.

Whether Trump's Pentagon speech amounts to a real change in his views on war, or just words shaped by the pressures of the moment, is genuinely disputed. Presidents often use solemn anniversaries to justify current policies, regardless of what they've said in the past — that's a well-worn pattern in American politics. But it's also true that Trump built much of his political identity on opposing exactly this kind of open-ended military commitment abroad, which is why his critics and even some allies noticed the contrast.

  • Intervention — A military intervention is when a country sends its armed forces into another country's conflict or territory, often without a formal declaration of war.

Two Measles Deaths Are Missing From the Official Count

Measles has been spreading across the United States this year faster than it has in more than 30 years. Two people in Pennsylvania died from it, and state health officials say that's true. But for three weeks in a row, the country's top health agency has left those two deaths off its official list.

Why this mattersDoctors and parents use these numbers to decide how worried to be and whether more people need the measles vaccine. Measles is a sickness that spreads easily, like a very fast-moving cold. It gives people a fever, a cough, and a rash all over their skin. A vaccine — a small shot — can stop most people from ever catching it.

This year in the United States, measles has spread more than it has in over 30 years. So far, 3,134 people have gotten sick. That's roughly enough people to fill every seat in a big sports stadium's lower section.

Two of those sick people, in the state of Pennsylvania, died. Pennsylvania's own health officials confirmed both deaths. But the CDC — the national agency in charge of tracking diseases — has not added those two deaths to its official count. This has now gone on for three weeks.

People who work at the CDC had hoped its new boss would fix this and make sure the numbers matched what Pennsylvania says. So far, that hasn't happened, and no one has fully explained why the deaths are still missing.

Two Measles Deaths Go Missing From America's Official Count

Pennsylvania says two people died of measles in the state. The CDC, the federal agency that tracks disease outbreaks, has left both deaths out of its official count for three weeks running. Meanwhile the U.S. is having its worst measles outbreak in more than thirty years, with over 3,100 cases confirmed this year.

Measles is a virus that used to infect millions of American children every year before a vaccine nearly wiped it out in the country by 2000. It has been creeping back for years, spread mainly among unvaccinated communities, but this year's outbreak is the largest since that milestone. Tracking it accurately matters because health officials use the numbers to decide where to send vaccine teams, warn hospitals, and tell parents how worried to be.

That tracking job belongs to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, or CDC, the federal agency responsible for counting disease cases nationwide and publishing the official tally that doctors, reporters, and other governments rely on. Pennsylvania's own health department has confirmed two measles deaths in the state. For three weeks straight, those two deaths have not appeared in the CDC's national count, even though the state has told the agency about them.

No one in public has given a clear reason why. That absence is the whole story here: it is a fact in dispute, with Pennsylvania saying the deaths happened and are confirmed, and the CDC's leadership not explaining, on the record, why its own published numbers do not match. There are two ways adults read this. One is bureaucratic — health data moves through layers of verification, and mismatches between state and federal counts happen even in ordinary years. The other is political, and this is the reading that has alarmed CDC staff enough to speak to reporters: that the numbers are being managed to avoid making the outbreak look as bad as it is.

The political reading matters because of who runs the agency now. The CDC's new director, Erica Schwartz, reports to Robert F. Kennedy Jr., the Secretary of Health and Human Services — the cabinet department that oversees the CDC and sets its priorities. Kennedy has a long history of expressing skepticism about vaccines, and some CDC staff had hoped Schwartz would resist any political pressure to soften the outbreak's official numbers. According to reporting by NPR, a public radio news organization, that hope has not been borne out: the deaths remain uncounted, and the reasons remain unstated.

The timing raises the stakes. Flu season is arriving, which means hospitals are about to get busier handling a second contagious respiratory illness on top of measles. If the public and even doctors cannot trust that the government's own numbers reflect what state officials are reporting, it becomes harder for anyone to judge how serious the outbreak really is, or to plan for it. That is the deeper worry driving current CDC staff and outside health experts: not just two missing deaths, but what an unexplained gap between state and federal data suggests about whether the country's health statistics can be relied on at all.

Words worth knowing
  • CDC (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention) — The U.S. federal agency responsible for tracking disease outbreaks nationwide and publishing official case and death counts.
  • Health and Human Services Secretary — The cabinet official who oversees the CDC and other federal health agencies and sets their overall direction.
  • outbreak count — The official running total of confirmed cases and deaths from a disease, used by officials and hospitals to judge how serious a situation is.

A Computer Company's Giant Business Just Got Even Bigger

Oracle is a huge American company that owns giant buildings full of powerful computers. It just announced that renting out those computers made more than twice as much money as it did last year, mostly because AI companies need so much computer power. The news made Oracle's stock price jump around all day, as people tried to decide if this was purely good news or something to worry about too.

Why this mattersIf you've ever used a chatbot or asked a computer to draw a picture, it probably ran on machines just like the ones Oracle builds. This story shows how much money and construction is happening behind the scenes to make AI work, and how one company's bet on giant computer buildings can ripple out to jobs, prices, and the wider economy that your family lives in.
Read the whole story

Oracle doesn't make phones or shoes. It builds huge buildings called data centers, packed with powerful computers, and rents time on them to other companies.

One of its biggest customers is OpenAI, the company that makes the chatbot called ChatGPT. Training and running AI programs takes enormous amounts of computer power, so companies like OpenAI pay Oracle to use its machines instead of building their own.

In the last three months, Oracle earned $7.4 billion just from this computer-renting business. If you counted out loud, one number every second, without stopping, it would take you over 230 years to count that high. That's more than double what Oracle earned from this same business last year.

Oracle's total earnings for the three months reached $19.3 billion, and it kept $4.68 billion in profit after paying its costs. Investors were excited about those numbers, but also nervous, because building all these giant computer buildings costs Oracle a fortune too, and nobody knows for certain the demand will keep growing fast enough to pay for it all.

Oracle's Profits Jumped 60% in Three Months — Because Everyone Wants to Rent Its Computers

Oracle, the business software giant, released quarterly earnings on September 10 that stunned Wall Street: sales of its cloud computing services jumped 121% compared with a year earlier, and overall profit rose 60%. The numbers beat what analysts had predicted, and investors reacted by sending Oracle's stock swinging wildly in a single day of trading. Behind the swing is a bigger question nobody has answered yet: is this the start of a genuinely new economy, or the leading edge of a very expensive bet that might not pay off.

Why this mattersAlmost every AI tool you or your family might use — chatbots, image generators, voice assistants — runs on computing power rented from companies exactly like Oracle, so this earnings report is really a snapshot of how much money is flowing into the infrastructure behind those tools. It also matters because a growing slice of the stock market, and therefore many people's retirement savings and pension funds, is now tied to whether this AI spending boom keeps paying off or turns out to be overbuilt.
Read the whole story

On September 10, after the stock market closed for the day, Oracle told investors how its business had done over the previous three months. The headline number: sales from its cloud infrastructure division — essentially, the computing power it rents out to other companies over the internet — jumped 121% compared with the same period a year earlier, reaching $7.4 billion. That beat what Wall Street analysts had expected. Total revenue across the whole company grew 30% to $19.3 billion, and the profit left over for shareholders, called net income, hit $4.68 billion — up roughly 60% from a year before.

To understand why this matters beyond Oracle's own balance sheet, it helps to know what "cloud infrastructure" actually means. Training and running artificial intelligence systems requires enormous amounts of computing power — specialised chips, storage, and networking housed in huge warehouses called data centers. Building and running those data centers is expensive, so most companies rent capacity instead of owning it outright, the same way a small business might rent office space rather than buy a building. Oracle has spent years and huge sums constructing that capacity specifically to serve AI companies, including OpenAI, the maker of ChatGPT. This quarter's results are the clearest evidence yet that the bet is generating real paying demand, not just promises.

That's also why Oracle is being watched so closely by people who don't own a single share of its stock. In financial reporting, a company whose results are seen as an early signal for a much bigger trend is called a bellwether — the name comes from a lead sheep in a flock, wearing a bell so the shepherd can track where the whole group is heading. Oracle has become a bellwether for the AI boom itself: because so much of its growth now depends on selling computing power for AI, its earnings are treated as a rough gauge of how much of the broader economy is genuinely being carried by AI spending, and how much might be hype.

Yet even with numbers this strong, Oracle's stock swung sharply during Friday's trading rather than simply rising. The reason is a tension that the good results didn't resolve: building data centers at this scale requires spending enormous amounts of money upfront, known as capital expenditure, on land, buildings, chips and electricity contracts, often years before that spending is repaid by customer revenue. Investors have to weigh two possibilities at once — that demand for AI computing keeps climbing fast enough to justify the spending, or that Oracle and its rivals are building more capacity than customers will ultimately want, leaving expensive infrastructure sitting underused.

Oracle isn't alone in making this bet. Microsoft, Amazon, and Google are pouring similarly vast sums into their own data centers, all competing to be the company that AI firms rent their computing power from. That means Oracle's results this week aren't just a story about one company's quarter — they're a live data point in a much bigger argument happening across the tech industry and the stock market about whether the AI buildout is the foundation of the next phase of economic growth, or a very costly gamble that hasn't been tested by a downturn yet.

Words worth knowing
  • Cloud infrastructure — The data centers and computing power that companies rent out over the internet instead of every customer building and owning their own.
  • Bellwether — A company or event whose results are watched as an early signal of where a much bigger trend is heading.
  • Capital expenditure — Large upfront spending on physical things like buildings, chips, and equipment, which a company expects to pay off gradually over years.
