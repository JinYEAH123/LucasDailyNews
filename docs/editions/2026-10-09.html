A Giant Storm Races Toward the Gulf Coast

A storm called Hurricane Isaias grew strong very fast over the ocean. It is heading toward the coasts of Alabama, Florida, and Georgia. Forecasters say it could hit late Friday night with powerful winds and flooding. People living near the beach are packing up and leaving before it arrives.

Why this mattersEven if you live far from the coast, storms like this show how fast nature can change and how people must prepare quickly to stay safe. Families near the Gulf Coast are deciding right now whether to leave their homes, pack their pets, and find somewhere safe to wait it out. It is a reminder that weather can disrupt school, travel, and even holidays with very little warning.

Isaias is a hurricane. That means it is a giant spinning storm that forms over warm ocean water. It grew from a weak storm into a powerful one in just a short time. Scientists call this 'rapid intensification,' and it makes storms harder to predict.

The storm could push ocean water onto land. This is called a storm surge, and it could rise as high as 7 feet. That is taller than most grown-ups standing on each other's shoulders. The storm could also dump up to 15 inches of rain. Picture a ruler stacked on top of another ruler, that is how much rain could fall in just a day or two. Fast little tornadoes could also spin up without much warning.

Because of this danger, the governors of three states have declared a state of emergency. That means extra police, trucks, and helpers are ready to act fast. In some towns near the coast, people are being told they must leave their homes. Airports have shut down, and trains have stopped running in the storm's path.

Nobody knows exactly where the storm will hit hardest or how strong it will be when it does. Forecasters think it might briefly become a 'major hurricane,' one of the strongest kinds, before weakening right before it reaches land. That kind of not-knowing is normal when a storm is still out over open water.

Hurricane Isaias Goes From Tropical Storm to Major Threat in Under a Day

Hurricane Isaias strengthened explosively over the Gulf of Mexico and is now barreling toward the northern Gulf Coast, expected to make landfall late Friday night. Forecasters say it could briefly reach Category 3 strength before weakening just before it comes ashore. Alabama, Florida, and Georgia have all declared states of emergency, and some coastal counties have ordered residents to leave.

Why this mattersIf you live anywhere near the Gulf Coast, this is the kind of storm that can turn a normal Friday into an emergency within hours, which is exactly why officials act on uncertain forecasts rather than waiting for certainty. Even for readers far away, Isaias is a reminder that hurricanes are intensifying faster than they used to on average, a trend many scientists tie to warmer ocean temperatures — meaning the gap between 'manageable storm' and 'major hurricane' is shrinking, and so is the time people have to prepare.

A day and a half ago, Isaias was a disorganized tropical storm. Now it's a hurricane, and a strong one, expected to graze or briefly touch 'major hurricane' status — Category 3 or higher, meaning sustained winds above 111 miles per hour — before it loses some strength right at landfall. That kind of jump doesn't happen slowly. It happens because the storm found exactly the conditions it needed: warm ocean water acting like fuel, and calm air above it that let the storm's structure organize without being torn apart. Meteorologists call this rapid intensification, and it's one of the hardest things to forecast accurately, which is part of why hurricane warnings sometimes feel like they change by the hour.

The danger isn't just wind. Forecasters are warning of storm surge up to seven feet — that's ocean water, pushed by the storm's winds, shoving inland over roads, yards, and ground floors that are normally dry. Surge is usually what actually kills people in a hurricane, not the wind itself, because it rises fast and traps people who assumed they had more time. On top of that, parts of the coast could get up to 15 inches of rain, which is enough to flood areas far from the beach, and the storm is expected to spin off short-lived tornadoes that can form and dissipate within minutes, giving almost no warning.

Governors in Alabama, Florida, and Georgia have declared states of emergency, which unlocks emergency funding and lets the state move resources — National Guard troops, supplies, equipment — into position before the storm hits, rather than scrambling afterward. In some coastal counties, officials have gone further and ordered mandatory evacuations, meaning residents in the highest-risk zones are legally required to leave, not just advised to. Airports along the coast have closed or cut flights, and Amtrak has suspended train service on affected routes, because moving people and planes out of a hurricane's path is safer and cheaper than moving them through it.

As of Thursday, nobody can say exactly where Isaias will come ashore or precisely how strong it will be at that moment. This isn't forecasters hedging — it's a real limit of the science. A storm still sitting over open water can wobble, speed up, slow down, or interact with other weather systems in ways that shift its track by dozens of miles in a day. That uncertainty is why emergency officials tend to order evacuations for a wide stretch of coastline rather than just the exact spot models currently predict, accepting that some people will leave unnecessarily so that nobody who truly needs to leave gets missed.

Words worth knowing
  • Rapid intensification — When a hurricane's wind speeds jump by at least 35 mph within 24 hours, often giving forecasters and residents very little warning.
  • Storm surge — A wall of ocean water pushed inland by a hurricane's winds, which in most hurricanes is more dangerous to life than the wind itself.
  • Mandatory evacuation — An official order requiring residents in a high-danger area to leave, as opposed to a voluntary advisory that simply recommends it.

  1. Should the government be able to force people to leave their own homes during a hurricane, even if they refuse?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes, evacuation orders should be enforceable

    • People who stay behind often end up needing dangerous, risky rescues that put emergency workers' lives at risk too.
    • Officials with access to forecasts and flood models can see risks that an individual in their own house often can't judge for themselves.
    • A society has some responsibility to prevent entirely foreseeable deaths, even if that means overriding someone's personal choice.

    • It's a person's home and their life; the risk of staying is theirs to weigh, not the government's to decide for them.

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes, keep supporting rebuilding

    • People have deep roots, jobs, and communities in these places, and it's not fair to ask them to abandon their homes because of a risk they didn't create.
    • Coastal areas generate huge economic value — tourism, fishing, shipping — that the country would lose if people left.
    • Better building codes and stronger infrastructure can reduce the damage from future storms without forcing anyone to move.

    No, stop subsidizing rebuilding in the riskiest zones

    • Every rebuild in a flood zone that gets destroyed again costs taxpayers money that could prevent damage elsewhere instead.
    • Continuing to rebuild in the same spots sends a signal that the risk isn't real, even as storms get stronger and more frequent.
    • Helping people relocate now, while it's still a choice, is cheaper and less traumatic than forcing them out after the next disaster.

A Mix-Up Over ChatGPT's Money Shakes Up the Stock Market

OpenAI, the company that makes the chatbot ChatGPT, was thought to be earning about $70 billion a year. A new report says the real number is closer to $50 billion. When the news came out on Thursday, stock prices for several big tech companies dropped.

Why this mattersEven if you've never bought a share of stock, this story shows how one company's numbers can ripple out and affect many others — including companies that make the computer chips inside phones and game consoles. It's also a reminder that even huge, important-sounding numbers in the news sometimes come from just one source, and might need to be checked or corrected later.

OpenAI makes ChatGPT, a computer program that can chat with you, answer questions, and write stories. Lots of people and businesses pay to use it, so people have been trying to guess how much money OpenAI actually makes in a year.

For a while, many people believed that number was about $70 billion. A news organization called the Financial Times looked closely and reported the real figure is about $50 billion instead. That is still an enormous amount of money, but it is $20 billion less than people thought — like finding out your school's fundraiser made $50,000 instead of the $70,000 everyone was celebrating, just with nine extra zeros added on.

This news made investors nervous. Investors are people who buy small pieces of companies, called shares, hoping the company will do well. On Thursday, the prices of those shares dropped for several tech companies, including Oracle, Nvidia, and AMD, because many of them sell computer chips and equipment that OpenAI uses. If OpenAI is making less money than expected, investors worry it might buy less from them too.

It is also worth knowing that this whole story comes from just one report. Other news sites wrote about it, but they were really just repeating what the Financial Times found. OpenAI itself has not said whether the new number is correct.

One Report, One Afternoon, and Billions Wiped Off Tech Stocks

A single newspaper report claiming OpenAI's actual yearly revenue is $20 billion lower than previously thought sent a shock through the stock market on Thursday. Shares in Nvidia, AMD and Oracle all dropped sharply, and the wider Nasdaq index fell more than one percent, as investors reacted to the idea that the AI boom's star company might be growing slower than they'd been told.

Why this mattersIf you have a pension, a savings account linked to an index fund, or parents who do, you likely already have some money riding on whether companies like Nvidia and Oracle keep growing — and their growth is increasingly tied to how well AI companies like OpenAI actually perform, not just how exciting their technology sounds. This story is a reminder that huge sums of real money can swing on a single unverified report, long before anyone can check if it's true.
Read the whole story

For the past two years, OpenAI, the company behind ChatGPT, has been treated as the engine room of the entire artificial intelligence boom. Investors in chip companies, cloud providers and software firms have all been betting, directly or indirectly, that OpenAI's revenue would keep climbing at a breathtaking pace, justifying the hundreds of billions of dollars being poured into AI data centres and processors. So when the Financial Times reported that OpenAI's "annualized revenue" — a way of estimating a company's yearly income by taking its most recent month or quarter and multiplying it out — was roughly $50 billion as of September, not the $70 billion that earlier reports had suggested, it mattered far beyond OpenAI itself.

A $20 billion gap is not a rounding error. It's roughly the entire annual revenue of a major global airline. The new figure also means OpenAI, long assumed to be the clear leader in the field, now trails its closest rival Anthropic, which reportedly has annualized sales of about $65 billion. If accurate, this flips the assumed pecking order of the AI industry on its head and raises an uncomfortable question: were investors pricing OpenAI's value based on numbers that were simply too high?

Markets responded immediately. The Nasdaq, an index that tracks mostly technology companies and is often used as a barometer for how investors feel about tech, fell 1.25% to close at 27,193.34. The broader S&P 500, which covers 500 large American companies across all industries, dropped 0.47%. The damage was sharpest for companies whose fortunes are tightly bound to AI's continued growth: Oracle, which has signed huge cloud-computing deals linked to OpenAI, fell more than 5%, while chipmakers Nvidia and AMD, whose processors power AI systems, lost nearly 3% and almost 4%. These are the companies investors believe have the most to lose if OpenAI's growth turns out to be slower than advertised.

It's worth being precise about what actually happened here, because it says something about how information travels. The $20 billion gap comes from one Financial Times report. Other outlets that covered the story, including CNN, Yahoo Finance and TechCrunch, were reporting on that same FT article rather than digging up their own independent numbers. That doesn't make the figure false, but it does mean a single piece of reporting, not yet confirmed or denied by OpenAI itself, was enough to move the valuations of some of the world's largest public companies in a single afternoon.

That's partly because OpenAI itself isn't publicly traded, so there's no stock price or quarterly earnings report to check its numbers against directly. Investors are left relying on leaks, estimates and reports like this one to judge whether the eye-watering sums being invested in AI infrastructure make financial sense. When the only yardstick available shifts by $20 billion overnight, nervous money moves fast, even before anyone has confirmed whether the yardstick itself is accurate.

  • Valuation — The estimated total worth of a company, often based on investors' expectations about its future growth rather than just its current profits.
  • Stock index — A number that tracks the combined value of a group of companies' shares, used as a quick way to measure how a whole market or sector is doing, like the Nasdaq for technology stocks.

President Trump tried to stop reporters from three news companies — CNN, MS NOW, and Politico — from coming into the White House to ask questions. A judge named Timothy Kelly is now deciding if that's allowed. While he decides, he said those reporters can keep coming in for now.

A Judge Is Deciding Whether Trump Can Ban CNN and Politico From the White House

The Trump administration cut off three news organizations — CNN, MS NOW (formerly MSNBC), and Politico — from their usual access to cover the White House. Those outlets sued, and a federal judge is now deciding whether the ban can stand permanently. For now, the judge has ordered their access restored while he thinks it over, and during a hearing he reportedly sounded unconvinced by the government's defense of the ban.

Why this mattersHow governments treat journalists who ask tough questions says a lot about how much scrutiny those in power are willing to accept — and that scrutiny is part of how citizens, including future voters, find out what their government is actually doing. A ruling here could shape whether future presidents, from either party, can use access to the White House as a tool to reward friendly coverage and punish critical reporting.
Read the whole story

For decades, reporters from major news organizations have had standing access to the White House: seats in the briefing room, spots in the press pool that travels with the president, and the ability to ask questions at news conferences. This access isn't guaranteed by law in the way that, say, the right to publish a story is — it's something the White House manages, and administrations have always had some say over who gets credentials. But it has traditionally been treated as a professional courtesy extended broadly across the press, not as a reward handed out based on whether a president likes an outlet's coverage.

That changed when the Trump administration stripped CNN, MS NOW, and Politico of their regular access. All three sued, arguing the move was retaliation for critical coverage — the kind of government punishment of specific viewpoints that the First Amendment is generally understood to forbid. The case landed in front of Judge Timothy Kelly, who has the power to decide whether the ban violates the Constitution and, if so, to block it permanently.

The Justice Department, defending the ban in court, didn't argue that the administration can exclude any outlet it dislikes. Instead, it framed this as a specific case: Trump had previously said he was putting CNN "on notice" over coverage he called "fake news," and government lawyers argued this gave the administration a legitimate, non-political reason tied to that history — not an attempt to punish a particular point of view. Whether that distinction holds up is central to the case: a judge has to decide if "we warned them first" is meaningfully different from "we didn't like what they wrote about us."

Judge Kelly did not sound persuaded. According to reports from the hearing, he pressed the government's lawyers skeptically on their reasoning and, in the meantime, ordered that the three outlets' access be restored while he works through a final decision. That temporary order doesn't settle the case, but it signals how the arguments landed with him so far.

The dispute has drawn attention well beyond the three banned outlets. Seventy-seven news organizations and press-freedom groups — including outlets that often compete fiercely with CNN and Politico for the same stories — signed a joint legal filing, organized by the Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press, urging the court to block the ban. Their shared concern isn't really about these three organizations specifically; it's about precedent. If a president can legally cut off press access to outlets whose coverage he dislikes, any future president, of either party, could do the same to different targets.

No final ruling has come down yet. The case remains open, and until Judge Kelly issues a decision, the temporary access order is the only thing protecting CNN, MS NOW, and Politico's usual place in the White House press operation.

Words worth knowing
  • Press access — The official permission that lets a journalist into White House briefings, press conferences, and the press pool that travels with the president.
  • Viewpoint discrimination — When the government treats speech or speakers differently based on the opinions they express, something the First Amendment generally prohibits.
  • Amicus brief — A legal document filed by a group that isn't part of a lawsuit, submitted to share arguments or information the court might find useful.
