Trump Says He's Close to Deciding on Bigger Strikes Against Iran

President Trump says he is almost ready to decide whether to launch major new military strikes on Iran's government. On the very same day, a huge oil ship was attacked in a narrow, important stretch of water near Iran. Oil prices have stayed high ever since.

Why this mattersWhen oil ships can't travel safely, the price of oil goes up — and that can make gas for cars, buses, and even some foods more expensive for families everywhere, including yours. It's also a story about who gets to decide when a country goes to war, which is one of the biggest and most serious decisions a government can make.

Trump Weighs New Strikes on Iran as Tanker Attack Shakes Oil Markets, Congress Pushes Back

President Trump has told the news outlet Axios that he is close to deciding whether to launch major new U.S. military strikes aimed at "annihilating" Iran's government. In the past day, a tanker was hit in the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway that carries a huge share of the world's oil, and prices have stayed elevated since. Before deciding, Trump is set to meet leaders from six Gulf Arab states. Two days earlier, the House of Representatives voted for the third time to try to limit his power to order strikes without Congress's approval.

Why this mattersHigher oil prices from a shipping-lane attack can show up quickly in things you or your family pay for, from petrol to plane tickets to anything shipped by sea. And the fight in Congress over who gets to authorise military action isn't abstract lawmaking — it's about whether one person or an elected body decides when the country goes to a wider war.
Read the whole story

The war between the United States, Israel and Iran has dragged on with no clear end point, and Trump now says he is close to a decision on whether to go further than strikes on military and nuclear sites. He has talked about "annihilating" Iran's regime, meaning not just damaging its weapons or army but trying to bring down the government itself. That is a much bigger step than the strikes carried out so far, and it is one previous American presidents have generally avoided, because toppling a government by force is unpredictable: nobody can guarantee what, or who, replaces it.

In the middle of this, a tanker ship was hit in the Strait of Hormuz, a narrow sea passage between Iran and the Arabian Peninsula so important that roughly a fifth of the world's oil shipped by sea passes through it. Because it is so narrow, it is called a chokepoint: a single incident there can spook markets even if the actual disruption to supply is small, because traders start pricing in the risk of more attacks or an outright closure. Oil prices have stayed higher since the attack, which matters well beyond the shipping industry, since oil prices feed into the cost of petrol, flights, heating and almost anything that has to be transported.

Before making his call, Trump is scheduled to meet leaders from six Gulf Arab states, including Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates. These countries sit right across the water from Iran, host American military bases, and have the most to lose if the war spreads into their own waters or territory. Their input is not just diplomatic courtesy; several of them have spent years trying to lower tensions with Iran after decades of rivalry, and a U.S. campaign to remove Iran's government could blow up that work and drag them into a wider regional war they did not choose.

Two days before Trump's Axios interview, the House of Representatives voted for the third time on a resolution meant to stop him ordering further strikes on Iran without Congress's approval. That it happened a third time, and with support from both parties, is notable: it shows unease that crosses the usual party lines, at a moment when Congress rarely acts against a sitting president from its own side. Constitutionally, Congress alone has the power to declare war, but presidents for decades have argued that as commander-in-chief they can order military action to protect American interests without a formal declaration, especially when action is framed as defensive rather than a full war. That disagreement over where the line sits, not just this specific war, is what keeps resurfacing every time a president threatens major new strikes.

Whether Trump will actually go ahead is genuinely unclear. He has made this same threat before without following through, and it isn't known yet how heavily the tanker attack will weigh on his decision compared with what the Gulf states tell him, or what Congress's vote is meant to signal. What is certain is that the decision, whichever way it goes, will be read closely both by Iran's government and by lawmakers watching how far a president can push military action without asking them first.

Words worth knowing
  • Strait of Hormuz — A narrow sea passage between Iran and the Arabian Peninsula through which a large share of the world's oil shipments pass, making it a frequent flashpoint when tensions with Iran rise.
  • War Powers Resolution — A U.S. law meant to limit a president's ability to send the military into extended conflict without Congress's approval, though presidents have long argued it doesn't fully bind them.
  • regime change — Using force or other pressure to remove a country's government entirely, rather than just damaging its military capability.

  1. Should a U.S. president be able to order major new strikes on another country without a vote from Congress first?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Presidential authority

    • Wars can require fast decisions, and requiring a lengthy congressional vote could hand adversaries a window to strike first or hide their capabilities.

    • Congress can act afterward through funding decisions or votes if it disagrees, so the president's initial authority isn't unchecked, just faster.

    Congressional check

    • The Constitution explicitly gives the power to declare war to Congress, not the president, precisely to prevent one person from committing the country to conflict alone.
    • Wars have enormous, long-lasting costs in lives and money that should require broad political agreement, not a single official's judgment.

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Support

    • A government that sponsors attacks or destabilizes a region may pose an ongoing threat that limited strikes alone can't resolve.
    • Removing a hostile regime can end a source of danger for good rather than managing it indefinitely through repeated smaller conflicts.
    • Allies and neighbors directly threatened by that government may welcome, or even quietly encourage, decisive action to end the threat.

    Against

    • Toppling a government by force is unpredictable, and past attempts have often led to chaos, civil war, or a replacement just as hostile.
    • It sets a precedent that any powerful country can decide to overthrow another nation's government, which weakens the international norms that protect all countries, including one's own allies.
    • The people who suffer most from regime-change wars are usually civilians in the target country, not the officials being targeted.

America's Money Referee Raises the Cost of Borrowing

A group called the Federal Reserve decides how expensive it is to borrow money in America. This week they raised that cost for the first time in two years. Now buying a house, a car, or even using a credit card will cost more for millions of families.

Why this mattersIf your family ever borrows money for a car, a house, or even puts something on a credit card, this decision could mean paying more each month. It also shows how a handful of people making one decision in Washington can ripple out and touch families all across the country.
Read the whole story

Imagine you borrow $100 from a friend and promise to pay back $104 later. That extra $4 is called interest. The Federal Reserve sets a rate that affects how much extra everyone in America pays when they borrow money, whether it's a bank lending for a house or a company borrowing to grow.

This week, all 12 people who vote on this decision agreed together, like a class where every single hand goes up, to raise that rate a little bit. It's now between 3.75% and 4.00%, which doesn't sound huge, but it touches almost everyone's wallet.

They did this because prices for things like food and energy have been climbing fast, and the Fed's job is to try to slow that down. Raising the rate makes borrowing pricier, which usually means people spend a bit less, which can help cool down fast-rising prices.

Not everyone agrees this was the right move. Some experts think the economy was already slowing down on its own, and worry this extra squeeze could cause problems, like people losing jobs or businesses struggling to grow.

Words worth knowing
  • Interest rate — The extra amount you have to pay back when you borrow money, usually shown as a percentage.
  • Inflation — When prices for things like food, gas, and toys keep going up over time, so your money buys less than it used to.

The Fed Raises Interest Rates Again — and Markets Aren't Happy

The U.S. Federal Reserve, the country's central bank, raised its key interest rate on Wednesday for the first time since 2023, pushing it to a range of 3.75% to 4.00%. Every single one of the twelve officials who vote on the decision backed it — a rare unanimous result. Investors had been betting on rate cuts. They got the opposite.

Why this mattersIf you or your family have a credit card balance, a car loan, or are planning to buy a home, this decision touches the interest you'll actually pay — and it can keep rising if the Fed hikes again. It's also a reminder that a handful of officials meeting in Washington can shift costs for nearly every household in the country without a single new law being passed.
Read the whole story

The Federal Reserve doesn't set the price of a loaf of bread or the rent on an apartment, but it sets something that quietly shapes both: the interest rate banks charge each other to borrow money overnight. That single number, adjusted eight times a year by the Fed's rate-setting committee, ripples outward into what banks charge you for a mortgage, a car loan, or a credit card balance. On Wednesday, under new Fed Chair Kevin Warsh, the committee voted 12-0 to raise that rate by a quarter of a percentage point, to a range of 3.75%-4.00%.

The Fed's official job is to keep prices stable and keep as many people employed as possible — two goals that don't always pull in the same direction. Raising rates makes borrowing more expensive, which tends to slow down spending and cool off inflation, the rate at which prices for everyday things rise. The Fed said this hike was aimed squarely at inflation, which it argued had been made worse by a sudden jump in energy prices — the kind of shock that pushes up the cost of everything from gasoline to heating a home to running a factory.

What makes this move notable is the reversal it represents. Just weeks earlier, investors in bond and stock markets had priced in the opposite: they expected the Fed to start cutting rates, on the theory that the economy was cooling enough that borrowing costs should come down, not go up. Instead, the Fed hit the brakes harder. Markets reacted with visible unease in the days that followed — a sign that traders read this not as a routine adjustment but as a signal the Fed sees more inflation risk than they had assumed.

One widely discussed analysis went further, arguing the Fed had effectively been "bullied" into this hike by incoming inflation data rather than acting on a timeline of its own choosing. That's a pointed claim, because the Fed's entire authority rests on the idea that it makes calm, independent judgments about the economy — not that it gets pushed around by the latest report. Fed officials would likely reject the framing, saying that responding to new data is exactly what a central bank is supposed to do. But the fact that the debate is happening at all tells you something about how unsettled the mood around this decision is.

For ordinary Americans, the practical effect arrives gradually but widely. Banks use the Fed's rate as a baseline for setting the interest they charge on new mortgages, car loans, credit card balances, and business loans. A quarter-point increase might sound small, but multiplied across tens of millions of borrowers, it adds up to real money — extra dollars a month on a mortgage payment, a slightly higher minimum payment on a credit card. Fed officials signaled that this hike might not be the last, which means the direction of borrowing costs, at least for now, is up rather than down.

Words worth knowing
  • Federal Reserve (the Fed) — The central bank of the United States, whose job is to keep prices stable and support employment by controlling how expensive it is to borrow money.
  • Benchmark interest rate — The key rate the Fed sets for banks borrowing from each other overnight, which then influences the rates banks charge ordinary borrowers on mortgages, loans, and credit cards.
  • Inflation — The rate at which prices for goods and services rise over time, eroding how much your money can buy.

China's AI Companies Lose Billions as America Debates Locking Them Out

Chinese companies that build smart computer programs called AI have lost about $33 billion in value this month. At the same time, an American AI company called Anthropic wants the U.S. government to stop Chinese companies from using America's best AI programs. If that happens, American and Chinese AI would grow further apart than ever.

Why this mattersYou might already talk to AI when you ask a smart speaker a question or use it to help with homework. The AI being built right now in America and China could end up running the phones, cars, and even toys you use when you're grown up. Whether these two countries build separate AI worlds, or find ways to work together, will shape how smart — and how different — our machines become.
Read the whole story

Companies in China build AI, short for artificial intelligence — computer programs that can chat, write stories, and solve problems almost like a person. Two of these companies are called Z.AI and MiniMax. This month, people who buy and sell tiny pieces of companies, called stock, decided these companies were worth much less. Together, China's AI companies lost about $33 billion — that's enough money to buy tens of millions of laptops.

At the same time, an American company called Anthropic, which builds some of the smartest AI programs in the world, is asking the U.S. government to do something big. Anthropic wants to stop Chinese companies from using America's most advanced AI programs, called frontier models because they sit at the very edge of what AI can do. Anthropic says this would help America stay ahead of China.

China disagrees. Chinese officials say blocking them is unfair, and they believe being cut off wouldn't actually slow them down. Instead, they think it would push Chinese scientists to build their own AI chips and programs even faster, so they don't need America's help at all.

This wouldn't be the first wall built between American and Chinese AI. The U.S. government already stops the chip company Nvidia from selling its most powerful computer chips to China. If Anthropic gets its way, that wall between the two countries' AI would grow even taller — though nobody knows yet if the government will agree.

China's AI Giants Lose $33 Billion as US Weighs a Total Cutoff

Chinese AI companies including Z.AI and MiniMax have watched about $33 billion evaporate from their stock market value this month. At the same time, American AI company Anthropic is pushing the US government to block Chinese firms from using top-tier American AI models entirely, arguing it's the only way to keep America ahead.

Why this mattersThe chatbots, phone assistants, and apps you use are built on this same technology, and where it's allowed to flow shapes what gets built and how fast. If the US and China's AI industries fully separate, it could mean two increasingly different versions of AI develop in parallel — one you'll likely grow up using more of than the other, without ever choosing which.
Read the whole story

Investors have been dumping shares in Chinese artificial intelligence companies all month, wiping out roughly $33 billion in combined value from firms like Z.AI and MiniMax. That's not pocket change — it's more than the market value of many well-known public companies, gone in weeks. The selloff reflects growing doubts among investors about how competitive Chinese AI firms can stay if the walls between the US and Chinese tech industries keep going up.

Those walls are already partly built. For a few years now, the US government has restricted which advanced computer chips American companies like Nvidia can sell to China, specifically the powerful chips needed to train the largest AI systems. The idea was to slow China's ability to build cutting-edge AI by starving it of the best hardware. Now some American AI companies want to go further. Anthropic, which makes the Claude chatbot, is lobbying Washington to cut Chinese companies off from frontier AI models altogether — meaning the most advanced AI systems available, the ones at the very edge of what the technology can currently do.

The argument for going further is straightforward: whoever builds the most capable AI first gets outsized economic and military advantages, and every point of access Chinese firms have to American AI tools — even indirectly, through partnerships or workarounds — chips away at that lead. Anthropic and others worry that Chinese companies use exposure to leading US models to help train their own, effectively getting a head start paid for by someone else's research.

The argument against it is that this kind of pressure has backfired before. When the US tightened chip exports, it didn't stop Chinese AI development — it pushed Chinese companies and the government to pour money into building their own chips and software from scratch, a goal officials call self-sufficiency. A Chinese AI lab called DeepSeek surprised the industry last year by producing a highly capable model despite the chip restrictions, which some took as proof that cutting China off makes it more resourceful, not weaker.

Nobody knows yet which reading is correct, and Washington hasn't decided whether to adopt the stricter rules Anthropic wants. Chinese officials, for their part, are likely to call any new restrictions unfair and use them as further justification for building an AI industry that doesn't depend on American technology at all. Whatever happens, the stock market has already made its own bet: that the space for Chinese AI companies to grow by using American tools is shrinking, and that shrinkage carries a real financial cost.

Words worth knowing
  • Frontier AI models — The most advanced AI systems that exist at any given time, sitting at the very edge of what the technology can currently do.
  • Export controls — Government rules that stop companies from selling certain technology, like advanced computer chips, to specific countries or buyers.
  • Self-sufficiency — A country's effort to build the technology it needs on its own, so it no longer depends on suppliers from other countries.

    • Companies closest to the technology often understand its risks and capabilities better than anyone else, including government officials.
