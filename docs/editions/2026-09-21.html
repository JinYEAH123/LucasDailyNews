Three News Companies Sue the President Over Being Kept Out of the White House

Guards stopped reporters from CNN, MS NOW and Politico at the White House gates over the weekend. The three news companies say President Trump banned them for good. Now they are suing him in court to get back in.

Why this mattersThis is about whether the people who run a country can pick and choose which reporters are allowed to watch and ask them questions. If a leader can ban reporters he dislikes, people might only hear the news the leader wants them to hear. That matters for everyone, because knowing what your government is doing is part of how a country like the United States is supposed to work.

On Saturday, reporters from three news companies tried to walk into the White House to do their jobs. Guards would not let them through the gate. This had never happened to them before.

The reporters work for CNN, MS NOW and Politico. These are companies that report news about the government every day. President Trump, the leader of the United States, had ordered them banned from the building.

On Monday, all three companies filed a lawsuit. That means they asked a judge to decide if the ban is allowed. They say the ban breaks a rule in the U.S. Constitution that protects reporters' right to cover the government.

The White House says the president is allowed to decide who comes into the building, just like anyone can decide who visits their house. A judge will now have to decide who is right.

CNN, Politico and MS NOW Sue Trump Over White House Ban

On Saturday, security guards physically stopped reporters from CNN, MS NOW and Politico at the White House gates and refused to let them in. Two days later, on Monday, all three news organizations filed a federal lawsuit against President Trump, arguing the ban is illegal. The case will decide whether a president can name specific news outlets and simply lock them out.

Why this mattersThis case is really about a simple question with big consequences: can a government leader decide, on his own, which reporters are allowed to ask him questions? Whatever a court decides will shape not just these three outlets, but how much independent scrutiny any future president can be forced to accept.
Read the whole story

Reporters showed up for work on Saturday the way they always had, expecting to pass through White House security and take their usual seats. Instead, guards turned them away at the gate. The reporters worked for CNN, MS NOW and Politico — three organizations the Trump administration had decided, without much public explanation, no longer belonged inside.

By Monday morning, all three outlets had filed a lawsuit in federal court. Their argument rests on the First Amendment, the part of the U.S. Constitution that stops the government from punishing or silencing speech and the press. The lawsuit calls the ban 'a direct assault on the First Amendment,' saying the president cannot simply decide which news organizations are allowed to cover him and which are shut out because he dislikes their reporting.

The White House has pushed back, saying the president has the authority to control who gets access to his own building. That is true up to a point — every administration sets rules about who can enter and how many seats exist in the briefing room. What's unusual here is that the administration has not, at least in court filings so far, spelled out the specific legal reasoning for banning these three organizations by name rather than adjusting general access rules.

This is why the case matters beyond the three outlets involved. For decades, the White House press pool — the rotating group of reporters allowed close access to cover the president day to day — has operated as a shared arrangement, not something handed out or taken away based on whether coverage is flattering. If a president can permanently exclude named outlets, future presidents of any party could do the same to whichever news organizations they find inconvenient.

Because CNN and Politico are suing on their own behalf, their coverage of this story can't be treated as a neutral outside account — they have a direct stake in the outcome. Independent reporting from outlets like NPR and The Washington Post has covered the filing as well, and Al Jazeera has too, though it's funded by the government of Qatar, which is worth keeping in mind when reading its framing of a story about press freedom in another country.

Words worth knowing
  • First Amendment — The part of the U.S. Constitution that prevents the government from restricting free speech and freedom of the press.
  • White House press pool — A rotating group of reporters given close access to cover the president's daily activities, traditionally shared across news organizations.
  • Federal lawsuit — A legal case filed in a national court, used here to ask a judge to rule that the ban violates the Constitution.

  1. Should a president have the power to ban specific, named news organizations from the White House?

Two Powerful Countries Talk About Robots, Rare Rocks, and a Big Meeting on Thursday

Why this mattersThe phone or tablet you might use has computer chips inside it, and those chips need critical minerals to be made. Scott Bessent runs the US Treasury. That means he handles America's money matters with other countries. He Lifeng is a top Chinese official too. This weekend, they sat down to talk.

They talked about artificial intelligence, or AI. That's the smart computer technology behind things like chatbots and self-driving cars. Both countries want to be the best at building it. They also talked about critical minerals, special metals used to build batteries and computer chips.

Bessent had a new idea. He suggested the US and China should tell each other when something goes wrong with their AI systems. Think of it like two kids on a seesaw agreeing to warn each other before jumping off.

These talks are getting ready for an even bigger meeting on Thursday. The leaders of both countries, Donald Trump and Xi Jinping, will meet at the White House. AI competition is expected to be one of the biggest topics they discuss.

US and China Talk AI Safety Days Before Trump and Xi Meet

Over the weekend, America's top economic official and a senior Chinese official sat down to discuss artificial intelligence, trade and rare minerals. The US side called the talks 'successful' — though China has not yet said whether it agrees. The conversation is meant to smooth the way for a face-to-face meeting between Presidents Trump and Xi this Thursday, where AI is expected to be a central subject.

Why this mattersThe chips in your phone, the batteries in electric cars, and the AI tools increasingly used in schools, hospitals and workplaces all depend on this US-China relationship staying at least partly functional. If the two countries can't agree on basic safety communication about AI even as they race to build it, the technology reaching your life faster than ever could also be reaching you with fewer safeguards.
Read the whole story

Scott Bessent, the US Treasury Secretary — effectively America's finance minister, in charge of the government's money and economic policy — met with He Lifeng, one of China's Vice Premiers, a senior official who helps run the country's economy under President Xi. Over the weekend the two discussed artificial intelligence, trade, and critical minerals: the metals and rare elements, like lithium and cobalt, that go into everything from smartphone batteries to fighter jets, and that China currently dominates the mining and processing of.

Bessent described the talks as 'successful.' That word matters, because so far it comes from only one side of the table. No Chinese government statement about the same meeting had appeared by the time this was reported, so it is not clear whether Beijing sees things the same way — or whether it has its own, less rosy account it simply hasn't shared yet.

The most interesting new detail to come out of the weekend is a proposal from Bessent: that the US and China set up a system to notify each other about AI safety issues. Think of it like two rival companies agreeing to warn each other if they spot a fire risk in a shared factory, even while they're still competing fiercely to beat each other to market. The idea would be to flag dangerous glitches or unexpected AI behavior across the border, rather than each country discovering problems only when it's too late.

This all sits inside a much bigger and tenser story. For months, the US and China have been locked in a contest over who leads in AI, which has spilled into fights over export controls — US rules that block advanced computer chips and technology from being sold to Chinese companies, on the grounds that the same chips could power Chinese weapons or surveillance systems — and over access to those critical minerals China controls. Each side has leverage over the other: the US has the most advanced chips, China has the raw materials several key industries depend on.

All of this is the backdrop for Thursday's summit at the White House between Trump and Xi. Analysts — people who study these relationships for governments, banks and news organizations — are watching closely to see whether the AI rivalry helps push the two leaders toward practical agreements, or whether it becomes one more point of friction that derails everything else on the agenda.

Words worth knowing
  • Critical minerals — Metals and rare elements — like lithium and cobalt — needed for batteries, electronics and military equipment, which China currently dominates in mining and processing.
  • Export controls — Government rules that block certain technology, like advanced computer chips, from being sold to specific countries or companies for security reasons.
  • AI safety notification — A proposed system where two countries would formally warn each other about dangerous or unexpected problems discovered in AI systems.

Google's New Laptops Come With an AI Helper Built In From the Start

On Monday, Google said you can now order its very first "Googlebook" laptops. They start at $899 and go on sale October 4. Instead of building the laptops itself, Google asked five companies to make them.

Why this mattersLots of kids use a laptop or tablet for schoolwork, and grown-ups use them for their jobs. If Googlebooks catch on, the next laptop your family buys might come with an AI helper built into its bones instead of added on top — changing how you search for answers, write essays, or ask a computer for help.
Read the whole story

Google is famous for its search engine and its Gemini AI assistant, but it does not usually build computers. That is changing. This week, Google announced laptops called Googlebooks, and people can order one right now, even though they will not arrive until October 4.

The starting price is $899. If you saved five dollars every single week, it would take you more than three years to save that much. Google is not making these laptops in its own factories. It asked five companies that already make laptops — Dell, HP, Lenovo, Acer, and Asus — to build them instead.

What makes a Googlebook different is not the outside of the laptop. It is what is running on the inside. These laptops use a new operating system — the main program that controls everything a computer does — built completely around Gemini, Google's AI assistant. On most laptops, an AI helper is just one more app you open. On a Googlebook, it is woven into everything from the very start.

Dell has already shown its own version, called the XPS Googlebook, which matches Google's pricing and timeline. What nobody knows yet is how well these will sell compared to the laptops people already use, or what they will cost in other countries.

Words worth knowing
  • AI assistant — A computer program, like Google's Gemini, that can answer questions, write text, or help you finish tasks when you talk or type to it.
  • operating system — The main software that runs everything else on a computer, like the control room that keeps all the other parts working together.

Google Bets That Laptops Need AI Built In, Not Bolted On

On Monday, Google opened preorders for its first 'Googlebook' laptops, priced from $899, with sales starting October 4. Instead of building the machines itself, Google is working with five manufacturers to produce computers built around its Gemini AI assistant from the ground up, rather than adding AI as just another app you download.

Why this mattersIf you or your family buy a laptop in the next year, this is part of why the choices in the store look different: companies are now selling AI as a built-in feature of the machine, not an app you add later. It also matters because it's an early test of whether 'AI-first' computing is something people actually want, or something companies are pushing because they've already spent billions building it.
Read the whole story

Google's pitch is simple to say and expensive to prove: that people will trade in a perfectly working laptop for a new one because the artificial intelligence lives inside the operating system itself, not in a browser tab or a separate app you have to remember to open. The company confirmed on Monday that these 'Googlebook' laptops start at $899, that preorders are open now, and that they go on sale October 4 — the first time it has nailed down pricing and specifications since first showing the concept back in May.

Google isn't making the hardware itself. It's partnering with five companies that already build laptops for a living — Dell, HP, Lenovo, Acer, and Asus — the same way it has long let other companies build the physical phones that run its Android software. Each manufacturer will produce its own Googlebook model; Dell has already put out its own announcement for an XPS version, which lines up with Google's numbers on price and release date, giving the story two independent sources rather than one company's claim about itself.

The laptops run an operating system — the base software that controls a computer and everything running on it, the layer beneath every app — that resembles Android, the system found on most non-Apple phones, but built specifically around Gemini, Google's AI assistant. The bet here is structural, not cosmetic. Google isn't asking you to install a chatbot on your existing laptop; it's arguing that AI should be woven into how the whole machine works, so it can see what's on your screen, help across every app, and respond instantly rather than through a website you have to visit.

This is a familiar move for Google, which used the same playbook with Android over a decade ago: give the software away to hardware makers, let them compete on price and design, and try to become the default layer everyone builds on top of. The question this time is whether AI is actually the kind of feature people will buy a new laptop for. Smartphones replaced flip phones because they did something genuinely new. Whether an AI-native laptop clears that bar, or just repackages things a browser extension can already do, is exactly what's unproven.

What reporting hasn't settled yet is how this plays outside the United States — no pricing or launch details exist yet for other countries — or how these machines will actually sell against Windows laptops, which dominate offices and schools, and Chromebooks, Google's own cheaper laptops that have sold well for years partly by not trying to be flashy. Google now has three different kinds of laptop in the market at once, competing partly against itself.

Words worth knowing
  • AI-native — Describes a device built from the start around artificial intelligence at its core, rather than having AI features added on top of an existing design later.
  • Operating system — The base software that runs a computer or phone and controls everything else on it, like Windows, Android, or Apple's iOS.
  • Gemini — Google's AI assistant, similar to ChatGPT, which can answer questions, write text, and help with tasks across apps and devices.
