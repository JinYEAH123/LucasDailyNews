China Removes Dozens of Top Leaders in Biggest Shake-Up in Decades

China's ruling Communist Party just kicked 33 of its most powerful officials out of the party. It's the biggest group removed at once since the 1960s and 70s, when Mao Zedong ruled the country. One of those removed was China's top military general.

Why this mattersThis is happening far away, but China has the world's second-biggest economy and a huge military, so changes at the top matter everywhere. China's military leaders make decisions about Taiwan, an island many countries, including the United States, care about keeping safe. When a government suddenly swaps out so many leaders, it can change how that country acts toward its neighbors.

China is run by one political party, called the Communist Party. Its leader, Xi Jinping, has been in charge for over ten years. This week the party held a big meeting, called a plenum, where top leaders make major decisions.

At this meeting, 33 senior officials were kicked out. That's like removing the entire coaching staff of several sports teams in one day. One of them was Zhang Youxia, the highest-ranking general in China's military. Another was Liu Jianchao, a diplomat many expected might become China's top foreign policy official.

The party says these officials broke the rules through corruption, meaning they may have stolen money or misused their power. News organizations report the real number pushed out is even bigger, including around 16 generals and several business leaders tied to the government.

Xi Jinping defended the sweep, saying strict rules keep the party strong and don't hurt the economy. But losing the top general raises a big question: who takes charge of the military next, and how might that change things with Taiwan, a nearby island that China claims as its own?

China Purges Its Top General in the Biggest Communist Party Shakeup Since Mao

China's Communist Party has expelled 33 senior officials, including its highest-ranking uniformed general, in the largest single purge tracked since the Mao era. New reporting suggests the real number reaches further, taking in roughly 16 generals and several executives at state-linked companies. Xi Jinping has publicly defended the sweep as good discipline rather than a sign of instability, even as it raises hard questions about who actually controls China's military.

Why this mattersHow China's government and military are run shapes things far beyond its borders — the price of the electronics in your home, the safety of shipping lanes, and the odds of a crisis over Taiwan that could pull in the United States. A leadership shake-up at the top of the world's second-largest economy and largest standing military is exactly the kind of story that quietly changes decisions made in government offices and company boardrooms everywhere, decisions that eventually land on ordinary people.

This week China's Communist Party wrapped up a major internal meeting called the fifth plenum, and announced it had expelled 33 senior officials from the party — the biggest single purge of this scale since the era of Mao Zedong, the party's founding leader who ruled until 1976. Purges are a recurring feature of Chinese politics, a way the party disciplines and reshuffles itself. What makes this one different is the seniority of who was removed, and how many of them there were at once.

The most striking name is Zhang Youxia. Until now he was China's highest-ranking uniformed military officer and a vice chairman of the Central Military Commission, the body that commands China's armed forces and answers directly to Xi Jinping. Also expelled was Liu Jianchao, a career diplomat who had been floated as a possible future foreign minister. Losing two officials this senior in a single sweep is unusual even by the standards of Xi's decade-plus anti-corruption campaign.

New reporting suggests the purge reaches well beyond the headline figure. Alongside the 33 named officials, roughly 16 generals and several executives at state-linked companies have also been expelled from the party, according to detailed accounts from Hong Kong's South China Morning Post and Japan's Nikkei Asia. The Morning Post is owned by the Chinese tech company Alibaba, which is worth keeping in mind when reading its coverage of Chinese politics — though its specific personnel reporting here is corroborated by Nikkei, an outlet with no such ownership tie.

Xi Jinping addressed the sweep publicly, arguing that strict party discipline 'has a positive impact' and does not get in the way of economic growth. That was a direct response to concerns, raised by some foreign analysts, that the purges are destabilizing China's government at a sensitive moment. Xi is widely expected to seek a fourth term as party leader, breaking with an unwritten two-term limit that governed Chinese politics for decades until he set it aside.

The military angle carries particular weight. Zhang Youxia outranked every other uniformed officer in China's chain of command, so removing him forces a rethink of who actually runs the People's Liberation Army day to day, and who might eventually succeed him. That matters well beyond China's borders: the PLA is the force that would carry out any move against Taiwan, the self-governing island Beijing claims as its own territory. A military mid-shakeup behaves differently than one with settled, predictable leadership — though people who study this closely disagree about whether that makes a crisis more or less likely.

Words worth knowing

  • Central Military Commission — The body that commands China's armed forces; it is chaired by Xi Jinping, which makes it the ultimate authority over the military.
  • Purge — The removal of officials from a political party or government, often announced as an anti-corruption measure but sometimes used to eliminate political rivals as well.

America's Economy Grew Fast — But So Did Prices

The U.S. government said the country's economy grew quickly this summer, growing by 3.4% in just three months. But at the same time, prices for everyday things kept climbing too, about 3.6% higher than a year ago. The people in charge of interest rates met and decided not to change anything — even though some of them wanted to.

Why this mattersWhen prices go up, it means the money in your family's wallet or piggy bank doesn't stretch as far as it used to — a dollar buys less candy, less gas, less of everything. These numbers also matter because they came out just before Americans voted in an election, and polls show rising prices were the thing voters cared about most.
Read the whole story

Imagine the whole country's businesses — every store, factory, and company — made and sold things last summer. If you added up the value of all of it, that total grew by 3.4% compared to the time before. That's a pretty fast growth rate for a country this big, like a kid growing two inches in just three months.

But here's the tricky part. At the same time, prices for things families buy — food, gas, toys — went up by 3.6% over the past year. That means the same allowance or paycheck buys a little less than it used to. Economists call this inflation.

A group called the Federal Reserve decides on interest rates, which affect how expensive it is to borrow money for things like houses or cars. They met and chose to leave rates the same. But two of the people in that group disagreed — they wanted to raise rates to fight rising prices, even if it meant the job market might slow down.

Grown-ups don't agree on what these numbers mean. Some say fast growth proves the economy is strong and healthy. Others point out that a lot of that growth came from companies spending huge amounts on artificial intelligence (AI) technology — not from regular families feeling richer. And prices are still rising faster than the Fed wants, which makes many people nervous right before an election.

  • Inflation — Inflation is when prices for things you buy, like snacks or toys, keep going up over time, so your money buys a little less each year.

The Economy Grew 3.4% Right Before the Election — So Why Are Voters Still Angry About Prices?

Four days before Americans voted in the 2025 midterm elections, the government released its last major economic report card: the economy had grown at a healthy 3.4% annual pace, but prices were still rising 3.6% faster than a year earlier. The Federal Reserve, the institution that sets U.S. interest rates, met the same week and decided not to change anything — even though two of its own officials wanted to raise rates. The numbers told two true but conflicting stories at once, and which one you believed probably depended on whether you felt richer or poorer than last year.

Why this mattersInterest rates and inflation aren't abstract — they decide how expensive it is for your family to borrow money for a car or a house, and whether your allowance or your parents' paycheck actually buys more or less than it did last year. When politicians and the Fed argue over which economic number matters most, they're really arguing about whose experience of the economy counts as the 'real' one.
Read the whole story

Gross domestic product, or GDP, is the total value of everything a country produces — goods, services, construction, software, all of it — in a given period. When the U.S. Commerce Department announced the economy had grown at a 3.4% annualized rate in the third quarter, that's normally the kind of number that gets celebrated: businesses are selling more, people are working, money is moving. But growth alone doesn't tell you whether ordinary people feel better off, and a separate report released around the same time showed consumer prices were up 3.6% over the previous year — well above the 2% inflation rate the Fed treats as healthy.

That gap matters because wages don't always keep pace with prices. If your paycheck grows 3% but the cost of groceries, rent, and gas grows 3.6%, you're not actually getting ahead — you're falling behind in real terms even though the economy, measured in total dollars, looks fine. Polling before the midterms consistently found that the cost of living was voters' single biggest worry, which is why a 'strong' GDP number didn't necessarily translate into people feeling good about the economy or the politicians in charge of it.

The Federal Reserve sits at the center of this tension. It's the U.S. central bank, and one of its main tools is the benchmark interest rate — essentially the cost of borrowing money, which ripples out into mortgage rates, credit card interest, and business loans. Raising rates tends to cool off inflation by making borrowing and spending more expensive, but it also risks slowing the job market, since businesses hire less when borrowing costs more. At its October 28 meeting, the Fed chose to hold its rate steady, judging that the risks of inflation and a weakening job market were roughly balanced. Two officials disagreed and wanted to raise rates anyway — a notable split that shows even the experts inside the institution aren't sure which danger is bigger right now.

There's a second argument buried in the growth number itself: where is it actually coming from? A number of economists point out that a large share of the recent GDP growth is concentrated in business investment tied to artificial intelligence — companies building data centers, buying computing chips, and expanding AI infrastructure. That's real economic activity, but it's not evenly spread. If you work in AI, cloud computing, or construction for tech companies, the boom is probably visible in your paycheck or your company's spending. If you work in retail, hospitality, or a small local business, you might see almost none of it, even though the national GDP figure counts it all the same way.

That disconnect — a national number that looks strong next to millions of individual households that don't feel it — is exactly what made this report land awkwardly right before an election. Growth and inflation are both real, both measured honestly, and both true at the same time. The disagreement isn't really about the numbers; it's about which number should define how people judge the economy, and that's a question statistics alone can't settle.

  • Inflation — The rate at which prices for everyday things rise over time, which shrinks how much your money can actually buy even if you're earning the same amount.
  • Federal Reserve — The United States' central bank, which sets interest rates to try to control inflation and support the job market, independent of the president or Congress.

  1. Should the Federal Reserve have raised interest rates to fight inflation, even at the risk of slowing hiring?

The Man Behind ChatGPT Wants Almost Half the World's Computer Memory

Sam Altman runs OpenAI, the company that made ChatGPT. He flew all the way to South Korea to meet with Samsung, a company that makes the tiny memory chips inside phones, laptops, and computers. He said OpenAI's computers might someday need up to 40% of all the memory chips made on Earth — and even he admitted that could be bad news for anyone trying to buy a new phone or laptop.

Why this mattersIf memory chips become scarce, the price of your family's next phone, laptop, or gaming device could go up — not because the device got better, but because AI companies are buying up the parts first. It's a reminder that the AI boom you hear about isn't just happening on computer screens — it can reach right into the shops where your parents buy electronics.
Read the whole story

Every phone, laptop, and game console has a memory chip inside it. Think of it like a notebook the device uses to remember what it's doing right this second — open apps, loaded games, half-typed messages. These chips are called RAM, which stands for 'random access memory.'

Sam Altman's company, OpenAI, runs huge computer warehouses called data centers that power ChatGPT and other AI tools. These computers need enormous numbers of memory chips to work. Altman met with about 50 leaders from Samsung, one of the biggest chip-making companies in the world, to lock in deals for even more chips.

Here's the eye-popping part: Altman said OpenAI's future needs could gobble up 40% of all the memory chips made worldwide. Imagine a classroom of 100 kids sharing one big bag of candy — if OpenAI took 40 pieces, that leaves only 60 for everyone else, including people who just want a new phone.

Samsung and another chip company, SK Hynix, have seen their stock prices jump because of this growing partnership with OpenAI. But some people who watch the tech industry worry that if AI companies keep buying up chips this fast, regular phones and laptops could get more expensive for everybody else.

Words worth knowing
  • Memory chip (RAM) — A tiny part inside phones and computers that remembers what the device is doing while it's turned on, like a notebook it writes in and erases constantly.
  • Data center — A huge warehouse full of powerful computers, often as big as a football field, that companies use to run things like ChatGPT.

OpenAI's Chip Hunger Could Make Your Next Laptop More Expensive

Sam Altman, the CEO of OpenAI, flew to South Korea to meet around 50 executives from Samsung, asking for more memory chips to power ChatGPT and other AI systems. Along the way, he admitted something striking: OpenAI's own demand for memory chips could eventually swallow up to 40% of the world's entire supply — a prospect he himself called 'quite terrible' for anyone just trying to buy a phone or laptop.

Why this mattersThe price of your next phone or laptop might go up — not because of anything happening in the electronics industry, but because AI companies are buying up the same memory chips that go into consumer devices. It's a reminder that AI's infrastructure needs — chips, factories, electricity — ripple out into markets that have nothing to do with chatbots.
Read the whole story

Sam Altman didn't fly to Yongin, South Korea, for a casual chat. He went to meet roughly 50 executives from Samsung Group, one of the world's largest makers of memory chips, to lock down more supply for OpenAI's exploding computing needs. The meeting builds on a deal struck in October 2025, when Samsung and another Korean chip giant, SK Hynix, agreed to supply chips for OpenAI's 'Stargate' project — a massive buildout of data centers, the warehouses full of computers that run AI systems like ChatGPT.

The chips in question are DRAM and HBM — two types of memory chips. DRAM (dynamic random-access memory) is the short-term memory your computer uses to run programs; it's in every laptop, phone and game console you own. HBM (high-bandwidth memory) is a specialized, faster version increasingly used in the powerful computer chips that train and run AI. Both are now in what analysts call a structural shortage — not a temporary blip, but a long-term supply crunch, because AI companies are buying memory chips faster than factories can make them.

That's what made Altman's own comment so notable. He said OpenAI's computing needs could eventually absorb up to 40% of the world's entire RAM supply — RAM being the general term for the memory chips used in nearly all electronics. He called this 'quite terrible,' acknowledging that if OpenAI (or any AI company) buys up that much of the world's memory chips, there's simply less left for everyone else. Since phone and laptop makers use the exact same types of chips, a shortage driven by AI data centers can push up prices on the device in your pocket, even though you have no interest in AI at all.

Investors have noticed the opportunity, not just the risk. Shares in Samsung and SK Hynix have jumped sharply as their relationship with OpenAI has deepened, because selling enormous quantities of chips to the biggest AI companies is extremely profitable. But consumer advocates and some industry analysts warn that this profitability comes at a cost to ordinary buyers: as AI companies outbid everyone else for limited chip-making capacity, the price of memory for regular electronics rises too.

There's no sign OpenAI is pulling back. While some other AI companies have reportedly scaled back hardware commitments elsewhere, Altman's trip to Samsung signals the opposite: OpenAI is doubling down, trying to secure as much chip supply as it can get, for as long as the AI boom continues.

Words worth knowing
  • DRAM — A common type of memory chip found in nearly every computer, phone, and electronic device, used to temporarily store data while a program runs.
  • HBM (high-bandwidth memory) — A faster, specialized memory chip increasingly used in powerful AI computers, built to move huge amounts of data very quickly.
  • Data center — A large building full of computers and servers that companies use to run software, store information, and power AI systems like ChatGPT.
