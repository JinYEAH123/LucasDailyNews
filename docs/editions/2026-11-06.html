One Team Wins Control of Both Parts of Congress

On Tuesday, people across the United States voted for new members of Congress, the group of lawmakers who help run the country. The final counts are in, and one political party, the Democrats, won so many seats that they now control both halves of Congress. People are calling it one of the biggest wins in years.

Why this mattersCongress decides things that touch your everyday life, like how much money schools get or what rules protect the air and water. When one party suddenly controls both parts of Congress, it can move faster on the things it cares about. This is also a reminder that every single vote counts, like in Mississippi, where the race might be decided by just a couple thousand votes.
Read the whole story

Congress has two parts: the House of Representatives and the Senate. Think of them like two teams that both have to agree before a new law can happen. Every two years, some seats on these teams get voted on again, in elections called midterms.

This time, Democrats won more than 250 seats in the House, out of 435 total. That's like winning more than half of a huge classroom of 435 kids switching to your side. They also flipped at least 30 seats that used to belong to the other party, Republicans, and won 54 seats in the Senate, which only has 100 seats total.

Since the vote, there have been ripples. Harmeet Dhillon, a top official at the Justice Department, quit her job. Democrats are also arguing among themselves about who should lead them now that they're in charge. And in Mississippi, one Senate race is still too close to call, with fewer than 2,500 votes separating the two candidates. That's a small enough gap that officials might recount every single vote by hand.

People who study elections say this result is historic, meaning it doesn't happen often. Because Democrats now control both parts of Congress, they'll have much more power to decide what new laws get made, what investigations happen, and who gets approved as judges for the next two years.

Democrats Sweep Both Houses of Congress in a Landslide Midterm

Final counts from Tuesday's midterm elections show Democrats have won control of both the House of Representatives and the Senate, flipping at least 30 Republican-held House districts and pushing their total past 250 seats. The scale of the win is being called a historic landslide, and the fallout has already begun: a top Justice Department official has resigned, and House Democrats are now fighting among themselves over who gets to lead.

Why this mattersFor the next two years, almost nothing the president wants to turn into law can pass without Democratic cooperation, and almost any controversial decision their administration makes can be investigated by a Congress that no longer answers to them. That changes the pace and shape of everything from economic policy to court appointments — the kind of slow-moving shifts that quietly affect jobs, prices, and rights long after election night is forgotten.

When people vote in a midterm election — the vote held two years into a president's four-year term, choosing all of the House and about a third of the Senate — they're not picking a president. They're picking the people who write laws, approve budgets, and confirm the president's appointments to courts and federal agencies. This week, voters handed nearly all of that power to one party: Democrats now hold more than 250 of the House's 435 seats and 54 of the Senate's 100, a margin wide enough that political analysts on multiple networks are struggling to find a recent comparison.

Control of Congress is not symbolic. The party with the majority in each chamber picks the Speaker of the House and the Senate's committee chairs, which means they decide which bills even get a vote and which investigations get funded and staffed with subpoena power — the legal authority to force officials and companies to hand over documents or testify. A president whose own party has lost Congress typically finds it far harder to pass new laws, and far easier to find their administration under formal investigation.

One early sign of that shift: Harmeet Dhillon, who ran the Justice Department's Civil Rights Division — the office that enforces federal laws against discrimination in voting, housing, employment and policing — resigned this week. The timing, right after the election, has fueled speculation that she expects incoming Democratic committee chairs to scrutinize her division's record closely, though neither she nor the department has given a detailed public reason for the departure.

Not every result is settled. In Mississippi, Democrat Colom and Republican incumbent Hyde-Smith are separated by fewer than 2,500 votes out of hundreds of thousands cast, close enough that a recount is possible. The outcome matters beyond Mississippi: it could push the Democrats' Senate total even higher, or confirm a Republican hold on one of the few seats the party has left.

Back in Washington, House Democrats are now sorting out who actually runs the chamber they just won — multiple members are competing for leadership posts that come with real power over which bills advance and which investigations move forward. How that contest resolves will shape the party's priorities for the next two years, from the kind of oversight hearings they hold to the judicial confirmations — Senate votes to approve a president's nominees for federal judgeships — they're now positioned to slow or block.

  • Civil Rights Division — A Justice Department office responsible for enforcing federal laws against discrimination in areas like voting, housing, and policing.
  • Judicial confirmations — Senate votes required to approve a president's nominees for federal judgeships before they can take office.

Stock Markets Take a Big Tumble Over AI Worries

Other markets, including the Nasdaq (which tracks lots of tech companies), fell even more. Stocks Just Had Their Worst Day in Years — And It's All About AI

One day after US stock markets rallied on the midterm election results, they crashed. The Dow Jones fell more than 1,800 points, the S&P 500 dropped 4.4%, and the Nasdaq fell 5.5% — enough to put it into what traders call a 'correction.' The reason: investors suddenly fear the AI spending boom that's powered this year's stock gains is about to slow down.

Why this mattersStock markets aren't just numbers on a screen for investors — pensions, retirement accounts, and many parents' savings are tied up in index funds that track the Dow, S&P 500, and Nasdaq, so a drop like this can shrink real household wealth. It's also a preview of a bigger argument that's going to keep playing out: how much should governments slow down a technology boom to deal with its real costs, like strained power grids and water use, even if doing so rattles the economy.

On Thursday, US stock markets rose after midterm elections — the vote held roughly halfway through a president's four-year term, where Americans choose every member of the House of Representatives and about a third of the Senate, the two chambers of Congress that write US law. On Friday, those gains vanished and then some. The Dow Jones Industrial Average, an index that tracks 30 large American companies, dropped more than 1,800 points. The S&P 500, a broader index of 500 companies, fell 4.4%. The Nasdaq, which is heavy with technology stocks, fell 5.5% and slid into 'correction territory' — Wall Street's term for any drop of 10% or more from a recent peak.

The flip in mood came down to one thing: Congress. Democrats won control of it in the midterms, and investors now expect that new Congress, along with a wave of state and local ballot measures, to slow the breakneck construction of data centers — the enormous warehouses full of computer servers that run artificial intelligence programs like chatbots and image generators. Several communities have recently voted to restrict or block new data centers near them, often because they use huge amounts of electricity and water and can strain local power grids.

That matters to the stock market far more than it might sound like it should, because a handful of companies building and supplying this AI infrastructure — chipmakers, cloud computing firms, and the tech giants buying their products — have been responsible for an outsized share of this year's market gains. When those companies' stock prices climb because investors believe AI spending will keep growing for years, the whole market tends to rise with them. When investors start doubting that growth will continue as fast, or as unregulated, as expected, the same stocks can fall hard — and drag indexes like the Nasdaq down with them.

The sell-off didn't stay inside the United States. Markets in Asia and Europe opened sharply lower on Friday morning, following Wall Street's losses, because major stock markets are deeply linked: investors in Tokyo, London, and elsewhere often hold the same American tech stocks, or stocks in companies that supply them, so a shock in New York ripples outward within hours.

What analysts can't agree on is how serious this is. Some think it's a short, sharp reaction to political uncertainty — once it becomes clearer what the new Congress will actually do, prices could recover. Others think it's the start of something bigger: a reassessment of whether AI companies' valuations — the price investors have been willing to pay for their stock, based on expected future profits — were ever realistic in the first place. If they weren't, Friday's drop could be the first stage of a longer decline rather than a one-day scare.

Words worth knowing

  • Data center — A large building full of powerful computers that stores information and runs programs, including the artificial intelligence systems behind chatbots and image generators.
  • Valuation — How much a company's stock is worth based on what investors believe it will earn in the future, not necessarily what it earns right now.

California's Governor Changes the Punishment for More Than 500 People on Death Row

In California, a U.S. state, Governor Gavin Newsom just announced a huge change. More than 500 people who had been sentenced to death will now spend the rest of their lives in prison instead. They will never be executed. This is one of the biggest single changes to punishment in American history.

Why this mattersThis story is about what happens when a system might have made a mistake, and who gets to fix it. You've probably argued about a rule at home or school that felt unfair, and wondered who should be allowed to change it. Here, one person — the governor — decided for 500 people at once, which is exactly why people disagree so strongly.
Read the whole story

Some states allow the death penalty. That means a court can decide that someone who committed an extremely serious crime should be executed instead of sent to prison.

In 2019, California pressed pause on this. No executions happened, but the sentences stayed the same on paper. People were still technically waiting to be executed, even though it wasn't happening.

Now Newsom has gone further. He is officially changing the sentences themselves. More than 500 people — enough to fill about ten school buses — will now be sentenced to life in prison with no chance of ever being released, instead of death.

People disagree about this. Some say it ignores what juries and victims' families were promised. Others say it fixes a system that has sometimes made mistakes or treated people unfairly. Courts will likely be asked to decide if the governor was allowed to do this.

  • death penalty — A punishment where a court orders that someone be executed, used in some places for the most serious crimes.
  • commute a sentence — When a leader officially changes someone's punishment to something less severe, like changing a death sentence to life in prison.

California's Governor Just Emptied Death Row — With One Signature

Gavin Newsom, the governor of California, announced he will change the sentences of more than 500 people on the state's death row, from execution to life in prison with no chance of ever getting out. He did this using his own power as governor, not by passing a new law. It is one of the largest single acts against capital punishment in U.S. history.

Why this mattersThis isn't just a California story — it's a live example of how much power one elected official has to change a system the public thinks only courts or legislatures control. It also puts a real question in front of you: if a justice system can be proven to make fatal mistakes, how sure does it need to be before it's allowed to use an irreversible punishment?
Read the whole story

For years, California has had more people sentenced to death than any other U.S. state — over 600 of them, sitting in a prison near San Francisco. Executions there have already been on pause since 2019, when Newsom declared a moratorium, essentially a government-ordered freeze. No one was being killed by the state, but their death sentences were still technically on the books, waiting for a future governor to lift the freeze.

This announcement is different. A moratorium can be undone by the next person who takes office. A commutation — formally changing someone's sentence — cannot be easily reversed. By converting these sentences to life without parole, Newsom is trying to make the end of executions in California permanent, or at least much harder to undo, without ever asking the state legislature or voters to pass a law banning the death penalty outright.

That's exactly what makes this controversial before anyone even debates the death penalty itself. Governors have a legal power called clemency, which lets them pardon or reduce sentences in individual cases — it exists partly as a safety valve for cases where the justice system got something wrong. Using it on more than 500 people at once, instead of law by law or case by case, is a much larger use of that power than it was designed for, and lawyers expect this to be challenged in court by prosecutors, victims' families, or state legislators who argue that Newsom is rewriting criminal law by himself instead of letting elected lawmakers vote on it.

The case for his decision rests on two well-documented problems with capital punishment in America: wrongful convictions and racial disparity. Since the 1970s, more than 190 people sentenced to death nationwide have later been proven innocent and released — meaning the system has, provably, sent innocent people toward execution. Studies have also repeatedly found that defendants are more likely to receive a death sentence depending on the race of the victim and the defendant, not just the severity of the crime. Supporters of ending the death penalty say that a punishment this irreversible shouldn't exist in a system that makes documented, serious mistakes.

Opponents of the move make a different argument, and it isn't really about innocence or bias — it's about whose decision this should be. Many of these death sentences were handed down by juries made up of ordinary citizens, after trials where victims' families testified and prosecutors argued for the harshest punishment available under the law at the time. Critics say Newsom, a single elected official, is overriding thousands of jurors and victims' families who went through that process, on cases some of which involved torture, the murder of children, or multiple killings — without those families getting a vote.

What happens next will likely play out less in public opinion and more in court, where judges will decide whether a governor's clemency power can be used this broadly. However that goes, the political fight over capital punishment in America — which has been declining in use for two decades even as it remains legal in 27 states — just moved to a new stage.

Words worth knowing
  • commute (a sentence) — To officially change someone's punishment to something less severe, such as changing a death sentence to life in prison.
  • moratorium — A temporary, official pause on doing something, which can be reversed later by a different decision-maker.
  • clemency — The power a governor or president has to pardon someone or reduce their punishment, usually meant for correcting individual cases.
