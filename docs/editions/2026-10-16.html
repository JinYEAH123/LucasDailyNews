Chip Companies Lose More Than a Trillion Dollars in a Week

This week, the companies that make the computer chips powering artificial intelligence (AI) had a rough few days. Their stock prices fell hard, first in America and then in Asia. Together, these companies lost more than one trillion dollars in value — one of the biggest drops the tech world has seen in years.

Why this mattersLots of people have money in these companies without even knowing it — through their parents' retirement savings or the funds their family might invest in. AI chips also power things you might use every day, like voice assistants or video game graphics, so how these companies do affects what gets built next.
Read the whole story

On Thursday, shares in big American chip companies like Nvidia, AMD, Intel, and Dell all dropped. A share is like owning a tiny slice of a company — if you own one, you win a little when the company does well, and lose a little when it doesn't.

The trouble didn't stay in America. It spread to Asia too, hitting companies like SK Hynix and Samsung in South Korea, and SoftBank in Japan. All of these companies help build the chips and computers that run AI programs like chatbots and image generators.

Part of the reason is money. Banks had been lending huge piles of cash to AI companies so they could build giant computer centers. But in June, banks lent out $113 billion for AI projects — and by September, that had shrunk to just $23 billion. Banks got nervous about putting so much money into one industry.

Experts don't agree on what this means. Some think prices just got too high, too fast, and this is a normal cool-down. Others worry it's an early sign that the AI boom has been built on too much borrowed money, and trouble could be coming.

  • Debt — Debt is money a person or company borrows and promises to pay back later, usually with a bit extra added on top.

AI Stocks Just Lost $1 Trillion in a Week — Is the Bubble Popping?

On Thursday, October 15, shares in the companies building the world's artificial intelligence boom fell sharply, and the losses spread from Wall Street to Asia within hours. Nvidia, AMD, Intel and Dell dropped in the US; SK Hynix, Samsung and SoftBank fell in Seoul and Tokyo. Over the week, the 20 most valuable AI and chip companies on the planet lost more than $1 trillion in combined value — roughly the size of Australia's entire economy, gone from paper valuations in days.

Why this mattersIf your family has a pension, a retirement account, or savings invested in index funds — which most working adults do, often without realising it — AI stocks are almost certainly a meaningful chunk of that money, because a handful of tech giants now make up a huge share of major stock indexes. A slowdown in AI spending could also mean fewer new jobs in chipmaking, data centres and software over the next few years, in exactly the industries many young people are being told to aim for.
Read the whole story

For the past two years, AI companies have been the main engine pulling global stock markets upward. Nvidia alone, which makes the specialised chips that power AI systems like ChatGPT, became one of the most valuable companies in history almost entirely on the bet that every business on earth will need more of its hardware. That bet made a small number of chip and cloud-computing firms responsible for an outsized share of the gains in pension funds, index funds and retirement accounts worldwide. When those stocks fall together, it is not a niche tech story — it shows up in millions of ordinary investment accounts.

What makes this sell-off different from an ordinary bad week is what is happening underneath it, in the market for debt. Building AI infrastructure — the giant data centres full of chips that run these systems — costs enormous amounts of money, and companies have increasingly paid for it by borrowing rather than using their own cash. In June 2026, companies tied to AI issued $113 billion worth of new debt. By September, that figure had collapsed to just $23 billion. Banks, it seems, have grown nervous about having too much of their lending concentrated in one fast-moving, unproven sector, and have started pulling back.

That financing squeeze is the real story behind the stock price drops. If AI companies cannot borrow as easily to fund new data centres and chip orders, the whole buildout could slow down — and the stock prices of firms betting on endless AI growth would need to adjust downward to match a less certain future. Investors watched this happen in real time this week, and sold.

Analysts disagree sharply about what it means. Some argue this is simply a healthy correction: AI stock prices rose so fast over the past two years that a pullback was overdue and even welcome, clearing out speculative excess without threatening the underlying technology or the companies' long-term prospects. Others see something more worrying — an early warning that an industry which has financed its expansion heavily through debt and speculative investment, rather than proven profits, may be building on a foundation that cannot hold. Nobody will know for certain which reading is correct for months or years; stock market swings reveal what investors feel in the moment, not what is objectively true about a technology's future.

The reason this counts as a global story rather than just an American one is the supply chain running through it. Nvidia's chips are useless without memory chips from Samsung and SK Hynix in South Korea, and SoftBank, the Japanese investment giant, has poured enormous sums into AI ventures worldwide. A wobble in US chip stocks travels instantly to Seoul and Tokyo because these companies are links in the same production chain, not separate businesses that merely share a sector.

Words worth knowing
  • Market value (market capitalisation) — The total worth of a company's shares if you added them all up, used to compare how big companies are to each other.
  • Debt issuance — When a company raises money by selling bonds — essentially IOUs it promises to repay with interest — rather than using its own cash or selling shares.
  • Correction — A noticeable drop in stock prices after a period of rapid rises, which investors may see as prices returning to a more realistic level rather than a sign of deeper trouble.

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    It reflects real value

    • These companies dominate because they genuinely produce the technology nearly every other industry now depends on, so their size reflects real influence, not just hype.
    • Markets are free to reward the companies investors believe will matter most, and second-guessing that on principle is a form of picking winners that usually backfires.
    • Concentration has happened before with railroads, oil and the internet, and economies adjusted each time without the warnings proving fatal.

    • When a handful of companies make up a huge share of retirement funds and index funds, millions of ordinary people's financial futures ride on decisions made inside a few corporate boardrooms.
    • Heavy concentration means a shock to one sector — a bad earnings report, a financing crunch, a technological setback — can ripple through the entire global economy at once.
    • Spreading investment more broadly across many industries would make markets, and the people who depend on them, far more resilient to any single company's mistakes.

Why Trucks (and Your Groceries) Just Got More Expensive

A new U.S. government report shows that businesses are paying a lot more for fuel and supplies than they were a year ago. The biggest reason is diesel, the fuel that powers big trucks, which now costs about two-thirds more than it did before a war started in a country called Iran. When trucks cost more to run, moving almost everything gets more expensive too.

Why this mattersWhen trucks cost more to run, the price of things your family buys, like food and clothes, can creep up too. Your parents might notice it when they fill up the car or pay at the grocery store checkout.
Read the whole story

Think about the truck that brings food to your grocery store. It needs fuel called diesel to run. Right now, diesel costs $6.31 for one gallon. That is about 66% more than it cost before a war began in Iran back in February.

Every month, the U.S. government checks how much businesses pay for things before those things even reach a store shelf. This check has a name: the Producer Price Index, or PPI. In September, that number jumped faster than it has since 2022.

Trucks carry almost everything you touch: your cereal, your sneakers, the paper in your notebook. When truck fuel costs more, moving those things costs more too. Businesses then have to decide whether to absorb that extra cost or pass it on to shoppers.

This news arrives just weeks before an important U.S. election, when people vote for who runs the government. Rising prices make people frustrated, and some blame President Trump's administration for not doing more to control fuel costs, while others say a faraway war is mostly to blame.

Business Costs Just Jumped the Most in Three Years — Here's Why Your Parents Might Feel It First

On Thursday, October 15, the US government reported that the prices businesses pay each other for goods and services jumped 6.3% over the past year — the sharpest rise since 2022. A big part of the story is diesel fuel, which now costs $6.31 a gallon, about two-thirds more than it did before a war involving Iran began in February. The report lands just weeks before US midterm elections, turning the price of everyday things into a political battleground.

Why this mattersIf businesses are paying more to ship and make things, that cost usually finds its way to the price tags your family sees — on groceries, school supplies, even the cost of a bus or car ride. It also shapes a vote your household may be thinking about: whether rising prices should count against the people currently in charge, even when a war overseas is part of the cause.
Read the whole story

Every month, the US Bureau of Labor Statistics — a government agency whose entire job is to measure things like jobs, wages, and prices — publishes a number called the Producer Price Index, or PPI. It tracks what businesses charge each other for goods and services before those products ever reach a store shelf: the price a bakery pays for flour, the price a trucking company pays to ship it, the price a factory pays for steel. Think of it as a preview of costs working their way through the economy, one step before you see them at the checkout.

This month's preview was not gentle. The PPI rose 1.3% in September alone, and 6.3% over the past year — the fastest annual climb since 2022, when inflation was a dominant national story. One number stood out as a major driver: diesel fuel, the fuel that powers most trucks, trains, and construction equipment, is now selling for $6.31 a gallon. That is roughly 66% higher than it was before a war involving Iran broke out in February, a conflict that has rattled global oil markets and made shipping fuel more expensive almost everywhere.

Diesel matters more than its price tag suggests, because nearly everything you buy — groceries, clothes, furniture, parts for a phone — spends part of its life on a truck. When diesel gets more expensive, the cost of moving goods rises too, and that cost tends to ripple outward into the prices businesses charge each other, which is exactly what the PPI is designed to catch early.

The timing makes this more than an economics story. The report arrived weeks before the US midterm elections, the nationwide vote that will decide control of Congress, and it has sharpened scrutiny of the Trump administration's handling of fuel and consumer costs. Rising prices are one of the most consistently painful issues for any sitting government, because almost every voter notices them directly, without needing to understand an index or a report to feel the squeeze.

What is not settled is what happens next. The PPI number itself is not in dispute — it comes from a single, consistently reported government release. The real disagreement is about pass-through: how much of this rise in business costs will actually show up in the prices you and your family pay, and how quickly. Some economists expect companies to absorb part of the hit to protect sales; others expect it to land squarely on consumers within months. Layered on top of that is a political question with no clean answer: how much credit or blame voters will assign to a government for a global oil price shock it did not start, but may or may not have insulated the country from.

Words worth knowing
  • Producer Price Index (PPI) — A monthly US government measure of the prices businesses charge each other for goods and services, before those products reach a store or customer.
  • Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) — The US government agency that collects and publishes official data on prices, jobs, and wages.
  • Pass-through — The process by which higher costs for businesses eventually turn into higher prices for the things ordinary people buy.

  1. When a war overseas sends fuel prices soaring, should the government step in to control prices, or let the market adjust on its own?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Step in

    • Sudden price spikes from a war hit families and small businesses that have no way to plan around them or absorb the shock.
    • Temporary subsidies or tax relief can keep goods and people moving without choking off ordinary economic activity.
    • Waiting for the market to self-correct can take many months, and in that time struggling households fall behind on rent, bills, and basic costs.

    Let the market adjust

    • Price controls often cause shortages, because sellers lose the incentive to supply goods at an artificially low price.
    • Subsidies cost taxpayers money and are politically difficult to remove once people get used to them.
    • High prices send a signal that encourages people to conserve fuel and invest in alternatives, which solves the underlying problem faster than a government cap.

  2. Is it fair for voters to blame the government in power for price rises caused mainly by a war it did not start?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes, hold them accountable

    • Governments choose how much to shield their economy from outside shocks, through reserves, trade deals, or energy policy, so inaction is itself a choice.
    • Voting is one of the only tools ordinary people have to express how economic hardship is affecting their daily lives.
    • Long-term dependence on vulnerable fuel sources is often the result of policy decisions made well before any crisis hits.

    No, it's unfair

    • No government controls decisions made by foreign powers or global oil markets, no matter how strong its domestic policy is.
    • Punishing leaders for events outside their control discourages anyone from taking tough, necessary action during a crisis.
    • Judging a government purely on outcomes it cannot fully control turns elections into blame exercises rather than debates over real policy choices.

Ukraine Strikes Russian Oil Plant After a Truce Falls Apart

Early Thursday, Ukraine said it hit an oil refinery deep inside Russia. This happened just hours after Ukraine's president said a short pause in attacking power plants and energy sites was over, because Russia had kept attacking anyway. Overnight, Russia had launched hundreds of drones at Ukraine. The fighting is getting more intense, and leaders on both sides are arguing about who broke their word first.

Why this mattersWhen two countries break an agreement and the fighting grows bigger, it does not stay in just one place. Other countries, including the United States and nations in Europe, get pulled into deciding how much help to send and what rules to follow. Wars like this can also affect prices for things like fuel, because oil refineries like the one that was hit make the fuel that goes into cars, trucks, and planes everywhere.
Read the whole story

Ukraine and Russia had agreed to stop attacking each other's energy buildings, like power plants and fuel stations, for a short time. Ukraine's president, Volodymyr Zelenskyy, said Russia never really stopped. So he said the truce, meaning a short break from fighting, was finished.

Hours later, Ukraine struck an oil refinery, a huge factory that turns crude oil into fuel, in a part of Russia called Ryazan. That same night, Russia had sent wave after wave of flying robot bombs called drones toward Ukraine. Ukrainian officials counted more than 850 drones fired since the day before, that's more drones than students in several large schools combined.

On top of the fighting, Zelenskyy said he felt let down by the President of the United States, Donald Trump. The two countries are disagreeing about Ukraine's access to a system called Starshield, a network of satellites that lets soldiers send messages and information quickly, even in remote places.

So right now there are two arguments happening at once: a bigger, bloodier fight on the battlefield, and a trickier argument between Ukraine and one of its biggest supporters, the United States.

Ukraine Strikes Russian Oil Refinery as Truce Collapses and Drone War Intensifies

Ukraine says it hit an oil refinery deep inside Russia early Thursday, just hours after its president declared a brief truce over energy attacks dead. The strike came amid one of the heaviest waves of drone attacks of the war, with Ukraine reporting more than 850 Russian drones launched since Wednesday. The military escalation is unfolding alongside a separate political rift between Kyiv and Washington over satellite access.

Why this mattersWars that intensify and alliances that wobble rarely stay contained to the countries directly involved — they shape fuel prices, defense budgets, and how safe European governments feel, all of which eventually reach family finances and national politics far from the front line. The dispute over Starshield is also a preview of a bigger question every country allied with the US now has to ask: how much can you plan around support that depends on one president's goodwill rather than a fixed national commitment?
Read the whole story

Early Thursday, October 15, Ukraine's military said it had struck an oil refinery in Russia's Ryazan region, a facility several hundred kilometers from the front line that turns crude oil into the fuel that runs tanks, planes, and the rest of Russia's war machine. The strike came only hours after President Volodymyr Zelenskyy announced that a brief, informal truce over attacks on energy infrastructure — power plants, refineries, pipelines — was over, because he said Russia had never actually stopped hitting Ukrainian energy sites in the first place.

The truce's collapse came amid an extraordinary surge in drone warfare. Ukrainian officials said Russia launched hundreds of drones overnight, pushing the total fired since early Wednesday past 850 — a figure Russian authorities have not confirmed, and that no independent outlet has verified either. The drones involved are often not the small camera-equipped kind most people picture; many are long-range exploding aircraft the size of a small plane, flown in swarms specifically to overwhelm air defenses that can only shoot down so many targets at once.

Zelenskyy paired this military news with a political grievance. He said he felt 'betrayed' by President Trump over Ukraine's access to Starshield, a secure military satellite communications system — a more heavily encrypted relative of Starlink, the satellite internet service Ukrainian forces have relied on throughout the war to coordinate drone strikes and keep front-line units in contact when phone networks go down. What exactly triggered the dispute wasn't fully detailed, but the complaint points to a deeper unease in Kyiv: that American support, especially anything requiring Trump's personal approval, no longer feels guaranteed from one month to the next.

None of this is happening in isolation. Washington remains Ukraine's single most important military backer, supplying weapons, intelligence, and — through companies like SpaceX — the communications systems that modern Ukrainian warfare runs on. A public rift between Zelenskyy and Trump, even over something as specific as satellite access, tells Moscow that the coalition backing Ukraine might be fraying, and tells European governments they may need to shoulder more of the burden themselves.

Both sides describe the collapsed truce differently, which is itself part of the story. Kyiv says Moscow never honored it and kept striking energy targets throughout; Russian officials have not offered an independently verified account of their own conduct during the same period. What's not in dispute is the scale of what followed: a drone exchange larger than almost anything seen earlier in the war, arriving at the exact moment the alliance supporting Ukraine looked shakiest.

Words worth knowing
  • Refinery — A facility that processes crude oil into usable fuels like gasoline, diesel, and jet fuel.
  • Truce — A temporary, often informal agreement to stop a specific kind of attack without ending the war itself.
  • Starshield — A secure, military-grade satellite communications system, built as a more encrypted relative of the Starlink internet service.

  1. If one side breaks a promise during a fight, is it fair for the other side to stop keeping their promise too?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes, it's fair

    • If you keep a promise alone while the other side breaks theirs, you end up worse off and they get away with it.
    • Promises during a fight are usually meant to go both ways, so if one side quits, the deal is already broken.
    • Sticking to a one-sided promise can make the side that kept it look weak, which might invite more attacks.

    No, it's not fair to just copy them

    • Breaking your own promise just because someone else broke theirs can make things spiral and get worse for everyone.
    • Someone has to be the one to stop the cycle, or the fighting never ends.
    • Keeping a promise even when it's hard is what makes your word mean something the next time you make one.

  2. Should a country that is helping another country in a war get to decide exactly what equipment that country can use?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes, the helper gets a say

    • If you're giving someone a powerful tool, like satellite communication, you have a right to control how it's used so it isn't misused.
    • The helping country might worry about being dragged deeper into the war than it wants to be.
    • Rules on equipment can stop a small disagreement from turning into a much bigger, more dangerous conflict.

    No, the country being helped should decide

    • The country actually fighting the war knows best what it needs to defend itself.
    • If help comes with too many strings attached, it might arrive too late or not be useful when it's needed most.
    • Telling a country how to defend itself can feel like the helper cares more about control than about truly helping.
