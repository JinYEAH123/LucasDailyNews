The US and Iran Just Fired Missiles at Each Other Directly — Here's Why That's a Big Deal

For the first time in about a month, the United States and Iran didn't just trade threats — they fired real missiles at real military targets. Iran's Revolutionary Guards, its most powerful military force, launched strikes at US bases in Jordan and the United Arab Emirates, calling it payback for an American attack on Iranian rocket launchers days earlier. Both sides say the other started it, and the fight is now happening near one of the most important oil routes on Earth.

Why this mattersIf fighting near the Strait of Hormuz disrupts oil shipments even briefly, prices at the gas pump and the cost of goods that arrive by ship can rise for people everywhere, not just in the Middle East. It's also a reminder that conflicts far from home rarely stay contained — six months of tension between two countries can suddenly turn into direct strikes with almost no warning, and once missiles start flying, both sides find it harder to back down without looking weak.

This isn't a new conflict — the US and Iran have been locked in tension for about six months, with neither side backing down. But most of that time, the two countries have avoided striking each other's forces directly. That changed this week. Iran's Revolutionary Guards, known as the IRGC — a branch of Iran's military that answers to the country's supreme religious leader rather than its elected government, and that the US has long treated as its most dangerous adversary in the region — fired missiles at American military targets in Jordan and the UAE.

Iran says this was retaliation. The US had struck what it called Iranian rocket launchers, which Washington claimed were being used to fire sea mines — underwater bombs designed to sink or damage ships — into the Strait of Hormuz. That's the narrow stretch of water between Iran and the Arabian Peninsula through which roughly one-fifth of all the oil shipped in the world passes every day. Iranian forces have specifically pointed to a Sunday-night US attack on Larak Island, an island Iran controls near the Strait, as the trigger for their strikes on Jordan.

So the sequence, as both sides describe it, looks like this: the US struck Iranian rocket positions it said were mining the Strait; Iran struck back at US bases it says were behind that attack. Each side is calling its own strike a response to the other's aggression — which is common in conflicts like this, and part of why they're so hard to end. There is no single moment either side agrees was 'the start.'

Adding to the confusion, President Trump posted video on social media claiming to show 'Kharg Island being blown to smithereens' — a reference to a major Iranian oil export terminal. But no independent news organization has been able to confirm that the footage shows what he says it shows, or that the strike happened as described. Until outside reporters or satellite images verify it, that claim should be treated as unconfirmed — a reminder that in fast-moving conflicts, even official statements from a country's leader aren't automatically fact.

What makes this exchange more dangerous than previous rounds is the target: the Strait of Hormuz itself. Any real disruption there doesn't just affect Iran and the US — it affects the price of oil and, through that, the price of nearly everything shipped, manufactured, or driven anywhere in the world. A shipping lane that carries a fifth of global oil is not the kind of thing either side can afford to close for long, which is exactly why some analysts see the strikes as posturing meant to demonstrate strength without triggering a full war — and why others see them as a fuse that's already been lit.

  • Strait of Hormuz — A narrow sea passage between Iran and the Arabian Peninsula through which about a fifth of the world's oil shipments travel, making it one of the most strategically important waterways on Earth.

A Mountain Flood Disaster Leaves Nearly 1,000 Dead, Thousands Still Missing

A giant wall of water, mud, and ice smashed through mountain villages in Nepal and Tibet, a region high in the Himalaya mountains that is controlled by China. More than 950 people have died, and over 4,400 are still missing. Rescue teams are struggling to reach the wrecked villages, and nobody agrees yet on the exact numbers.

Why this mattersGlaciers are giant blocks of ice sitting on mountains, and as the world gets warmer, more of that ice is melting into lakes that can burst without warning. This disaster shows how a place far away, high in the mountains, can suddenly affect thousands of families and even the electricity that powers nearby cities.

A wall of water, mud, and ice crashed down through mountain valleys in Nepal and Tibet, smashing villages, roads, and power stations. It happened so fast that people had almost no time to run.

More than 950 people have died. That is like every student in three or four big schools disappearing in just a few days. Over 4,400 people are still missing, which is more people than live in a small town.

Nobody has exact numbers yet. Rescue workers are trying to reach faraway mountain villages, but many roads and bridges are broken or gone. Different news reports give different totals, because things keep changing and it is hard to count people in such remote places.

Scientists think it started when a mountain lake made of melted glacier ice suddenly burst, like a giant bathtub tipping over. The water rushed downhill, picking up mud and rocks and growing bigger, smashing everything below it, including hydropower stations that use river water to make electricity.

Flash Floods Tear Through the Himalayas, Leaving Nearly a Thousand Dead and Thousands Still Missing

A wall of water, ice and rock smashed through mountain valleys spanning Nepal and Tibet, killing more than 950 people and leaving over 4,400 unaccounted for. The disaster hit hardest at hydropower dam sites, where hundreds of construction workers are still missing. Even now, nobody agrees on the exact numbers — different governments, agencies and news outlets are reporting different totals, because rescue teams still can't reach many of the areas that were hit.

Why this mattersWarming global temperatures are causing Himalayan glaciers to melt and retreat faster, which scientists say makes glacial lake outburst floods like this one more frequent and more dangerous — not just in Nepal and Tibet, but in mountain regions worldwide, including places that build hydropower dams to generate clean energy. It's also a reminder that in the first days after a major disaster, the numbers you read in the news are often provisional best guesses, not settled facts, and that gap between confirmed and missing can hide a lot of uncertainty about how bad things really are.
Read the whole story

High in the Himalayas, water builds up behind natural dams made of ice, rock and debris left over from retreating glaciers. These are called glacial lakes, and normally they just sit there, holding back meltwater. But when one bursts — sometimes because of an earthquake, sometimes because the ice dam simply gives way under pressure — the result is a flash flood unlike almost any other: a sudden, violent surge of water, ice chunks and boulders that can travel for miles down a narrow valley before anyone downstream has time to react.

That is what appears to have happened across the border region shared by Nepal and Tibet, the Chinese-controlled region that sits on the other side of the mountains. The flood tore through villages and, critically, through hydropower projects — dams and power stations built specifically to make electricity by channelling river water through turbines. These sites are usually built in steep valleys precisely because that's where the water flows fastest, which is efficient for generating power but puts them directly in the path of any flood coming down from higher ground. A private industry association representing power companies says at least 900 workers are missing across 12 damaged hydropower projects alone — meaning the death toll could still climb sharply once rescue teams reach isolated work camps.

The numbers being reported right now genuinely don't agree with each other, and that mismatch tells its own story about how chaotic the situation still is. Chinese authorities in Tibet have confirmed 16 deaths and 546 people missing on their side of the border. Meanwhile, one outlet has reported Nepal's death toll alone at 903 — a figure that doesn't line up cleanly with the combined cross-border total of over 950 that other reports are using. This isn't necessarily anyone lying or exaggerating. It reflects the reality of disaster reporting in remote, mountainous terrain: phone lines are down, roads are washed out, and rescue workers are still physically walking or flying into villages to find out who survived and who didn't.

Rescue operations are being slowed by the same geography that made the flood so destructive in the first place. Steep valleys and destroyed roads mean helicopters are often the only way in, and even they are limited by weather and altitude. Every day that passes without contact with an isolated village or work camp means the missing count could shift — up if bodies are found, down if survivors turn up alive but cut off from communication, which happens more often than you'd expect in disasters like this.

This is being described as one of the deadliest natural disasters of the year worldwide, and it's a disaster that crosses an international border between two countries — Nepal and China — that don't always share information smoothly, which is part of why getting one clean, agreed-upon number has proven so difficult.

Words worth knowing
  • Glacial lake outburst flood (GLOF) — A sudden, violent flood caused when a lake held back by ice or rock debris near a melting glacier bursts, releasing huge amounts of water, ice and rock downhill all at once.
  • Hydropower project — A power station and dam built on a river to generate electricity by using flowing water to spin turbines, usually located in fast-flowing mountain valleys.
  • Death toll vs. missing — The 'death toll' counts confirmed deaths, while 'missing' counts people who haven't been found alive or dead — a number that usually shrinks or grows sharply as rescue teams reach cut-off areas.

A New Telescope Blasts Off to Hunt for the Universe's Missing 95 Percent

Early this morning, a NASA space telescope the size of a tour bus rocketed off a Florida launch pad on top of a SpaceX Falcon Heavy rocket. Named the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, it's now on a million-mile trip to a permanent post in space, where it will spend years trying to pin down two of the biggest mysteries in physics: dark matter and dark energy. The mission cost roughly $4 billion, making it one of NASA's largest astronomy investments in years.

Why this mattersThis telescope won't change anything about your daily life directly, but it's a bet that understanding the universe's most basic rules — what it's made of, how it's expanding — is worth years of work and billions of dollars before anyone knows what the answer will be. It's also a reminder that huge questions in science often take huge, slow-moving projects: Roman was in development for over a decade before this morning's launch, and its own results will take years to arrive.
Read the whole story

At 7:26 a.m. Eastern Time, a rocket carrying an 18,000-pound telescope lifted off the launch pad. That's roughly the weight of two loaded school buses, and about the physical size of one too. The rocket, a Falcon Heavy built by SpaceX, is one of the most powerful launch vehicles flying today, and it needed all of that power to send something this heavy on a journey of about a million miles — roughly four times farther than the Moon.

The telescope's real job starts once it arrives. Scientists have known for decades that the matter we can actually see — stars, planets, gas, everything that gives off or reflects light — makes up only a small slice of everything in the universe. The rest is split between two things nobody has ever directly observed: dark matter, an invisible substance that seems to hold galaxies together through gravity, and dark energy, a mysterious force that appears to be pushing the universe to expand faster and faster over time. Together, these two unknowns are thought to account for roughly 95 percent of everything that exists, which means most of the universe is, quite literally, a mystery scientists are still trying to name.

Roman isn't the first telescope built to study space, but it's designed to do something existing telescopes can't do well: cover enormous amounts of sky quickly. The Hubble Space Telescope, still operating after more than three decades, produces incredibly detailed images but of small patches of sky at a time — like photographing a crowd one face at a time. Roman is built with a much wider field of view, letting it photograph patches of sky about 100 times larger than Hubble can in a single shot. That means scientists can map the large-scale structure of the universe — how galaxies and dark matter are distributed across huge volumes of space — in a way that was never practical before.

The telescope's name honors Nancy Grace Roman, who became NASA's first chief astronomer in the 1960s, back when the idea of putting a telescope in space at all was still a hard sell inside the agency. She was one of the key people who pushed for and helped design what eventually became the Hubble Space Telescope, a project that reshaped modern astronomy. Naming this new mission after her is a nod to the fact that a single mission's usefulness often outlives its own generation of scientists — Hubble launched in 1990 and is still producing discoveries today.

Getting a mission like this into orbit required years of coordination between NASA, which designed and paid for the science instruments, and SpaceX, which built and flew the rocket. That kind of partnership between a government agency and a private company has become the normal way NASA gets large payloads into space now, a shift from the decades when NASA mostly built and flew its own rockets.

Words worth knowing
  • Dark matter — An invisible substance that scientists believe makes up most of the material in the universe, detected only by its gravitational pull on stars and galaxies, never by light.
  • Dark energy — An unexplained force that seems to be causing the universe to expand at an ever-increasing speed.
  • Falcon Heavy — A powerful, reusable rocket built by SpaceX, used here to launch a heavy scientific spacecraft far beyond Earth's orbit.
