The US Treasury Secretary, Scott Bessent, announced a huge new sanctions push against Iran, and gave it a name that sounds like a military operation: 'Operation Economic Outcast.' He called it an 'economic D-Day' — a reference to the massive Allied invasion of Nazi-occupied France in 1944, one of the turning points of World War II. It's a big claim: Bessent is saying this campaign is meant to be just as decisive, only fought with financial rules instead of soldiers.

The plan targets the networks that keep Iran's economy connected to the rest of the world — the ships that carry its oil, the gold it uses to store value, the planes it flies, and even the cryptocurrency transfers it uses to move money around sanctions. Those networks run through places like the United Arab Emirates, Hong Kong, mainland China, Singapore, and Switzerland — financial hubs that Iran has used as pass-throughs to keep trading despite years of existing sanctions.

The tool doing the heavy lifting is called a 'secondary sanction.' Normal US sanctions only stop American companies from doing business with a target. Secondary sanctions go further: they threaten any foreign bank or company, anywhere in the world, with being cut off from the US financial system if it keeps trading with Iran. Because so much of global trade and banking runs through US dollars, that threat carries real weight — even for companies that have never set foot in America.

The most striking target is China, which buys roughly 90 percent of Iran's oil exports and is by far its biggest customer. Bessent said flatly that China would not get an exemption this time — a notable statement, since China is also the world's second-largest economy and a country the US usually treats carefully. President Trump has reportedly been personally calling other world leaders, urging them to cut their own ties with Tehran.

The timing matters too. This sanctions push comes about six months after a period of open conflict between the US and Israel on one side and Iran on the other. A deadline for a ceasefire — an agreement to stop the fighting — passed without both sides reaching a deal. In response, Iran has threatened to seize ships passing through the Strait of Hormuz, a narrow strip of water between Iran and the Arabian Peninsula that carries about a fifth of the world's oil every single day. Blocking or threatening that passage would ripple through gas prices worldwide.

What nobody can say for certain yet is whether any of this will actually work. China has weathered plenty of US pressure before, and both Iran and China have spent years building workarounds — disguised shipping routes, renamed tankers, non-dollar payment systems — specifically designed to dodge sanctions like these. Some analysts think this round, because it targets the entire supply chain at once, could genuinely bite. Others think it will mostly add cost and hassle without stopping the oil from flowing.

Chip stocks did not fall because of one bad piece of news — they fell because investors are getting nervous about a story they have been telling themselves for two years. On Tuesday, Intel dropped about 5%, AMD about 4%, and Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (TSMC), which makes the chips that power almost every major AI system, about 3%. The slide came a day before Nvidia — the company whose graphics chips are the closest thing the AI industry has to a bottleneck — was due to report earnings on Wednesday, a report the entire sector treats as a referendum on whether AI spending is still worth the money.

Adding to the unease were reports that Nvidia may raise prices on its AI servers by more than 15% for its largest customers. Those customers are the 'hyperscalers' — Microsoft, Google, Amazon, Meta — companies buying chips by the tens of thousands to build the data centers that run AI models. A price increase that size would boost Nvidia's own revenue, but it also raises the cost of the very infrastructure those companies are betting billions on, at a moment when some investors are already asking how long they can keep spending before the payoff shows up.

On the other side of the Pacific, Alibaba's Hong Kong-listed shares fell for a related but distinct reason. The company announced a share placement — selling $10 billion of new stock directly to investors — specifically to fund more AI infrastructure spending. The timing mattered: the announcement came just days after Alibaba reported that its quarterly profit had fallen 75% year-over-year, a drop the company attributed largely to the enormous sums it has poured into AI data centers and chip purchases.

Put next to each other, the two moves describe the same anxiety from opposite ends of the supply chain. In the US, investors are asking whether chipmakers can keep raising prices and shipping volume without the AI boom itself running out of steam. In China, they're asking whether a company can keep raising money to spend on AI while its actual profits shrink, and whether shareholders should be diluted — meaning each existing share represents a smaller slice of the company — to keep the buildout going.

What ties both stories together is a phrase traders use casually but rarely define: the 'AI trade.' It refers to the belief, priced into stock valuations across the tech sector, that AI will eventually generate enough revenue to justify the trillions being spent on chips, data centers, and electricity to run them. That belief has driven much of the stock market's gains over the past two years. Tuesday's sell-off doesn't prove the belief is wrong — but it shows that even a modest wobble, a pricing rumor here, a bad earnings report there, is enough to make investors ask the question out loud.

The lawsuit does not accuse Meta of publishing anything illegal. The states' lawyers argue these are not neutral technical choices but deliberate ones, made because a more engaged user is a more profitable user, and that Meta made them knowing the toll on younger, still-developing minds.

Meta's defense rests on a different framing: that these features are standard to nearly every app teenagers use, that plenty of research on social media and teen mental health is mixed or inconclusive, and that holding a company liable for how people choose to use a product opens a legal door nobody can predict the end of. The gap between $1.4 trillion and $200 billion isn't a rounding error — it reflects two different ways of counting. What makes this trial more than a dispute over money is the theory driving it: a product-design theory, borrowed from cases against carmakers and drug manufacturers, applied for the first time at real scale to a social media platform.
