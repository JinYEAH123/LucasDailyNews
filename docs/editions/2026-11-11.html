The Boss of a Giant Chip Company Faces Tough Questions in Washington

Jensen Huang runs Nvidia, a company that makes some of the world's most powerful computer chips. Now some US lawmakers want him to explain himself to Congress, and some are even asking him to resign. At the same time, records show a Chinese university that is supposed to be banned from buying these chips got some anyway, and the news has shaken stock markets around the world.

Why this mattersThe chips this story is about help power things you might use every day, like video games, voice assistants, and smart devices. When people worry that powerful technology might reach the wrong hands, or that a company's leader can't be trusted, it can shake the whole stock market and change how governments make rules about technology.

Nvidia makes super-powerful computer chips. These chips help computers 'think' fast, which is what makes artificial intelligence, like chatbots and smart robots, possible. Jensen Huang is the boss of Nvidia, and his company is one of the most valuable in the world.

Some US government leaders are worried about Huang. Reports say he had a personal relationship with someone connected to Chinese intelligence work, which is like spying. Now members of Congress, the group of lawmakers who help run the US government, want to question Huang directly. Some are even calling for him to step down from his job.

At the same time, records show that a Chinese university bought some of Nvidia's newest chips last month. That university is on a US list of places banned from buying these chips, because it has ties to China's military. This makes people wonder whether the rules meant to stop risky chip sales are actually working.

All this worry rattled stock markets hard. Investors who own pieces of chip companies got scared and started selling fast. If you saved one hundred dollars every single day, it would take you millions of years to save as much money as chip companies lost in just a few days this week.

Nvidia's CEO Faces Calls to Resign as a Security Scandal Wipes Trillions Off Tech Stocks

Jensen Huang, the billionaire chief executive of Nvidia — the company that makes most of the world's advanced AI chips — is under growing pressure in Washington after disclosures about a personal relationship with someone US officials allege works for Chinese intelligence. Members of Congress are now publicly demanding his resignation, and there is growing momentum to force him to testify under subpoena. At the same time, records show a Chinese university with military ties bought Nvidia's advanced H200 chips last month despite being on a US government blacklist. The controversy helped trigger one of the largest one-day value losses in chip-stock history.

Why this mattersThe chips at the center of this story are the same ones used to train the AI systems increasingly built into phones, search engines, and schoolwork tools, so decisions about who gets to buy them — and who's trusted to run the company that makes them — shape what technology looks like for years to come. It's also a case study in how fast trust can evaporate: a single set of allegations and one blacklist breach erased trillions of dollars in paper wealth in days, affecting retirement funds and investment accounts far beyond anyone who has ever met Jensen Huang.

Jensen Huang built Nvidia into the most valuable chip company on Earth by making the processors that power almost all serious artificial intelligence research, from chatbots to military simulations. That made him one of the most powerful unelected people in the global economy — and now one of the most scrutinized. The current uproar began with reports of a personal relationship between Huang and a person US officials allege is connected to Chinese intelligence services. Nvidia has not been accused of a crime, but lawmakers argue that even the appearance of a conflict of interest is dangerous when the company's products are central to a technology race the US government considers a matter of national security.

The stakes are higher because of what Nvidia actually sells. Washington has spent years restricting which AI chips American companies can export to China, worried that China's military or security services could use the most powerful ones. Those rules work through something called an export blacklist — a formal list, maintained by the Commerce Department, of foreign organizations that US companies are banned from selling certain technology to, usually because of ties to a foreign military or intelligence agency. Records obtained by reporters show that a Chinese university with documented military connections bought Nvidia's advanced H200 chips last month — despite being on that exact blacklist. Nobody has fully explained how the sale happened, which is why lawmakers are now asking whether export controls are being enforced at all, or merely written down on paper.

That question has landed on Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, whose department is supposed to police these sales, and by extension on the Trump administration, which has faced criticism for being inconsistent about how strictly to limit chip exports to China — sometimes tightening the rules, sometimes loosening them when it suited broader trade negotiations. Congress is now pressing the administration to explain the gap, and some members want Huang himself brought in through a subpoena — a legal order compelling someone to testify or hand over documents, which Congress can issue to force an unwilling witness to answer questions under oath.

The financial markets reacted to all of this almost immediately. By Tuesday, November 10, a Yahoo Finance index tracking 62 chip-related stocks had lost about $4.8 trillion in combined value, the Nasdaq 100 index had lost roughly $4.7 trillion, and the broader S&P 500 — which tracks 500 of the largest US companies — had lost about $2.7 trillion. These numbers describe market capitalization, meaning the total value investors place on a company's shares; when that value drops this fast and this far, it means investors collectively decided, almost overnight, that chip companies are riskier bets than they thought the week before. To put the scale in perspective, $4.8 trillion is larger than the entire yearly economic output of countries like Germany or Japan — wiped from paper valuations in a matter of days, not because factories stopped working, but because confidence did.

What happens next is genuinely unresolved. Huang has not said whether he will resign, and it is unclear whether Congress will actually issue a subpoena or whether political pressure alone will force the administration or Nvidia's board to act first. The company's own defense so far has been that selling chips is not the same as controlling who buys them once they leave the warehouse, and that Washington's rules — not Nvidia's conduct — are what failed in the university case. Lawmakers reject that framing, arguing that a company this central to national security cannot treat compliance as someone else's problem.

Words worth knowing
  • export blacklist — An official US government list of foreign organizations — often tied to a military or intelligence agency — that American companies are legally forbidden from selling certain sensitive technology to.
  • subpoena — A legal order, often issued by Congress, that forces someone to testify or hand over documents whether they want to or not.
  • market capitalization — The total value investors place on all of a company's shares combined, which can rise or fall sharply based on confidence rather than any change in the company's actual operations.

China Sold So Much Stuff Abroad, It Broke Its Own Record

China just finished selling way more goods to other countries than it bought from them. The gap between what it sold and what it bought topped one trillion dollars in the first ten months of this year. No country has ever reached that huge number so fast.

Why this mattersA lot of things in your house — maybe your shoes, your toys, parts of your tablet — were probably made in China. When one country sells so much more than it buys, it can lead to arguments between governments about fairness, jobs, and taxes on goods crossing borders. The United States and China have been arguing about this kind of thing for years, and this new record adds fuel to that argument.
Read the whole story

Factories across China make phones, toys, clothes, and machines. Many of these get shipped to other countries, like the United States and countries in Europe.

This year, China sold 35% more stuff to other countries than it did last year. That's like if you sold lemonade last summer and this summer you sold more than a third again as many cups.

But China also bought more from other countries too. Its purchases jumped 41.6%, the fastest increase in fifteen years. Still, China sold so much more than it bought that the leftover gap grew past one trillion dollars. One trillion is a number so big that if you counted one dollar every second, non-stop, it would take you over 31,000 years to reach it.

This is the second year in a row China has crossed that one-trillion-dollar mark. Nobody disputes these numbers — they come from China's own government records, and news organizations around the world have checked and reported the same figures.

China's Trade Surplus Blows Past $1 Trillion — the Fastest It's Ever Happened

China just posted the largest trade surplus in its history, crossing $1 trillion for the first ten months of 2026 faster than in any previous year. Exports jumped 35.2% compared with a year earlier, beating what economists had forecast, while imports surged even more — up 41.6%, the sharpest rise in over a decade. The numbers come from China's own government customs office, though several independent outlets have reported the same figures without disputing them.

Why this mattersTrade numbers this large eventually show up in ordinary places — the price tag on an imported phone or pair of shoes, whether a factory in your town is hiring or cutting shifts, and how loudly politicians argue for new tariffs the next time you're watching the news. The tug-of-war over who 'wins' global trade shapes decisions made in Washington and Brussels for years afterward, including ones that affect jobs and prices where you live.
Read the whole story

China's customs authority — the government agency that tracks everything crossing the country's borders — released figures this week showing the country's trade surplus has already topped $1 trillion for 2026, with two months of data still to come. A trade surplus is simply the gap between what a country sells abroad (exports) and what it buys from abroad (imports); when exports outpace imports by this much, money flows into the country rather than out of it. China has now cleared the trillion-dollar mark for two years running, but this year it got there faster than ever before.

The headline number came from exports rising 35.2% in October compared with the same month last year — itself a surprise, since forecasters had predicted a slightly smaller 33.5% jump. What caught more attention, though, was the import side: purchases from abroad rose 41.6%, the steepest increase since May 2011. That is a strange combination. Normally, when a country's imports surge that fast, its trade surplus shrinks, because it is spending more on foreign goods. Here the opposite happened — exports grew even faster, so the surplus kept widening despite China buying more from the rest of the world too.

The timing matters because this is happening in the middle of a tariff standoff between China and its two biggest trading partners, the United States and the European Union. A tariff is a tax a government places on goods coming in from another country, usually to make imported products more expensive and encourage people to buy domestic ones instead. Washington and Brussels have spent the last few years raising tariffs specifically to shrink their trade deficits with China — in theory, making Chinese goods pricier abroad should mean fewer of them get bought. The new data suggests that, so far, it hasn't worked as intended: Chinese exporters appear to be finding buyers elsewhere, routing goods through other countries, or absorbing costs to stay competitive.

Economists reading these numbers don't all draw the same conclusion. Some see proof that Chinese manufacturing is simply more efficient and scaled than anything tariffs can easily dislodge — factories there can still undercut rivals even with an extra tax tacked on at the border. Others are more skeptical of the raw numbers themselves, noting that a country's own customs office publishing a record result is not the same as independent verification; some trade economists have long suspected that Chinese export data gets inflated through practices like over-invoicing, where goods are recorded at higher values than they actually sold for, partly to move money across borders in ways that dodge capital controls.

What's not in dispute is the scale of the imbalance. A $1 trillion surplus means China is, on net, sending that much more value out into the world economy than it's taking in — money that lands as profit for Chinese exporters and as reserves for the Chinese state, while trading partners rack up the matching deficit. For the US and Europe, that deficit shows up as pressure on domestic manufacturers who compete directly with Chinese imports, and as a political argument for tougher trade measures. For China, it shows up as leverage: a government sitting on that much foreign currency has more room to maneuver, whether in trade negotiations or in managing its own currency's value.

Words worth knowing
  • Trade surplus — The amount by which a country's exports (what it sells abroad) exceed its imports (what it buys from abroad).
  • Tariff — A tax a government puts on goods coming in from another country, usually to make them more expensive than similar domestic products.
  • Year-over-year — A way of comparing a number to the same period exactly one year earlier, which cancels out normal seasonal swings.

  1. When a country posts a record trade surplus like this, is that a sign of real economic strength or a warning sign of a lopsided economy?

Lawmakers Vote to Stop a Push to Remove a Top Defense Official

On Tuesday night, the people who make America's laws took a vote about a very powerful man named Pete Hegseth. He runs the country's military as Defense Secretary. One lawmaker wanted to try to remove him from his job, but most lawmakers voted to stop that from happening, at least for now.

Why this mattersThis story shows how just a handful of votes, even people simply not showing up, can decide whether a powerful official keeps a job overseeing soldiers and weapons. It's a bit like a class vote where a few absent kids change the whole result, except here the decision affects the entire military.
Read the whole story

Pete Hegseth is in charge of America's armed forces. A lawmaker named Thomas Massie was upset about how Hegseth handled a war involving a country called Iran. So Massie wrote up a formal paper called a resolution, asking to impeach Hegseth. Impeach means to formally accuse someone powerful of doing something wrong, as a first step toward possibly removing them from their job.

The House of Representatives, one of the two groups that make U.S. laws, had to vote on what to do with Massie's paper. They voted 211 to 204 to send it to a small committee instead of debating it right away. Sending it to committee usually means the idea quietly stops there and nothing more happens this year.

Only one other Republican, from Massie's own political party, agreed with him. Eleven Democrats, who usually disagree with Republicans on many things, were not even there to vote. One of them, a well-known former leader named Nancy Pelosi, was at a different event honoring veterans, which are people who served in the military.

Some news reports asked whether those missing Democrats accidentally helped save Hegseth's job by not showing up. Democratic leaders say that's not fair, because the voting rules made the outcome likely no matter who showed up. Massie says he might try again later.

House Quietly Kills Push to Impeach the Defense Secretary

On Tuesday night, the House of Representatives voted 211-204 to send a resolution impeaching Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth into committee — a procedural move that effectively ends the effort for the year. The lawmaker who introduced the resolution, Kentucky Republican Thomas Massie, was the only Republican who voted against burying it. Eleven Democrats missed the vote entirely, including former Speaker Nancy Pelosi, raising questions about whether their absence helped save Hegseth from a more serious fight.

Why this mattersThis story is less about Pete Hegseth specifically and more about how Congress actually works day to day: most of the power to stop something lies not in dramatic votes on the merits, but in quiet procedural moves like 'referring to committee' that most people never hear explained. It's also a reminder that accountability for powerful officials often depends on unglamorous things — who shows up to vote, and when — as much as on the strength of the underlying argument.
Read the whole story

Thomas Massie, a Republican from Kentucky known for breaking with his own party on matters of war and executive power, introduced a resolution to impeach Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth over his handling of the recent US conflict with Iran. Impeachment is the process Congress uses to formally accuse a government official of serious wrongdoing — it's the first step toward possibly removing them from office, though it requires further votes (including in the Senate) to actually happen. Massie's resolution never got that far.

Instead, the House voted on something more mundane but just as decisive: whether to refer the resolution to a committee for further study, rather than debate and vote on it directly. In practice, referring a resolution to committee without a deadline is a well-known way for the House to let a proposal quietly die — it doesn't reject the idea outright, but it ensures nothing happens before the current session of Congress ends. That's what happened here. The vote was 211-204, largely along party lines, sending Massie's impeachment push into a kind of legislative limbo it is unlikely to escape before January, when control of the House changes hands.

What made the vote unusual was Massie himself. He was the only Republican who voted against tabling his own resolution, meaning he wanted his colleagues to actually debate and vote on whether Hegseth should be impeached, not bury the question in committee. Massie has been an outspoken critic of recent US military action in Iran, arguing that Hegseth mismanaged the Pentagon's role in the conflict. Most Republicans disagreed enough — or wanted to avoid the political fight badly enough — that they joined Democrats... except that Democrats didn't entirely show up either.

Eleven Democrats missed the vote altogether, including Nancy Pelosi, the former Speaker of the House, who was attending a Veterans Day event instead of being on the floor. Because the final tally was close, those absences mattered: had more Democrats been present and voted against tabling, the outcome might have been different. Some commentary, including from the Qatar-based broadcaster Al Jazeera, suggested that Democratic absences effectively helped protect Hegseth from a more serious impeachment fight — a claim worth noting came from an outlet that often takes a critical line on US foreign policy, even where the underlying facts check out.

Democratic Party leaders reject that framing. They argue the outcome reflects how House procedural rules work — tabling motions are won or lost by whoever shows up, and absences happen for mundane reasons like other commitments — rather than any deliberate decision to shield the defense secretary. Massie, for his part, has signaled he isn't finished, and could reintroduce the resolution next year. By then, Democrats will control the House, changing who sets the legislative agenda and whether an impeachment push would even get a hearing.

Words worth knowing
  • Impeachment — A formal process Congress uses to accuse a government official of serious wrongdoing, as a first step toward possibly removing them from office.
  • Refer to committee — A procedural move that sends a proposal to a smaller group for further study instead of voting on it directly — often used to quietly let it die without an official rejection.
  • Secretary of Defense — The Cabinet official who runs the Pentagon, the headquarters overseeing the US military, and reports directly to the president.
