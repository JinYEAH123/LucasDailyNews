People Who Build AI Are Warning: 'We Need to Slow Down'

Some of the very people building super-smart computer programs called AI are now saying it might be moving too fast and too dangerously. The US President says that's nonsense. Meanwhile, stock markets got jumpy, and companies that make AI computer chips lost billions of dollars in value in a single day.

Why this mattersAI already helps write your homework help, recommend videos, and power the voice assistants you might talk to at home. If the people building it are worried it could get out of control, that matters for the world you'll grow up in — but grown-ups don't agree on how worried to be, or whether slowing down would just let another country get there first.
Read the whole story

Dario Amodei runs a big AI company called Anthropic. He went on television and said AI is improving so fast that it's a 'warning sign.' He said, plainly, 'we need to slow down.'

That's a strange thing to hear from someone who builds AI for a living. It's a bit like a car company boss saying cars are going too fast and someone should put on the brakes.

Three researchers who work at top AI labs quit their jobs. One of them, Jacob Coxon, wrote that people building AI 'earnestly believe it could kill us all by the end of the decade.' That means before the year 2030.

President Trump disagreed strongly. He wrote online that worrying about AI is a 'sick conspiracy' aimed at hurting AI and the huge computer buildings, called data centers, that run it. He says the US is beating China at AI and wants to keep winning, saying 'whoever wins AI, wins.' China's government called the worrying 'fear-mongering.' Meanwhile, on the stock market, shares in chip-making companies like Nvidia and Intel dropped sharply, while shares in companies like Meta and Microsoft went up.

Words worth knowing
  • AI (Artificial Intelligence) — Computer programs that can learn from huge amounts of information and do things that used to need a human brain, like writing, answering questions, or recognizing pictures.
  • Data center — A giant building full of powerful computers that run AI programs — imagine a warehouse the size of several football fields, but instead of boxes, it's packed with humming machines.

AI's Biggest Builders Say Slow Down. The US President Says That's a 'Conspiracy.'

This week the argument over artificial intelligence stopped being a tech-industry debate and became a political fight with real money on the line. The CEO of one of the top AI companies said the technology's progress is moving too fast to be safe. President Trump called that kind of warning a 'sick conspiracy,' and stock markets swung hard as investors tried to figure out who to believe.

Why this mattersThis isn't an abstract argument about the far future — it's a fight over whether to press forward on a technology that could reshape jobs, information, and even warfare, happening right now, with the people who built it split on whether to hit the brakes. Whatever the US and China decide in the next few years about how fast to build and how tightly to regulate AI will shape the tools, and the risks, this generation grows up with.
Read the whole story

Dario Amodei runs Anthropic, a company that builds some of the most advanced AI systems in the world — the kind of company you'd expect to be cheering AI on, not warning about it. But in a television interview this week, he said the pace of AI progress is 'exponential,' meaning it isn't just improving steadily, it's improving faster and faster, doubling and redoubling in capability over shorter and shorter stretches of time. He called that a 'warning sign' for humanity and said plainly: 'we need to slow down.' That's a striking thing for someone whose company depends on AI moving fast to say in public.

Amodei isn't alone. Three researchers at major AI labs recently quit their jobs over fears that AI could 'spiral out of control' — meaning become powerful enough that its creators can no longer reliably predict or steer what it does. One of them, Jacob Coxon, wrote that people building this technology from the inside 'earnestly believe it could kill us all by the end of the decade.' That's not a fringe blogger talking — it's someone who worked at the center of the industry, walking away and saying so out loud.

President Trump responded by dismissing the alarm entirely. He posted that concerns about slowing AI down amount to a 'sick conspiracy' aimed at AI companies and the data centers — the huge warehouses full of computers — that power them. His view is that the real danger isn't AI moving too fast, it's the US losing the race to build it first. 'Whoever wins AI, wins,' he said, arguing the US is currently ahead of China and needs to stay there. Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer, from the opposing political party, took a middle position: he called for an official briefing on both 'the serious risks posed by uninhibited AI development' and the government's 'lack of action to stop China's development of the technology' — treating going too fast and falling behind as two dangers at once, not one or the other.

China's government pushed back too, but from a different angle. A Foreign Ministry spokesperson called the American safety alarm 'fear-mongering' that would 'disrupt the process of global AI governance' — the slow, difficult work of countries agreeing on shared rules for AI. It's worth noting whose voice that is: an official government spokesperson speaking for Beijing's interests, not an independent judge of the science.

Markets took the safety warnings seriously enough to move real money. Nvidia, which makes the specialized chips nearly all major AI systems run on, dropped more than 3%. Intel, AMD and Marvell — other chipmakers — fell 5 to 6%, and the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index, a measure that tracks chip companies as a group, fell almost 6% in a single day. Micron, which makes memory chips, tumbled over 5% separately. But not everything fell: Meta, Microsoft and Alphabet, which mostly build AI software and services rather than the chips themselves, actually rose. That split suggests investors weren't panicking about AI itself — they were specifically worried about a slowdown in the physical buildout of chips and data centers, the multi-billion-dollar bet that AI's usefulness would keep growing at the current breakneck pace.

Words worth knowing
  • Exponential — Growing at a rate that keeps speeding up, so each step forward is bigger than the last, rather than growing by the same steady amount each time.
  • Data center — A large facility packed with powerful computers that companies use to run AI systems, requiring enormous amounts of electricity and cooling.
  • AI governance — The rules, agreements and oversight that governments and organizations are trying to build to manage how AI is developed and used safely.

    • If your country slows down but another country doesn't, the risky version gets built anyway — just by someone else.

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Trust the warning

    • They have more direct, technical insight into what these systems can actually do than almost anyone else on earth.
    • Warning about your own product's dangers is a strange move if the goal were simply to make money — it risks scaring off customers and investors.
    • Multiple researchers, not just executives, have quit and said similar things, which suggests it isn't one company's marketing strategy.

    • Talking up how dangerous and powerful your technology is can also make it sound more impressive and important, which is good for attracting investment and talent.
    • Executives calling for regulation can conveniently make it harder for smaller competitors to catch up, since only large, well-funded companies can afford heavy compliance costs.
    • People are not always honest, even with themselves, about their motives when warning the public serves their company's positioning either way.

A Drone Hit a Train Right After Famous Visitors Got Off

A Russian drone struck a train near the border between Ukraine and Poland, just minutes after some very important visitors had left it. Another train full of officials was still sitting at the same station when the drone hit. Nobody was hurt, but the close call scared a lot of people.

Why this mattersWars can spread past their borders, a bit like a spilled drink reaching further across a table than you expect. If this war creeps closer to more countries, more people could get pulled into it, including places friendly with the United States. Even a near-miss like this one can change how safe countries nearby feel.
Read the whole story

Boris Johnson used to be the leader of the United Kingdom. He was riding a special train through Ukraine, heading toward the border with Poland. His train left the Yahodyn station a little early.

Right after his train pulled away, a Russian drone hit near the station. A drone is a flying robot with no pilot inside, and this one was carrying a weapon. A different train, carrying David Petraeus, who used to run America's spy agency, was still parked there when the strike happened. Somehow, nobody was hurt.

Ukraine and Poland do not think this was an accident. They believe Russia knew important people were nearby and struck anyway, almost like a warning. Boris Johnson said afterward that he could not understand 'what warped logic' made Russia's leader, Vladimir Putin, attack a parked train so close to Poland.

Poland belongs to NATO, a group of countries, including the United States, that promise to protect each other if attacked. Because the drone landed so close to NATO land, NATO countries are now worried that Russia might be testing how far it can push before something much bigger happens.

Words worth knowing
  • drone — A drone is a flying machine with no pilot inside, controlled from far away, that can carry a camera or even a weapon.
  • NATO — NATO is a group of countries, including the United States, Poland, and the UK, that have promised to protect each other if one of them is attacked.

Russian Drone Hits Train Station Right After It Carried Boris Johnson and Other Western Officials

A Russian drone struck a train at a station near Ukraine's border with Poland, just after one train carrying senior Western officials had left early and while a second, carrying a former CIA director, was still parked there. Nobody was hurt, but Ukraine and Poland are calling it a deliberate warning shot aimed at NATO, the military alliance that includes Poland but not Ukraine. The strike happened close enough to NATO territory that it has unsettled every country bordering the war.

Why this mattersThis wasn't just a story about a missed train — it's about how close the war in Ukraine keeps coming to spilling over into countries that are treaty-bound to defend each other, including places many readers might live in or visit. Every strike near a NATO border tests whether the alliance's promise of collective defense holds up in practice, not just on paper, and how governments answer that question shapes whether the war stays contained or grows.
Read the whole story

The trains were sitting at Yahodyn station, a rail crossing point right on the Ukraine-Poland border, when the drone came in. One train had just carried former UK Prime Minister Boris Johnson, former Swedish Prime Minister Carl Bildt, and security advisers from several EU countries into Ukraine. That train pulled out ahead of its schedule, for reasons that aren't fully clear, and avoided the strike. A second train, carrying former CIA Director David Petraeus, was still at the station when the drone hit a stationary locomotive there.

Foreign leaders travel into Ukraine by train rather than by plane because Ukraine's airspace has been closed to civilian flights since Russia's full-scale invasion began; anti-aircraft missiles and drones make flying too dangerous. This kind of overnight rail journey into Kyiv has become such a routine part of wartime diplomacy that journalists nicknamed it "rail diplomacy." It normally happens without incident, which is part of why this strike stood out.

Johnson wrote publicly afterward that he did not know "what warped logic drove Putin to blow up a stationary Ukrainian locomotive on the Polish border this morning." Ukrainian officials and the Polish government both described the strike as a deliberate act of escalation — meaning an action meant to raise tension and risk on purpose, rather than an accident of war. Neither government has produced hard proof that the drone was aimed specifically at the officials' trains rather than at the rail line itself, which Russia has targeted repeatedly throughout the war as a way to disrupt Ukraine's supply routes.

The location is what makes this different from routine strikes on Ukrainian rail infrastructure, which happen often and rarely make international headlines. Yahodyn sits right against the Polish border, and Poland is a NATO member. Under NATO's founding promise, known as Article 5, an attack on one member country is treated as an attack on all of them, obligating a collective response. A drone landing on NATO soil — even by accident — has already happened once before, in 2022, when a stray missile killed two people in a Polish village and briefly raised fears of a wider war before investigators concluded it was likely an errant Ukrainian air-defense missile rather than a deliberate Russian strike.

That history is exactly why officials are arguing over what this strike means. One reading is that Russia is deliberately testing how close it can come to NATO territory without triggering a serious response, using a strike near a diplomatic train as a way to unsettle Western leaders personally. The other reading is that this was simply another strike on rail infrastructure that Russia has hit before, and its timing next to the diplomats' trains was bad luck rather than a targeted message — drones aimed at tracks and stations don't always land precisely where their operators intend.

Words worth knowing
  • NATO — A military alliance of the United States, Canada, and many European countries who have agreed to defend each other if any one of them is attacked.
  • Escalation — An action that deliberately raises the danger or intensity of a conflict, rather than calming it down.
  • Rail diplomacy — The practice of foreign leaders traveling into Ukraine by overnight train instead of by plane, because Ukraine's airspace is closed to civilian flights during the war.

Both Political Teams Race to Win Control of America's Senate

In seven weeks, Americans will vote in an election that could shake up who runs a huge part of the U.S. government: the Senate. Right now, President Trump is not very popular, and both political parties are spending huge piles of money on TV ads to try to win.

Why this mattersThe Senate votes on big things that touch your life without you seeing it — like how much money goes to schools, hospitals, and scientific research. Whoever controls it after this election will have a lot of power to help or block the president's plans for the next two years.
Read the whole story

The Senate is a group of 100 lawmakers who help write the laws for the whole country. Every state picks two people to represent them there, no matter how big or small the state is.

Right now, only 35 out of every 100 people asked say they like how President Trump is doing his job. That's called his 'approval rating,' and 35% is pretty low for a president.

Because of this, both political parties smell opportunity. In just one state, New Hampshire, groups supporting Republicans have promised to spend about $28 million on TV ads before the election — roughly enough to buy 2,000 brand-new cars. Groups supporting Democrats have promised about $9.9 million, enough for about 700 cars.

Experts who study elections say that when a president is this unpopular, his political party usually loses seats in these kinds of elections. But nobody knows for sure — every state is a little different, and seven weeks is a long time in politics.

Trump's Approval Sits at 35% as Billions Pour Into the Fight for the Senate

With seven weeks left before Americans vote in this year's midterm elections, control of the US Senate is up for grabs. President Trump's approval rating has sunk to 35%, a number that historically spells trouble for a president's party — and both parties are now spending heavily to either capitalise on that unpopularity or contain the damage.

Why this mattersWhoever controls the Senate after this election will shape what actually gets passed into law for the next two years, regardless of who's president — it decides which judges get confirmed, which bills survive, and how effectively the government can act on almost anything voters care about. The size of the ad spending shown here is also a reminder of how much money now flows into deciding a single state's outcome.
Read the whole story

Every two years, roughly a third of the seats in the US Senate — one of the two chambers of Congress, alongside the House of Representatives — come up for election, even in years when there's no presidential race. These are called midterm elections, because they land in the middle of a president's four-year term. They matter enormously: whichever party controls the Senate can block or approve the president's judicial nominations, cabinet picks, and major legislation, regardless of what the president himself wants to do.

Right now, President Trump's approval rating — the percentage of Americans who say they think he's doing a good job — sits at 35%, according to CNN's running average of multiple polls, known as a Poll of Polls. Averaging several polls together smooths out the quirks of any single survey, giving a more reliable picture than trusting one poll alone. Political analysts who study midterm history point out that presidents with approval ratings this low have historically seen their party lose seats in Congress, sometimes badly — voters use these elections to register frustration with whoever is in the White House.

That historical pattern is shaping how both parties are spending money right now. In New Hampshire, a state Democrats currently hold, Republican-aligned groups have already reserved about $28 million in television advertising to run between September and Election Day. Of that, $11.2 million comes from a group called the No Going Back PAC and $7.9 million from the Senate Leadership Fund — both are political action committees, organisations that raise and spend money to influence elections but are legally barred from coordinating directly with candidates' own campaigns. Democratic-aligned groups have committed roughly $9.9 million in the same state, meaning Republicans are outspending them there by nearly three to one.

That spending gap tells its own story about strategy. Democrats are trying to expand the map by competing in states that usually lean Republican, betting that an unpopular president drags down his party's candidates even in friendlier territory. Republicans, meanwhile, are pouring money into a small number of Democratic-held seats they think are newly winnable, precisely because they can't count on the president's popularity to carry them — they need to outspend and out-organise instead.

Analysts are careful to note that a national approval number doesn't decide individual races. Senate elections are fought seat by seat, and local factors — a popular incumbent, a scandal, a state's particular economic mood — can matter more than what voters think of the president nationally. A 35% approval rating makes Republican losses more likely on average, but it guarantees nothing in any single state, which is exactly why both parties are still fighting hard rather than assuming the outcome.

Words worth knowing
  • Midterm elections — US elections held roughly halfway through a president's four-year term, when about a third of the Senate and all of the House of Representatives are up for a vote.
  • Approval rating — The percentage of people surveyed who say they think a political leader is doing a good job, usually measured through opinion polls.
  • Political action committee (PAC) — An organisation that raises and spends money to support or oppose political candidates but is legally required to operate separately from the candidates' own campaigns.
