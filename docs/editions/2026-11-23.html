Chip Company Boss Loses Job, Gets It Back Six Days Later — And Stock Markets Go Wild

Jensen Huang runs Nvidia, a company that makes the tiny computer chips used in video games and artificial intelligence. He had to step down after serious accusations were made about him. Then, just six days later, Nvidia's board of directors brought him right back — and the news made stock markets jump all over the world.

Why this mattersNvidia's chips power so much of the technology you might use or see — video game consoles, phones, and the AI tools grown-ups use at work — so what happens to its boss can ripple out and touch jobs and products far beyond Nvidia itself. It's also a reminder that serious accusations against someone can take a long time to fully sort out, even when a big company makes its decision in days.

Nvidia makes computer chips — small pieces of electronics that power video games, smartphones, and the artificial intelligence (AI) that runs things like chatbots. Jensen Huang helped start Nvidia more than 30 years ago and has led it ever since. He is one of the most famous bosses in the business world.

Eleven days before this story, Huang resigned after serious accusations came out, including a claim that he had secretly been in a relationship with someone connected to China's spy agency. China's government says that claim is not true. At the same time, several US government agencies were separately checking whether Nvidia had followed the rules about selling its chips to China.

Just six days after he left, Nvidia's board of directors — the group of people who make the company's biggest decisions — voted to bring Huang straight back as boss. Every single person on the board agreed, which almost never happens with a decision this big.

Investors, the people who buy tiny pieces of companies called shares, were thrilled. Nvidia's own share price jumped more than 30% in a single day. Big stock market measures like the Dow Jones and the Nasdaq jumped too, making it one of the best days for American stock markets in years.

Nvidia's CEO Was Fired, Then Rehired Six Days Later — and Wall Street Erupted

Nvidia's board of directors reinstated Jensen Huang as president and CEO on Monday, just six days after the company's chair said he would not be coming back. Huang had resigned eleven days earlier over an alleged affair, including a claim — denied by Beijing — that the other person was a Chinese intelligence operative, while the SEC, the Department of Justice, and the FBI were separately investigating the company. The moment his return was announced, stocks across the market jumped, with Nvidia's own share price rising more than 30% in a single day.

Why this mattersNvidia's chips are inside the AI tools millions of people already use daily, and its stock price has become one of the biggest single drivers of whether retirement funds, index funds, and the broader stock market go up or down. How the US handles the overlap between one company's leadership drama and national-security worries about China will shape what technology gets built where, and who gets to buy it, for years to come.

Jensen Huang has run Nvidia since 1993, long before most people had heard of it, and he built it into the company that makes the chips powering almost every major artificial intelligence system in the world — the kind of role where losing him doesn't just change an org chart, it changes what investors think the entire AI boom is worth. Eleven days before his reinstatement, he resigned after reports surfaced of an extramarital affair, with one allegation claiming the other person was a suspected Chinese intelligence operative — someone working to gather information on behalf of the Chinese government. China's government has denied this. At the same time, Nvidia was already facing separate scrutiny from the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC, the US agency that polices publicly traded companies for fraud and disclosure violations), the Department of Justice, and the FBI.

Six days after Huang resigned, Nvidia's board chair publicly said directors had no intention of bringing him back — about as final a statement as a company can make about a departed CEO. Then, on Monday, the full board unanimously reversed course and reinstated him, effective immediately. That kind of whiplash is rare. Boards usually take weeks or months to even discuss a CEO's return, let alone reverse a public statement that firmly within less than a week, and the fact that it happened unanimously suggests something — pressure from shareholders, fear of losing Huang to a competitor, or a judgment that the allegations wouldn't hold up — shifted hard and fast behind closed doors.

The market's reaction was its own kind of headline. The Dow Jones Industrial Average, a measure of 30 large US companies' stock prices, jumped more than 1,400 points, about 3%. The Nasdaq Composite, an index weighted heavily toward technology companies, rallied almost 6.5%. Nvidia's own stock rose more than 30% in a single trading day — a move so large for a company of its size that it helped make the day Wall Street's best since April 2025. That reaction tells you how much of the stock market's recent gains are tied to a handful of AI-related companies, and how much investors had been treating Huang personally as essential to Nvidia's value, almost as if the company and the man were the same asset.

The scandal isn't only personal — it's tangled up with US-China tension over computer chips. Nvidia's most advanced chips are powerful enough that the US government restricts which ones can be sold to China, worried they could be used to build military or surveillance technology that rivals American capabilities. US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said the government wants to find out who at Nvidia was responsible for making sure those chip sales to China followed the rules during Huang's prior leadership — meaning the affair allegation and the compliance question, while separate, are now being investigated against the same backdrop of distrust between Washington and Beijing.

The central fact that nobody disputes is also the one nobody can yet prove: the claim that Huang's affair involved a Chinese intelligence operative remains unverified, and China's government denies it outright. That leaves Nvidia in an unusual position — its CEO is back, its stock has roared back with him, but the investigations that were running alongside his resignation haven't gone away, and the question of what exactly happened, and why the board changed its mind so fast, is still open.

One Vote Count in North Carolina Gives a Party Its Biggest Win in 30 Years

On Sunday, officials finished recounting votes in a close election race in North Carolina. A man named Chuck Hubbard was declared the winner, beating a longtime lawmaker named Virginia Foxx. That single result gave the Democratic political party its biggest win in the US House of Representatives in thirty years.

Why this mattersThe House of Representatives votes on things that touch your everyday life, like how much money schools get or what rules companies have to follow. When one team wins a lot more seats than before, it can change which new laws get a chance to pass. And when someone becomes the first person like them to hold a powerful job, it can change what other kids think is possible for themselves someday.
Read the whole story

The US House of Representatives is a group of 435 people who help write the country's laws. Think of it like one giant classroom where every state sends some students, and the number of students each state sends depends on how many people live there.

Right now, two big teams compete for seats in that room: Democrats and Republicans. After Sunday's recount in North Carolina, Democrats reached 258 seats. That's more than half the room, and the most seats they've won in a regular election since before most kids' parents were born.

This election, Democrats gained 44 seats total. That's even more than the 40 seats they won back in 2018, during Donald Trump's first term as president. Winning that many seats is a big swing.

There's another first coming too. A Democrat named Hakeem Jeffries is now set to become the leader of the House, called the Speaker. He would be the first Black person ever to hold that job.

Democrats Win Their Biggest House Majority in Three Decades

On Sunday, Democrats clinched their 258th seat in the US House of Representatives, the chamber's largest majority for either party won in a general election in 30 years. The winning seat came from North Carolina, where Democrat Chuck Hubbard beat longtime Republican Congresswoman Virginia Foxx after a recount. The result puts Congressman Hakeem Jeffries on track to become the first Black Speaker of the House in US history.

Why this mattersWhoever controls the House decides which laws even get a vote — on everything from taxes to healthcare to how much money the government borrows — so this result will shape what actually happens in Washington for the next two years. It also means investigations into the president's administration become much easier to launch, which could dominate the news well beyond this one election.
Read the whole story

The US House of Representatives is one of the two chambers of Congress, the branch of government that writes and passes federal laws. All 435 seats are up for election every two years, and whichever party wins more than half of them — 218 seats — controls the chamber, deciding which bills even get a vote. On Sunday, Democrats crossed a different threshold: their 258th seat, which makes this the party's biggest House majority won in a general election since the early 1990s.

The deciding win was close and contested. In North Carolina, Democrat Chuck Hubbard was declared the winner over Republican incumbent Virginia Foxx — meaning she already held the seat — only after a recount, a formal re-counting of the ballots that candidates can request when a race is narrow enough that the original count might not reflect who actually won. Foxx's campaign pushed for the recount before conceding, accepting defeat, once the second count confirmed Hubbard's win.

This election happened during a midterm, the vote held halfway through a president's four-year term, when every House seat and a third of the Senate's is contested but the presidency itself is not. Midterms are usually rough for whichever party holds the White House, since voters often use them to express frustration with how things are going. Democrats gained 44 seats this cycle — four more than the 40 they flipped in 2018, during Donald Trump's first term, which was already considered a strong showing for the party out of power.

The scale of this win matters beyond the raw number. It puts Hakeem Jeffries, the Democrat who leads his party in the House, on track to become Speaker of the House when the new Congress begins — the person who runs the chamber's daily business, decides which bills reach a vote, and stands second in the line of succession to the presidency after the vice president. Jeffries would be the first Black Speaker in American history, a milestone that will likely get as much attention as the majority itself once the new Congress is sworn in.

A majority this size changes what a president can get done. With Democrats controlling the House, bills the Trump administration wants passed will need Democratic votes or compromise to move forward, and the House can also open investigations into the executive branch far more easily than a narrowly divided chamber would allow. Whether that produces gridlock, deal-making, or a wave of oversight hearings is something that will only become clear once the new Congress actually convenes.

Words worth knowing
  • Midterm election — A nationwide vote held halfway through a president's four-year term, when all House seats and some Senate seats are contested but the presidency is not.
  • Recount — A formal re-counting of ballots in a close race, usually requested by a candidate who thinks the original count might be wrong.
  • Speaker of the House — The lawmaker who leads the House of Representatives, controlling which bills get voted on and standing second in line to become president if the president and vice president cannot serve.

Almost 200 Countries Talk for Two Weeks About Climate Change — and Still Can't Agree

Leaders from nearly 200 countries met for two weeks in Antalya, Turkey, to talk about climate change. On Sunday, the big meeting ended without a deal. The main sticking point: how fast the world should stop burning oil, gas, and coal.

Why this mattersThe hotter the Earth gets, the more extreme weather we see — bigger storms, hotter summers, and more wildfires. When grown-ups at a big meeting can't agree on a plan, the planet keeps heating up a little longer before anyone makes new promises to stop it. The choices made (or not made) at meetings like this will shape how hot your summers are when you're grown up.
Read the whole story

Almost 200 countries sent people to a huge meeting in Antalya, a city in Turkey. For two weeks, they talked about one giant problem: how to stop the Earth from getting hotter. This yearly meeting is called COP31.

The groups got stuck on one big question. Many countries wanted a clear plan to stop using oil, gas, and coal — fuels that heat up the planet when we burn them for energy. But some countries that sell a lot of oil and gas did not want to promise to stop.

On Sunday, the meeting ended with no agreement at all. The leader of the United Nations, a man named António Guterres, said he was 'deeply disappointed.' No one announced when or where the next meeting will happen.

This means the world still has no new plan this year for slowing down climate change. The hardest decisions got put off again.

Words worth knowing
  • fossil fuels — Oil, gas, and coal are fossil fuels — they are dug up from deep underground, and burning them for energy releases gases that trap heat and warm up the Earth.
  • phase out — To phase something out means to stop using it little by little over time, instead of all at once.

World Climate Summit Ends With No Deal, UN Chief Calls It "Deeply Disappointing"

The United Nations' COP31 climate summit in Antalya, Turkey, closed on Sunday, November 22, without countries agreeing on a final plan. Negotiators from nearly 200 nations spent two weeks arguing, ran into overtime, and still couldn't settle their biggest disagreement: whether the world should commit to phasing out fossil fuels. No new commitments were made, and no date was even set for the next round of talks.

Why this mattersClimate change doesn't stop at a country's borders — a tonne of carbon released in one place warms the planet everywhere, which is exactly why these global negotiations exist in the first place. When the main venue for countries to agree on cutting emissions together fails, the gap between what scientists say needs to happen and what governments actually agree to do gets wider, and that gap doesn't close itself.
Read the whole story

Every year, almost every country on Earth sends representatives to a UN climate conference called COP — short for "Conference of the Parties" — to negotiate what the world will do about rising global temperatures. This year's version, COP31, was held in Antalya, Turkey, and was supposed to produce fresh commitments on cutting the greenhouse gas emissions that cause climate change. Instead, after two full weeks of talks that ran past their scheduled end date, the conference closed with nothing to show for it.

The sticking point was familiar: a split between countries that wanted binding language committing the world to phasing out fossil fuels — oil, gas, and coal — and countries whose economies depend heavily on producing and selling those same fuels, who refused to accept language that would force them to wind that down. "Binding" is the key word here. A country can always say it supports fighting climate change in general terms; the fight is over whether an agreement actually obligates countries to act, with real consequences if they don't, or whether it's just a statement of good intentions. The reporting on this summit does not name exactly which countries blocked the phase-out language, which is itself telling — these negotiations happen partly behind closed doors, and public blame is often avoided even when everyone in the room knows who dug in.

UN Secretary-General António Guterres, the UN's top official, didn't hide his frustration. He called the outcome "deeply disappointing" — unusually blunt language for someone whose job largely involves diplomatic phrasing. His comment matters because the UN climate summit is meant to be the world's main forum for countries to coordinate on climate action together, rather than each country acting alone. When it fails to produce an agreement, there's no backup forum that automatically picks up the slack.

Perhaps the most telling detail is what didn't happen at the closing session: no new date or location was announced for the next round of talks. Normally, even when a COP summit disappoints, organizers at least confirm when and where the next one will happen, since planning a conference of this size for representatives from 200 countries takes months. Leaving that open suggests the disagreement wasn't just about wording — it may reflect deeper uncertainty about whether continuing in the same format is even worth it right now.

This isn't the first time a COP summit has ended without a breakthrough, and it won't be the only story anyone tells about global climate politics this year. But a failed COP carries real weight: these talks are one of the few places where oil-producing nations and countries demanding faster climate action are forced to sit across from each other and negotiate specifics, rather than just issuing separate statements. When that process stalls, it stalls the one mechanism built to produce a coordinated global response, not just a collection of individual national promises.

Words worth knowing
  • COP (Conference of the Parties) — An annual United Nations meeting where almost every country sends representatives to negotiate action on climate change.
  • Phase-out — A gradual, planned end to using something — in this case, fossil fuels like oil, gas, and coal — rather than stopping all at once.
  • Binding agreement — A deal that creates real obligations countries must follow, as opposed to a statement of goals they can simply ignore without consequence.
