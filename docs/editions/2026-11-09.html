Nvidia's CEO Admits an Affair — and Wipes Out $192 Billion in a Day

Jensen Huang, the CEO and co-founder of Nvidia — the company that makes the chips powering most of the world's AI — admitted in a staff memo that he had an affair with a businesswoman U.S. intelligence officials reportedly believe may have ties to Chinese intelligence gathering. Nvidia's board immediately launched an independent investigation, and the stock dropped as much as 8.8% before closing down about 4.3%, erasing roughly $192 billion in the company's value in a single day.

Why this mattersNvidia's chips show up in the AI tools many people use every day, from phone assistants to the systems behind social media, so instability at the top of the company can ripple into products millions of people rely on. It's also a clear example of how personal relationships, corporate decisions, and national security can become tangled together in ways that are hard to untangle — and why governments pay such close attention to who has access to powerful technology.

Jensen Huang runs the most valuable chipmaker on Earth, a company whose processors are the backbone of nearly every major AI system being built right now, from chatbots to military research tools. So when he sent staff a memo admitting to an affair with a businesswoman named Alice Huang — and reports surfaced that U.S. intelligence officials suspect she may have connections to Chinese intelligence-gathering efforts — it wasn't treated as ordinary office gossip. Nvidia's board responded by opening an independent investigation, meaning outside lawyers or advisers, not Nvidia's own staff, will dig into what happened.

Markets reacted instantly and hard. Nvidia's shares fell as much as 8.8% right after the opening bell on Monday, recovering slightly to close down about 4.3%. That single-day drop wiped out roughly $192 billion in market value — the total worth of all the company's shares combined — which is more than the entire value of most companies in the world. Investors weren't necessarily reacting to proof of wrongdoing; they were reacting to uncertainty, and markets tend to punish uncertainty about a company's most important person hard and fast.

The story got more complicated with a separate report from Bloomberg: Nvidia's general counsel — the company's top in-house lawyer — had called a U.S. Commerce Department official in late summer 2025 to ask about an investigation into a firm called Megaspeed International, which was suspected of chip smuggling (illegally moving restricted American chip technology to buyers it isn't supposed to reach, often China, where U.S. export rules try to limit access). Megaspeed has now become central to the unfolding scandal, raising the question of whether Huang's personal relationship and Nvidia's business dealings intersect in a way that matters for national security.

That's the real reason this story is bigger than a CEO's personal life. The United States has spent years trying to control which countries, especially China, can get access to the most advanced chips, because those chips power artificial intelligence systems with both economic and military uses. Nvidia sits at the center of that fight — it designs the chips, and U.S. export rules decide who it's allowed to sell them to. If someone close to Nvidia's CEO had ties to Chinese intelligence efforts, and if sensitive information about chip technology or export enforcement moved in the wrong direction, it wouldn't just be a corporate scandal — it would touch the broader contest between the U.S. and China over who controls the technology shaping the next decades.

Huang has pulled out of several AI industry events this week while the investigation continues, which itself signals how seriously the board and perhaps Huang himself are treating the situation. But the central questions — what exactly he knew, when he knew it, and whether any sensitive information about chips or export controls was ever compromised — remain unanswered. Reporters are still working the story, and until the investigation produces findings, both the public and Nvidia's shareholders are left waiting.

Words worth knowing
  • independent investigation — A review carried out by outside lawyers or experts rather than the company's own staff, meant to be more trustworthy because it isn't controlled by the people being investigated.
  • chip smuggling — Illegally moving advanced computer chips to buyers who aren't allowed to have them under export rules, often because the chips could be used to build powerful AI or military systems.
  • market value — The total worth of all of a company's shares added together, which can rise or fall sharply based on how confident investors feel, even without any new proven facts.

A Republican Lawmaker Wants to Impeach Trump's Defense Secretary — And Democrats Say They'll Help

Republican Congressman Thomas Massie has filed formal impeachment charges against Pete Hegseth, the Secretary of Defense who runs the Pentagon and oversees the U.S. military. A vote in the House of Representatives is expected this week. President Trump has publicly defended Hegseth, saying his only crime was helping 'win two wars.'

Why this mattersWhoever runs the Defense Department controls decisions that reach into military families' lives and America's role in conflicts abroad, so a fight over whether that person keeps the job is not just Washington drama. It's also an early test of how an incoming Democratic majority plans to use its power to investigate and check a Republican administration — a dynamic that will shape fights over other officials for the next two years.
Read the whole story

Thomas Massie, a Republican member of Congress known for occasionally breaking with his own party, filed articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth last week. Articles of impeachment are the formal written accusations that start the process of trying to remove a federal official from office — think of them as the charging document that a trial will be built around, if it gets that far. A vote on whether to move forward is expected on the House floor this week.

What makes this notable is who's lining up behind it. House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries said his party would give the effort 'incredibly strong' support — significant because the midterm elections just handed Democrats a House majority for the next term, even though the current session (where this vote will happen) still runs under the old numbers. A Republican pushing to remove a Cabinet secretary — the top official running one of the government's major departments, in this case the military — while the opposing party cheers him on is an unusual alignment, and it signals how fragile support for Hegseth may be even within his own party.

Trump responded by defending his appointee directly, telling reporters Hegseth was 'guilty of two things: basically winning two wars.' That line frames the impeachment push as political persecution of a successful official rather than accountability for wrongdoing — a framing Hegseth's allies are likely to repeat if the case moves forward.

Here's what's missing so far, and it matters: the specific allegations driving the impeachment — what exactly Hegseth is accused of doing — haven't been laid out in detail in public reporting yet. Massie filed the articles and Democrats pledged support, but the underlying case against Hegseth remains unclear from what's been reported. That's an important gap for anyone trying to judge whether this is a serious accountability effort or largely a political maneuver.

Structurally, this is the first serious attempt to impeach a sitting Cabinet secretary since control of the House shifted after the midterms, and it will test how the incoming Democratic majority plans to use its oversight power — Congress's constitutional authority to investigate and check the executive branch, the part of government led by the president that includes departments like Defense. Even if the House votes to impeach, removal would require a two-thirds vote in the Senate, which is a high bar rarely cleared in American history.

Words worth knowing
  • Articles of impeachment — The formal written list of accusations a lawmaker files to begin the process of trying to remove a federal official from office.
  • Cabinet secretary — The official appointed by the president to run a major federal department, such as Defense, Education, or Treasury.
  • Oversight — Congress's constitutional power to investigate and check the actions of the executive branch, which includes the president and federal departments.

A Shooting in a Historic City Square Shocks Poland

On Saturday, a man opened fire with a gun in a big, old square in the city of Kraków, Poland. He killed six people and hurt more than a dozen others — about as many as a whole school classroom. He died later in the hospital. Poland's leaders say he held extreme, hateful political beliefs, and they are calling it a terror attack.

Why this mattersA town square is supposed to be one of the safest, most normal places there is — like a park or a playground where people just go about their day. When violence happens somewhere so ordinary, it reminds people everywhere that hateful ideas can turn into real harm, and that keeping public places safe takes real effort.

Kraków is a city in Poland, a country in Europe. Its Main Market Square is one of the oldest and most famous open squares in the world. People go there to shop, eat, and meet friends, much like a town square or a busy park near you.

On Saturday, a man walked into that crowded square and started shooting. Six people died. More than a dozen were hurt. The man later died in the hospital too.

Poland's government says the attacker held far-right, ultranationalist views. That means he believed his own country and people should be treated as better than everyone else, in an extreme and hateful way. The country's defense minister — the leader in charge of protecting the nation — called the attack a terror attack, which means violence done on purpose to scare people and push a political idea.

Poland announced two days of national mourning, a time when the whole country pauses to remember the people who died. Police are still trying to find out more about the man: who he knew, what groups he was part of, and how he got his gun.

Words worth knowing
  • terror attack — Violence done on purpose, often to scare a lot of people and push a political belief, not just to hurt one person.
  • far-right, ultranationalist — Extreme beliefs that say one's own country or group is better than others, in a way that can lead to hate toward people seen as different.

Gunman Kills Six in Kraków's Main Square in Attack Called Far-Right Terrorism

On Saturday, a man opened fire in the historic Main Market Square in Kraków, Poland, killing six people and wounding more than a dozen others. He died later in hospital. Poland's government has declared two days of national mourning, and the country's defense minister called the shooting a terror attack driven by far-right, ultranationalist beliefs.

Why this mattersAn attack like this in a crowded public square — the kind of place where you might walk with friends or family on a weekend — is a reminder that political extremism isn't just online arguments; it can turn into real violence against strangers. How Poland and other European countries respond, in terms of security, free speech, and watching for warning signs, will shape what public spaces feel like for everyone, not just Poles.
Read the whole story

It happened in one of Europe's most visited public spaces: the Main Market Square in Kraków, a medieval plaza usually filled with tourists, street performers, and people meeting for coffee. On Saturday, that changed in minutes. A gunman began shooting into the crowd, killing six people and injuring more than a dozen others before he was stopped. He later died in hospital, which means investigators cannot simply ask him why he did it — they have to reconstruct his motives from whatever evidence he left behind.

Poland's government reacted fast and seriously. It declared two days of national mourning, a formal period where flags are lowered and public events are often cancelled or quieted out of respect for the dead. More striking was the language used by the country's defense minister, who described the attack as an act of terrorism motivated by far-right ideology — meaning extreme nationalist and often racist political views that, in their violent form, can include believing certain groups of people don't belong in a country or deserve to be harmed.

Calling something terrorism rather than just a shooting is not a small distinction. Terrorism implies the violence was meant to send a political message and frighten a wider group of people, not just harm the immediate victims. When a government official attaches that label quickly, after a single day, it signals how seriously they're treating the ideological angle — but it also means the full picture is still being filled in behind the scenes.

That's because basic facts are still missing. Authorities haven't yet released detailed information about who the shooter was, whether he had ties to any organized far-right group or acted alone, or how he obtained the weapon he used. Poland has strict gun laws compared to some countries, so the question of how he got hold of a firearm matters a great deal for understanding whether this reveals a gap in the system or was always going to be hard to prevent.

The attack has drawn attention well beyond Poland's borders, partly because of how many people were hurt and partly because of what it represents. Europe has seen a string of politically motivated attacks in recent years, some from the far right and some from other extremes, and each one reopens a debate about whether governments are doing enough to track and stop radicalization before it turns violent. Kraków's case will likely become part of that larger argument, even as the specific details of this shooter and this attack are still being pieced together.

Words worth knowing
  • Far-right ideology — A set of extreme political beliefs on the nationalist end of the spectrum that, in violent forms, can include hostility toward immigrants, minorities, or anyone seen as not belonging to the nation.
  • Terror attack — Violence carried out to make a political point and spread fear among a wider group of people, not just to harm the immediate victims.
  • National mourning — An official period, declared by a government, when a country publicly marks grief over a tragedy, often by lowering flags or pausing celebrations.
