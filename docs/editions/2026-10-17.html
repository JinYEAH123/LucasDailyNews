On Friday, President Trump signed a special order that stopped a new rule from starting. That rule would have punished Russia for selling oil and fuel, as part of the response to Russia's war in Ukraine. The rule was about to begin in just a few days, but Trump paused it instead.

Russia has been fighting a war against its neighbor, Ukraine, for years. To push back, the United States made rules called 'sanctions.' These rules try to stop other countries from buying oil and fuel from Russia, so Russia has less money for its war.

Trump Pauses New Russia Sanctions Days Before They Were Set to Bite

On Friday, October 16, President Trump signed an executive order suspending a fresh round of sanctions on Russian oil and gas sales — sanctions that were about to take effect under a law he himself had signed only a month before. The White House pointed to a diesel-purchase arrangement and the broader 'national interest' as its reasoning. The order landed just three days after senators from both parties had publicly urged him to cancel it.

Why this mattersThis isn't just a story about Russia — it's about how much power one elected official has to override a law passed with support from both political parties, using a legal tool built into that very law. Decisions like this also ripple into everyday things you might not connect to Ukraine at all, like global oil and diesel prices, since Russia is one of the world's largest energy exporters.
Read the whole story

An executive order is a direct instruction from the president that doesn't need a vote in Congress — it's one of the fastest tools a president has to change policy overnight. Using one to pause sanctions that Congress had written into law is unusual, and the timing makes it more unusual still: the sanctions were only days from kicking in, aimed at squeezing Russia's income from selling oil and gas abroad, which is the single biggest source of money funding its war in Ukraine.

Sanctions are penalties — usually financial — that one country imposes on another, often by blocking companies or banks from doing business with it. Most sanctions laws include a built-in escape hatch called a waiver, which lets the president suspend the penalty if he decides it serves the 'national interest.' That's the provision Trump used here, citing an existing arrangement in which the US buys diesel fuel tied to Russian supply chains, and arguing that keeping it running matters more right now than enforcing the new penalties.

What makes this moment pointed is who was asking him not to. Just three days earlier, a bipartisan group of senators — meaning lawmakers from both the Republican and Democratic parties, who agree on very little these days — had publicly called on Trump to scrap the Russia diesel deal rather than protect it. The request came as his relationship with Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky has grown visibly colder, with the two men clashing over how the war should be fought and what Ukraine should be asked to give up to end it.

European governments, who have spent over three years coordinating sanctions and military aid alongside the US, are described as increasingly alarmed. For them, a wavering Washington isn't just an American story — it changes the calculation in Moscow, which has to decide whether the alliance against it is solid or starting to crack, and it changes the calculation in Kyiv, which depends on that alliance holding together to keep fighting.

Critics inside and outside Congress argue this isn't a one-time exception but a sign of where US policy is actually heading. Their argument is structural: when the same president who signs a sanctions law turns around and exempts its central target using a loophole in that same law, it raises the question of whether Congress can meaningfully bind a president's foreign policy at all, or whether sanctions bills are symbolic until a waiver quietly undoes them.

Words worth knowing

  • sanctions — Financial or trade penalties one country imposes on another, often to pressure it over its actions, such as blocking banks or companies from doing business with it.
  • bipartisan — Supported by members of both major political parties, rather than just one side.

Tech Stocks Have Their Worst Week Since June

On Friday, a big list of tech companies called the Nasdaq dropped sharply in value. It was the worst week for these companies since June. Computer chip companies, which make the tiny brains inside computers and AI systems, have been losing value for eight days in a row — the longest losing streak since 2008.

Why this mattersLots of grown-ups' retirement savings and jobs are connected to these tech companies, even if they don't work in tech themselves. If AI companies are spending more than they're earning, it could affect prices, jobs, and even how much new gadgets cost in the future.
Read the whole story

Imagine if everyone who owned a piece of your favorite toy company suddenly got nervous and decided to sell their piece at the same time. That's a bit like what happened with tech stocks this week. A "stock" is like owning a tiny slice of a company. When lots of people want to sell their slices at once, the price drops fast.

Chip companies — the ones that make the computer parts powering artificial intelligence (AI) — got hit hardest. They've now dropped in value for eight days straight. In just one day, AI-related software companies lost about $260 billion combined. To picture that: if you stacked $260 billion in dollar bills, the pile would reach higher than 17,000 Empire State Buildings.

Why are people worried? Companies have been spending huge amounts of money building AI systems, betting that AI will make them lots of money in the future. But some investors — people who put money into companies hoping to earn more back — are starting to wonder if that payoff is actually coming, or if companies are just borrowing and spending without enough proof it will work.

This isn't only an American story. Earlier in the week, chip companies in Asia, like Samsung and SK Hynix, lost more than a trillion dollars in value too. Some experts think this is just a healthy correction — prices getting more realistic after rising too fast. Others think it's a warning sign that the AI spending spree has gone further than it should.

Chip Stocks Just Had Their Worst Week Since the 2008 Crash — And It's About Whether AI Pays Off

On Friday, the Nasdaq — the stock exchange where most big tech and AI companies are listed — fell 2.14% in a single day, capping its worst week since June. Chipmaker stocks fell for an eighth straight trading session, their longest losing streak since the 2008 financial crisis. The sell-off wiped out roughly $260 billion in AI software stock value in one day alone, as investors started asking whether the hundreds of billions being poured into AI are actually going to make money.

Why this mattersIf you have a pension, a parent with a retirement account, or savings in an index fund, you likely own a slice of these companies without realising it — tech stocks make up a huge share of most ordinary investment portfolios, not just rich people's. A real slowdown in AI spending could also mean fewer new tech jobs, slower rollout of AI tools you might use at school or work, and a preview of how fast confidence can evaporate even in technology that genuinely works.
Read the whole story

For the past two years, companies like Microsoft, Google, Amazon and a wave of newer firms have been spending enormous sums building the physical backbone of artificial intelligence: warehouses full of specialised computer chips, cooled by industrial-scale air conditioning, running around the clock to train and operate AI systems like chatbots and image generators. This spending spree is often called the AI infrastructure buildout, and it has been one of the biggest drivers of stock market growth since 2023. The bet was simple — AI would become so useful, so fast, that whoever built the most computing power first would win.

On Friday that bet got a lot shakier. The Nasdaq Composite, an index that tracks thousands of tech-heavy stocks, dropped 2.14% in a single session, bringing its loss for the week to about 4.5% — its worst week since June. Chipmakers, the companies that make the processors AI runs on, fell for an eighth consecutive trading day, a losing streak not seen since 2008, the year the global financial system nearly collapsed. The question driving the sell-off isn't whether AI works. It's whether the companies spending fortunes to build it can actually earn that money back — what investors call return on investment, or ROI.

One company became the focus of that anxiety: CoreWeave, a firm that rents out AI computing power to other businesses. Like many AI infrastructure companies, CoreWeave has grown fast partly by taking on large amounts of debt — borrowed money it has to pay back with interest — to buy the expensive chips and build the data centres AI requires. That's normal for a company racing to expand, but it only works if customers keep paying enough, for long enough, to cover the debt. When investors started worrying that AI demand might not grow as fast as the spending has, CoreWeave's debt financing turned from a growth story into a warning sign — a reminder that someone has to eventually pay for all this hardware.

The scale of the reaction was striking even by tech-stock standards. AI-focused software companies alone lost around $260 billion in value in a single day — roughly the entire economic output of a mid-sized country, gone from paper valuations in a matter of hours. That wasn't an isolated US event, either. Earlier in the week, a separate but related sell-off had already erased more than a trillion dollars from Asian chip stocks, including South Korea's SK Hynix and Samsung, two of the world's largest chip manufacturers. Friday's numbers show that selling pressure didn't ease — it spread, moving specifically into US tech firms and the companies that finance AI expansion.

Not everyone reads this the same way. Some analysts think this is simply a healthy correction: markets got ahead of themselves, prices needed to come back down to something more realistic, and the underlying AI technology is still genuinely valuable and improving. Others think it's the first real crack in something closer to a bubble — a period where money poured into an idea faster than the idea could prove itself, similar to the dot-com crash of 2000, when internet companies with no profits were valued as if they'd already won. Both groups agree on one thing: investors are no longer willing to fund AI's growth on promises alone. They want to see the money coming back.

Words worth knowing
  • AI infrastructure buildout — The large-scale construction of data centres and purchase of specialised chips needed to run artificial intelligence systems.
  • Debt financing — Growing a business by borrowing money that must be repaid with interest, rather than only using money the company has already earned.
  • Return on investment (ROI) — The profit or value a company gets back from money it has spent, compared to how much it spent in the first place.

France's Biggest Protest in Years Turns Violent Over Rising Prices and Crumbling Schools

On Saturday, hundreds of thousands of people marched across France to protest the rising cost of living and poor conditions in public schools. In Paris, Lyon, and Marseille, some demonstrations turned violent: cars were set on fire, shops were looted, and police fired tear gas into crowds. More than 160 people were detained, and one protester lost a hand when a police crowd-control grenade exploded near him.

Why this mattersThe cost of living squeezing French families — higher prices for food, rent, and energy outpacing wages — is a pattern playing out in many countries right now, including ones you live in, which is partly why it keeps producing protests. And when a government has to decide how much tear gas and force to use against its own citizens, it's wrestling with a question every democracy faces: where is the line between keeping order and overreacting to people who are angry for real reasons?
Read the whole story

Saturday's protests brought together an unusual mix of people: university and high school students, teachers, members of labor unions (organizations that negotiate pay and working conditions on behalf of workers), and so-called 'yellow vest' activists — a loose protest movement, named for the fluorescent safety vests French drivers keep in their cars, that first erupted in 2018 over fuel taxes and has resurfaced periodically ever since to protest economic hardship. What united them this time was a shared frustration: prices for food, housing, and energy have climbed faster than many people's paychecks, and public schools — already short on teachers and funding — are, in protesters' view, being asked to do more with less.

The day started peacefully in most cities but escalated by evening. In Paris, demonstrators set fire to parked cars and police vehicles; some stores were broken into and emptied. Lyon and Marseille, France's second- and third-largest cities, saw their own fires. Police responded with tear gas, a chemical that irritates the eyes and lungs to break up crowds, and in at least one case fired a crowd-control grenade — a device designed to stun or disperse people with a loud blast and shock wave — that detonated close enough to a protester to blow off his hand. More than 160 people were taken into custody across the country by night's end.

Nobody agrees on exactly how many people showed up, and the gap matters. The CGT, one of France's largest and most left-leaning unions, put the national turnout at roughly 800,000. Al Jazeera, a Qatari-owned news outlet with no stake in French domestic politics, independently estimated the crowd at more than 500,000. Both figures would make this one of the largest protests in France in years, but the size of a protest shapes how seriously politicians take it — organizers have an incentive to count generously, and outside observers often count more conservatively, which is part of why the two numbers diverge so much.

The timing is not an accident. President Emmanuel Macron cannot run for reelection again — French presidents are limited to two consecutive terms — and the question of who leads the country next is already consuming French politics. A protest movement this size, cutting across generations and professions, sends a signal to every politician positioning themselves for that fight: economic frustration is not confined to one party's base, and whoever inherits Macron's job will inherit this anger too.

French governments have faced large protest movements before, including the original yellow vest uprising, and some won real concessions — delayed taxes, new spending — while others did not. What makes this one notable is less any single demand than the breadth of who turned out together: students worried about their schools, workers worried about their wages, and a protest movement born from a fuel tax seven years ago, all marching on the same day for overlapping reasons.

  • crowd-control grenade — A device police throw into a crowd that explodes with a loud bang and shock wave to scare or disperse people, which can cause serious injury if it goes off too close to someone.
  • yellow vests — A protest movement that began in France in 2018 over fuel taxes, named for the reflective safety vests drivers wear, which has returned periodically to protest economic hardship.

  1. If some people in a mostly peaceful march start breaking things, should the whole crowd be blamed, or only the people who did it?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Blame the whole crowd

    • If you choose to join a huge march, you know some people might cause trouble, so everyone shares a little responsibility for being there.
