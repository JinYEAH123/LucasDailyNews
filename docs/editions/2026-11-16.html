On Monday, China announced something that has never happened before. This Thursday, the leaders of China, Russia, and the United States will meet together in the Chinese city of Shenzhen. No one knows exactly what they will agree on, but just sitting down together is historic.

Why this mattersWhen the leaders of the three most powerful countries sit together, decisions they make, or don't make, can affect wars, prices in stores, and even how safe the world feels from dangerous weapons. You probably won't notice anything different at your dinner table this week, but choices made at that table in Shenzhen could shape news for months.

Imagine the three biggest kids in your whole school, the ones everyone looks up to, finally sitting at the same lunch table. That is basically what is happening this week. China's leader, Xi Jinping, is hosting Russia's leader, Vladimir Putin, and America's leader, Donald Trump, together in a city called Shenzhen.

This has never happened before. These three countries are some of the most powerful in the world, and they often disagree loudly with each other. But a sitting leader from all three has never appeared at the same table at the same time, until now.

For the First Time Ever, the Leaders of the US, Russia and China Will Sit at One Table

China's government confirmed this week that President Xi Jinping will host both Russian President Vladimir Putin and US President Donald Trump in the city of Shenzhen on Thursday. No sitting leader of all three countries has ever appeared together at the same meeting before. Officials say topics may include nuclear weapons, the Middle East, trade, and possibly the war in Ukraine — but no agenda has actually been agreed or published yet.

Why this mattersDecisions made by these three governments shape prices, wars, and the risk of nuclear conflict far beyond their own borders — including things like the cost of goods you buy or whether a war elsewhere spreads. A single meeting rarely fixes any of that, but how leaders who distrust each other choose to talk, or not talk, affects how safe and stable the next few years feel for everyone.

Three of the most powerful people on Earth are about to sit down together for the first time in history. China has confirmed that President Xi Jinping will host US President Donald Trump and Russian President Vladimir Putin in Shenzhen, a major Chinese tech city near Hong Kong, this Thursday. It sounds like the kind of meeting that should have happened many times before — but it hasn't. The US and Russia have had plenty of one-on-one summits, and the US and China have too, but never all three leaders at once, at the same table.

That matters because these three countries control the largest nuclear arsenals and the largest economies on the planet, and they are also, in different ways, rivals. The US and China are locked in a trade and technology rivalry. The US and its allies have armed Ukraine against Russia's invasion. China has become Russia's most important economic partner since Western sanctions hit Moscow. Getting the three men in one room, rather than talking past each other through statements and phone calls, is being treated as significant for that reason alone — regardless of what, if anything, gets decided.

What exactly will be discussed is genuinely unclear, and that's not just journalists being vague — officials on all sides have declined to confirm a fixed agenda. The likely topics being floated include nuclear non-proliferation (efforts to stop more countries building nuclear weapons, and to manage the ones that already exist), tensions in the Middle East, trade disputes, and the ongoing war in Ukraine. But floated is the key word. Some analysts who study these summits expect the meeting to produce broad, symbolic statements — leaders standing together, agreeing in general terms that dialogue is good — rather than any specific deal or treaty.

There's a political backdrop to Trump's trip worth knowing. He left for Asia the same day this news broke, and American coverage has noted that he and his party are still dealing with the fallout from recent losses in the US midterm elections — the elections held partway through a president's term that typically decide control of Congress. A high-profile overseas summit, especially one this historically unusual, gives a leader a chance to dominate headlines with something other than domestic political trouble, and some commentators have pointed that out as a possible motive, alongside the genuine diplomatic stakes.

It's also worth sitting with how strange this pairing is on paper. Putin is wanted by the International Criminal Court over the war in Ukraine, a body neither the US nor China fully recognises in this context. Xi leads a one-party state that the US regularly criticises over human rights and Taiwan. Trump has spent years threatening both countries with tariffs and sanctions. None of that history disappears because three men share a table — but the fact that they're doing it anyway, in public, is itself the story.

  • Summit — A meeting between top leaders of different countries, usually to discuss major issues between their governments.
  • Midterm elections — US elections held halfway through a president's four-year term, which can shift control of Congress without changing the presidency itself.

America Tightens the Rules on Its Super-Smart Computer Chips

On Monday, the US government said that giant computer warehouses in five countries now need special permission before they can send out powerful AI chips. The rule is meant to stop Chinese companies from sneaking access to these chips through a side door. It comes right after the boss of Nvidia, the company that makes many of these chips, quit his job over a personal scandal involving a suspected spy.

Why this mattersChips like these power things you might already use without knowing it, like game consoles, translation apps, and smart speakers. When countries fight over who gets the best chips, it changes which gadgets get built, how fast they improve, and who gets to use them first.
Read the whole story

Some computer chips are much smarter and faster than others. The best ones can run AI, the kind of computer program that can chat, recognize pictures, or help drive a car. Nvidia, an American company, makes many of the best AI chips in the world.

The US government does not want these super chips ending up in China, because it worries China could use them for things that threaten American safety. But there is a trick. Huge buildings full of powerful computers, called data centers, sit in countries like Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, and Brazil. The US thinks Chinese companies have been renting computer time in those buildings over the internet, almost like borrowing a neighbor's tool through a window instead of buying your own.

So this week, the US made a new rule. Anyone running a data center in those five countries now needs a license, which is like a permission slip from the US government, before they can let those chips be used this way. The goal is to close that side door for good.

This happened right after Nvidia's boss, Jensen Huang, resigned because of claims he had a relationship with someone suspected of spying for China. President Trump said he would welcome Huang back and claimed the relationship caused no safety problems. But Nvidia's board says it has not found proof that secrets were leaked, and Congress is still investigating, so nobody knows for sure yet.

Words worth knowing
  • data center — A huge building packed with powerful computers that other people can use over the internet, even from far away.
  • loophole — A gap or trick in a rule that lets someone do something the rule was supposed to stop.

The US Just Closed a Chip Loophole — Days After Nvidia's CEO Quit Over a Spy Scandal

On Monday, the US Commerce Department ordered data centers in five countries to get a government license before sending on advanced American AI chips. The rule is meant to stop Chinese companies from reaching restricted Nvidia chips through a back door. It lands just days after Nvidia's CEO resigned over an alleged affair with a suspected Chinese spy, and the two stories are now tangled together in an argument about what actually counts as a national security risk.

Why this mattersThe chips at the centre of this story already shape things you use every day, from the AI behind search engines and phone assistants to the systems that recommend videos — and who controls access to them is becoming a serious point of tension between the US and China. The Huang story is a reminder that huge technology companies are run by individual people whose private lives can suddenly become matters of government concern, not just gossip.
Read the whole story

The United States has spent years trying to stop its most advanced AI chips — the kind that power things like ChatGPT and self-driving car systems — from reaching China, which it sees as a rival in the race to build powerful AI. The main tool is export controls: rules that ban American chipmakers like Nvidia from selling their best products directly to Chinese buyers. But rules written for direct sales left a gap. Instead of buying restricted chips outright, companies could reportedly rent computing time on servers sitting in a data center in a third country — Singapore, say, or Indonesia — and run their AI work on American chips without ever owning one or crossing a border with it. Monday's interim rule, which takes effect immediately rather than waiting through the usual months of public comment, orders operators of data centers in Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil to get a US government license before exporting those chips onward, closing off that remote-access route.

The timing is awkward for Nvidia, the company that makes most of the chips in question and whose business depends on both selling as many of them as legally possible and staying on the right side of Washington's rules. Last week, Nvidia's chief executive, Jensen Huang, resigned after it emerged he had allegedly had an affair with a woman suspected of being a Chinese intelligence operative. Huang had been one of the most recognisable figures in tech, the public face of the AI boom, and his sudden exit immediately raised a harder question than the affair itself: did any sensitive information about Nvidia's chips or its dealings with the US government pass through that relationship?

Nvidia's board says no — it has stated it has found no evidence that any secrets were compromised. President Trump has gone further, saying publicly that he is open to Huang returning to the company and that the affair posed no national security risk at all. But that is Trump's own assessment, not a verified conclusion, and it is being made by the same administration that just tightened export rules because of exactly this kind of worry — chips and sensitive tech reaching Chinese hands through indirect routes. Congress is now running its own inquiry into the matter, which means the question of what, if anything, Huang's relationship exposed is still open. A person familiar with Nvidia's board told the business news channel CNBC that directors are not currently considering bringing him back, regardless of what the president has said.

So there are two separate decisions happening at once, made by different people for different reasons, that nonetheless point at the same underlying worry: can the US actually control who gets access to its most powerful AI chips once they leave American soil, whether through a server in Jakarta or a relationship inside Nvidia's own leadership? The chip rule treats the risk as structural — a gap in the system that needs fixing regardless of who is involved. The Huang situation treats it as personal — one executive's judgment and loyalty under question. Trump's comments blur the two by waving away the personal risk just as the government is spending real effort closing the structural one.

None of this changes the basic fact that Nvidia's chips remain the ones everyone wants. Governments, universities and companies around the world depend on them to build AI systems, and that dependence is precisely why both the export rule and the Huang scandal matter beyond one company's boardroom — they are both about who gets to decide where the most valuable technology in the world is allowed to go.

Words worth knowing
  • interim rule — A government regulation that takes effect right away, skipping the usual months-long process of collecting public feedback first, because the issue is treated as urgent.
  • export controls — Laws that restrict which products — in this case, advanced computer chips — a country's companies are allowed to sell to certain foreign buyers.
  • remote access — Using powerful computer hardware over the internet from far away, without ever physically owning or transporting the hardware yourself.

China's Shops and Factories Grew Slower Than Expected in October

New numbers from China show people bought less in stores last month than experts guessed they would. Factories also made things at a slower pace than the month before. Together, these numbers hint that China's giant economy might be cooling down.

Why this mattersChina makes an enormous share of the toys, phones, clothes, and gadgets used around the world — including things you might own. When its factories and shops slow down, it can eventually affect prices and jobs far beyond China, including places connected to trade with the US.
Read the whole story

China is a country of about 1.4 billion people — that's more than four times the number of people in the United States. Every month, the government counts how much stuff people buy in shops, and how much stuff factories make, called retail sales and industrial output.

In October, shopping only grew by 0.4% compared to last year. Experts had guessed it would grow twice as much, by 0.8%. Imagine saving up and expecting your allowance to double, but it only grows a tiny bit — that's the kind of gap we're talking about.

Factories slowed down too. The month before, factory output had grown by 5.5%, the fastest in five months. In October, that growth dropped to 4.8%. It's like a runner who was speeding up each week suddenly running a little slower than before.

People who study money and economies say this slowdown matters because China sells huge amounts of goods to other countries, including the United States. If people inside China are buying less, China may lean even more on selling things abroad — which makes it more exposed if trade arguments with the US get worse.

Words worth knowing
  • retail sales — The total amount of money people spend buying things in stores, like food, clothes, or toys.
  • industrial output — How much stuff factories make, such as cars, phones, or furniture.

China's Shoppers Have Stopped Spending Like They Used To

New government figures show Chinese shoppers barely increased their spending in October, growing just 0.4% compared to the same month last year, far below the 0.8% that analysts had predicted. At the same time, factory and mining output grew only 4.8%, a notable drop from September's pace. Economists say the numbers suggest China's economy is slowing in a way that could leave it more vulnerable if trade tensions with the United States worsen.

Why this mattersChina is the world's second-largest economy and a major trading partner for the US and countless other countries, so a real slowdown there can mean cheaper goods sitting unsold in factories, less demand for raw materials your country might export, and knock-on effects for companies and jobs tied to global supply chains. It's also a reminder that economic numbers aren't just abstract statistics — they reflect millions of individual decisions by shoppers about whether to feel confident enough to spend.
Read the whole story

Imagine a country where, for years, people kept buying more stuff every year almost automatically — more phones, more clothes, more furniture — because incomes were rising and people felt confident about the future. That's roughly how China's economy has worked for the past two decades. The new data suggests that pattern is breaking down. Retail sales, which track how much people spend in stores and online, grew only 0.4% in October compared to a year earlier. Economists who study China had expected double that.

Factories told a similar story. Industrial output — a measure of how much China's mines, mills, and factories actually produced — grew 4.8% year-over-year in October. That sounds like solid growth until you compare it to September, when the same measure grew 5.5%, itself described as a five-month high. A slowdown from a recent peak doesn't necessarily mean collapse, but it does mean the engine is losing some of its horsepower just as it had seemed to be picking up.

Why does this matter beyond China's borders? For years, Chinese consumers and factories have been one of the major forces driving global economic growth — buying raw materials from other countries, manufacturing goods the whole world uses, and generally acting as a second major engine for the world economy alongside the United States. If Chinese households pull back on spending, and Chinese factories produce less, that ripples outward: to companies that sell into the Chinese market, to countries that export raw materials to China, and to the global supply chains that run through Chinese ports and factories.

There's a specific worry analysts raised in connection with these numbers: a weaker domestic economy leaves China more exposed if trade tensions with the US flare up again. The logic is straightforward. If Chinese consumers were spending heavily and the domestic economy were roaring, China could absorb some pain from tariffs or trade restrictions by leaning on internal demand. With domestic demand already fading, there's less of a cushion — making the economy more sensitive to external shocks it doesn't control.

It's worth being honest about how solid this account is. The interpretation here — that this points to a 'deepening slowdown' and greater vulnerability — comes from one news organization's reporting on the government's official data. A second, independently owned outlet to confirm or challenge that framing wasn't available before this was written, so readers should treat the analysis, though not the raw numbers themselves, as resting on a single source for now. The numbers are undisputed; what they mean is where the real argument begins.

That argument splits economists into two camps. Some think this is a temporary dip — perhaps tied to a shaky housing market, cautious consumers after years of pandemic disruption, or a seasonal lull — that will correct itself as confidence returns. Others think it reflects something more structural: a fundamental shift in how Chinese households save and spend, tied to deeper issues like property market troubles, an aging population, and years of households saving more out of uncertainty rather than spending freely. Which camp is right will shape how worried the rest of the world should be.

Words worth knowing
  • Year-over-year — A way of comparing a number, like sales or output, to the same month or period exactly one year earlier, to see real growth rather than normal seasonal ups and downs.
  • Industrial output — The total amount produced by a country's factories, mines, and utilities, used as a key signal of how strong the broader economy is.
  • Structural slowdown — A decline in economic activity caused by deep, long-lasting changes — like shifting demographics or habits — rather than a temporary dip that naturally bounces back.
