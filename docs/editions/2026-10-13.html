China Is Making More of Its Own Computer Brains Instead of Buying America's

Nvidia is an American company that makes special computer chips used to run artificial intelligence, or AI. In the first half of 2026, Nvidia sold far fewer of these chips in China than before. At the same time, a Chinese company called Huawei sold a lot more of its own AI chips. Two different governments' rules helped cause this swap.

Why this mattersAI chips power things like voice assistants, video game graphics, and the computer programs that can write stories or spot diseases in medical scans. When one country can't buy these chips from another, it has to build its own factories and inventions, which changes who controls this powerful technology for years to come. This kind of competition between countries can shape what phones, cars, and robots look like by the time you're grown up.
Read the whole story

Nvidia shipped 678,000 AI chips into China this year. If you counted to 678,000 out loud, one number every second, it would take you more than a week without stopping to sleep. That sounds like a lot, but it's actually 44% less than Nvidia sold the year before, almost like losing half of all your Halloween candy overnight.

Meanwhile, Huawei, a Chinese tech company, shipped 572,000 of its own AI chips, almost catching up to Nvidia. A few years ago Huawei barely made chips like this at all. Now it controls 28% of the market, while Nvidia's share shrank to 34%. That means out of every chip sold, Nvidia used to sell most of them, and now it only sells about one in three.

Two things caused this shift. The United States government put rules in place that block Nvidia from selling its most powerful chips to China, worried they could be used to build powerful weapons or spy tools. China's own government then added its own rules, making it harder for companies there to buy even the Nvidia chips that are still allowed.

Experts who study this say the American rules may be backfiring in one way. Instead of just slowing China down, the rules are pushing Chinese companies to build their own chips faster, like Huawei is doing. One report found this, but no other independent newsroom has checked the numbers yet, so it's worth remembering this comes from a single source.

Nvidia's China Sales Collapse by Nearly Half — and Huawei Is the One Picking Up the Pieces

Nvidia, the world's dominant maker of AI chips, shipped 44% fewer units into China's data-center market in the first half of 2026 than it did a year earlier, according to a report from the research firm IDC cited by Reuters. Its market share there fell to 34%, while Huawei's climbed to 28%. The squeeze is coming from both directions at once: Washington restricting what Nvidia can sell, and Beijing now restricting what its own companies can buy.

Why this mattersThis is a preview of a fight that will shape what computer chips end up inside the phones, cars, and AI tools you use for the next decade: whether countries can wall themselves off from each other's technology, and what it costs them to try. If China successfully builds a self-sufficient chip industry, the global tech market splits into two separate tracks — American and Chinese — instead of one shared supply chain, which could change prices, innovation speed, and who controls the AI tools spreading everywhere.

Nvidia makes the specialized computer chips — called AI accelerators — that train and run artificial intelligence systems like chatbots and image generators. For years, Nvidia's chips were considered so far ahead of any rival's that Chinese tech companies bought them whenever they legally could, even at a steep markup. That dominance is now cracking in a way that matters well beyond one company's balance sheet.

The first force squeezing Nvidia is American policy. Since 2022, the U.S. government has restricted which of Nvidia's most powerful chips can be legally exported to China, worried that cutting-edge AI hardware could boost China's military and surveillance capabilities. Nvidia has repeatedly built watered-down versions of its chips specifically to stay inside the rules, but each version has eventually been restricted too, shrinking what it's allowed to sell.

The second force is new, and in some ways more surprising: China's own government has started discouraging domestic companies from buying even the Nvidia chips that remain legal. Reuters reports new licensing curbs from Beijing itself, nudging state-linked firms and major buyers toward Chinese-made alternatives instead. In effect, China is choosing to wean itself off Nvidia faster than it strictly has to, treating dependence on foreign chips as a vulnerability worth sacrificing some performance to fix.

The chip stepping into that gap is Huawei's, a Chinese telecom-and-tech giant that has spent years building its own AI chip line after facing its own set of U.S. sanctions since 2019. Huawei shipped 572,000 units in the same six-month period — still fewer than Nvidia's 678,000, but growing fast, and closing a gap that once looked unbridgeable. Analysts quoted in the report describe this as evidence of a pattern U.S. policymakers may not have fully intended: instead of simply slowing China's AI progress by denying it top chips, the restrictions appear to be speeding up China's push to build a fully domestic alternative.

It's worth noting a limit on how this story is being reported: every account traceable in this search leads back to the same original IDC data and the same Reuters report, republished by other outlets rather than independently confirmed by a second newsroom with its own reporting. The underlying numbers are credible — IDC is a respected research firm whose shipment estimates are widely used across the tech industry — but readers should know the figures haven't yet been cross-checked by a separate source with independent access to the same market.

  • Market share — The percentage of all sales in a particular market that one company captures, used here to measure how much of China's AI-chip business belongs to Nvidia versus Huawei.

China Punishes Two Ministers From Small Countries Over Taiwan

China has punished two government ministers this week, one from Paraguay and one from Belize. Their countries both say the island of Taiwan is its own separate country, something China strongly disagrees with. China froze their money and banned them from two of its cities.

Why this mattersThis is a story about a big, powerful country trying to pressure smaller ones into agreeing with it. It's a bit like a big kid telling smaller kids in the playground what they're allowed to believe, and punishing the ones who don't go along. How countries handle disagreements like this affects whether smaller nations feel safe sticking to their own opinions.
Read the whole story

Meet the two men. Gustavo Villate runs technology for Paraguay, a country in South America. William Fonseca handles foreign relations for Belize, a small country in Central America. This week, both got hit with a surprise punishment from China.

China announced sanctions against them. That means any money they have sitting in Chinese banks gets frozen, like a video game character stuck in ice, unable to move. They also can't travel to Hong Kong or Macau, two cities that belong to China. And no Chinese company or person is allowed to do business with them.

The reason goes back to an island called Taiwan. China says Taiwan belongs to it. Taiwan says it is already its own country. Most of the world's roughly 195 countries agree with China on paper, but 12 countries, including Paraguay and Belize, officially say Taiwan is separate, about as many players as on a soccer team.

This is new. China usually punishes officials in places like the United States or Europe who speak up for Taiwan. This is the first time it has punished ministers from one of Taiwan's own allies. It happened two days after Taiwan's president gave a speech defending his country's growing military spending.

Words worth knowing
  • Sanctions — Sanctions are punishments one country uses against people or places it disagrees with, such as freezing their money or banning them from visiting.
  • Diplomatic recognition — When one country officially says that another country is real and separate, treating it as its own nation, that's called diplomatic recognition.

China Sanctions Two Foreign Ministers for Backing Taiwan

China has sanctioned two sitting cabinet ministers for the first time over their countries' decision to diplomatically recognize Taiwan instead of China. The targets are Gustavo Villate, Paraguay's information technology minister, and William Fonseca, Belize's foreign affairs minister. It's a notable shift: Beijing usually reserves this kind of punishment for Western politicians who criticize its Taiwan policy from the outside, not officials from Taiwan's own small circle of diplomatic allies.

Why this mattersThis story is a small, concrete example of how major powers increasingly use targeted punishments against individual officials rather than open conflict - a tool any government, including the one you live under, could someday face or use. It also shows that a small country's foreign policy choices can carry real personal consequences for the people who make them, even when the bigger dispute driving it all plays out mostly between superpowers far from their borders.
Read the whole story

China's government announced sanctions against two sitting ministers this week: Gustavo Villate, who oversees information technology in Paraguay, and William Fonseca, Belize's foreign affairs and trade minister. Their offense, according to Beijing, was serving in governments that officially recognize Taiwan as its own country rather than accepting China's position that Taiwan belongs to it. The sanctions freeze any assets the two men hold in China, bar them from entering Hong Kong and Macau, and forbid Chinese companies or individuals from doing business with them.

To understand why this matters, it helps to know the basics of the dispute. China's government considers Taiwan a breakaway territory that must eventually come under its control, and it has not ruled out using force to make that happen. Taiwan, meanwhile, has governed itself as a democracy for more than seven decades, with its own elected government, currency, and military. Over the years, most countries have switched which government they officially recognize - from Taiwan to Beijing - partly because keeping trade and political ties with China, the world's second-largest economy, matters more to them than keeping ties with Taiwan. Today only around 12 countries, mostly small nations in Central America, the Caribbean, and the Pacific, still formally recognize Taiwan instead of China.

What makes this week's sanctions different is who they targeted. Until now, China's punishments over Taiwan have mostly hit politicians in the United States or Europe who spoke out against its policies - people who never had official ties to Taiwan to begin with. This is the first time Beijing has punished officials from a country that is itself one of Taiwan's dozen remaining allies. News outlet Bloomberg flagged this as a meaningful shift: it suggests China is now willing to apply direct pressure even to the shrinking group of governments that still side with Taiwan, not just to outside critics.

The timing adds another layer. The sanctions came two days after Taiwan's president, Lai Ching-te, gave a National Day speech defending his government's decision to keep increasing defense spending, arguing it was necessary to discourage China from attempting an invasion. The sanctions weren't officially linked to that speech, but they landed in the same week Taiwan's leadership was publicly making its case for a stronger military, in the middle of an ongoing argument about how much deterrence actually deters.

In practical terms, the sanctions will likely change very little for either minister. Officials from Paraguay and Belize rarely travel to China or hold assets there, so there's not much for Beijing's measures to actually freeze or block. What analysts are watching instead is the precedent it sets: China has now shown it's willing to formally punish individual officials from countries that recognize Taiwan, a move it could repeat or escalate against the other 10 or so countries that still do the same.

Words worth knowing
  • Diplomatic recognition — When one country officially treats another country's government as legitimate and establishes formal political ties with it.
  • Sanctions — Official penalties, like frozen money or travel bans, that one government imposes on another country, organization, or person.
  • Precedent — An action or decision that sets a pattern future situations are likely to be compared to or handled by.

Two American Professors Win Top Prize for Studying Shopping and Prices

On Monday, two professors from the United States won one of the biggest prizes in the world for figuring out how people decide what to buy and how stores decide what to charge. Their ideas now help the people who run a country's money system spot trouble before it gets too big.

Why this mattersWhen prices for things you buy, like snacks or a new bike, suddenly go up, it is not random. These two professors spent their careers explaining why that happens and what grown-ups in charge of money can do about it. The tools they built are still used today whenever a country tries to stop prices from getting out of control.
Read the whole story

Robert Hall, from Stanford University, and Michael Woodford, from Columbia University, just won the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences. It is one of the most famous prizes a thinker can win, announced every year in Stockholm, a city in Sweden.

Their big idea sounds simple. They studied how a family decides whether to buy something now or wait, and how a shop decides whether to raise or lower its prices. It turns out these small, everyday choices, multiplied across millions of people, explain a lot about whether a whole country's economy is healthy or shaky.

Banks that manage a country's money, like the Federal Reserve in the United States, use these ideas like a weather forecast. They help predict when an economy is about to speed up too fast or slow down too much, the way a see-saw tips one way then the other.

The judges said this work was especially useful during the pandemic, when prices for things like groceries and gas jumped suddenly almost everywhere. Understanding why that happens helps banks decide what to do about it.

Words worth knowing
  • central bank — A special bank that manages a whole country's money and tries to keep prices and jobs steady, like the Federal Reserve in the United States.
  • boom-bust cycle — The pattern where an economy speeds up and does really well for a while, then slows down and struggles, over and over, like a see-saw.

Two Economists Win This Year's Nobel-Style Prize for Explaining Why Recessions Happen — and How to Soften Them

On Monday, October 12, the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences in Stockholm announced that American economists Robert E. Hall of Stanford and Michael Woodford of Columbia had won this year's economics prize. Their work explains how ordinary people and businesses actually decide what to buy and what prices to charge — and turned those small, everyday decisions into the models central banks now use to steer entire economies through booms and busts.

Why this mattersThe ideas this prize celebrates are the reason your family's mortgage rate, the interest on a car loan, or the price of groceries can shift when a central bank changes policy — those decisions trace back to models built on exactly this kind of research. Understanding why prices are 'sticky' and why recessions happen at all helps explain the economic news you hear about inflation and interest rates, rather than leaving it as background noise.
Read the whole story

Every autumn, the Nobel committees in Stockholm and Oslo hand out prizes in physics, chemistry, medicine, literature, peace, and economics. This year's economics prize went to two Americans who spent decades studying something that sounds almost too ordinary to win an award for: how people decide when to spend money, and how businesses decide when to raise their prices.

That might sound like a strange thing to build a career on, but it matters enormously for understanding recessions — the periods when an economy shrinks, people lose jobs, and spending dries up. For a long time, economists built big-picture models of entire national economies without carefully working out how individual households and firms actually behave. Hall and Woodford helped fix that. Their research gave large-scale economic models solid 'microfoundations' — a bottom-up starting point grounded in real decision-making, rather than just broad assumptions about how an economy moves.

This work became a cornerstone of what's called New Keynesian economics, an approach that explains why prices don't adjust instantly when the economy changes. If a coffee shop's costs suddenly rise, the owner doesn't necessarily change the menu price that same week — there's a delay, a stickiness, partly because changing prices has its own costs and partly because businesses worry about annoying customers. That stickiness is exactly why economies can swing into booms and busts rather than smoothly correcting themselves, and it's why central banks — the institutions, like the US Federal Reserve, that control interest rates — can influence the economy at all by raising or lowering the cost of borrowing.

The prize committee specifically pointed to recent history as proof the work still matters: the economic shocks of the COVID-19 pandemic, when supply chains broke down and prices spiked unpredictably, tested exactly the kind of models Hall and Woodford helped build. Central bankers around the world used New Keynesian-style frameworks to decide how aggressively to raise interest rates as inflation surged, then how quickly to ease off as it cooled.

It's worth knowing that this prize isn't technically one of the original Nobel Prizes that Alfred Nobel's will created in 1895. It's formally called the Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel, set up by Sweden's central bank in 1968 and awarded alongside the others each year. It carries the same prestige, the same ceremony, and a similarly large cash award, but some critics have long argued that attaching Nobel's name to it gives economics — a field where theories can fall in and out of favor — an aura of hard-science certainty it doesn't always deserve.

With this announcement, the 2026 Nobel season is now complete. Hall and Woodford join a long list of economists whose academic work on decision-making, pricing, and markets has quietly shaped decisions that affect millions of people who have never read a word of their research.

Words worth knowing
  • New Keynesian economics — An approach to studying national economies that explains why prices and wages adjust slowly rather than instantly, which is why interest rate changes by central banks can actually affect jobs and spending.
  • Microfoundations — The idea of building big economic models starting from how individual people and businesses actually make decisions, rather than just guessing at broad national patterns.
  • Central bank — A country's official institution — like the US Federal Reserve — that controls interest rates and the money supply to try to keep prices stable and employment steady.
