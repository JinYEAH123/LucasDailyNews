Six Giant Tech Companies Promised to Build AI Safely — But Will They Keep Their Word?

The bosses of six huge companies that build artificial intelligence just made a big promise at the White House: they said they would build AI carefully and safely. Only days later, a famous whistleblower said she doesn't believe they'll really keep that promise. And one of the people whose job was to make AI safer just quit, saying the company he worked for cares more about speed than safety.

Why this mattersYou might use AI already, maybe to help with homework or to chat with a game character. These are the exact companies building the AI tools that will shape how you learn, play, and even get information for years to come. If the people building them don't truly follow their own safety promises, mistakes or dangers could slip through — which is why some people think stronger rules are needed, not just promises.
Read the whole story

Six powerful companies make the computer programs called AI — artificial intelligence — that can chat with you, write essays, or answer questions like a very fast, very knowledgeable robot brain. The people in charge of these companies, including the bosses of Google, Meta, and the company that makes a car called Tesla, all signed a paper promising to build AI safely.

But here's the catch: it's just a promise. Nobody can punish them if they break it. No judge, no police officer, no government rule says they have to follow it. It only works if they choose to keep their word.

Right after the promise was signed, a woman named Frances Haugen spoke up. She used to work at Facebook and became famous for warning people about problems there. She said she doubts these companies will really follow the promise in their hearts, even if they technically follow the words.

Then something else happened. A man named David Robinson, who had worked longer than anyone else on keeping one AI company's programs safe, quit his job. He said the company's culture was broken — that it cared more about building things fast than building them safely.

  • Non-binding pledge — A promise that nobody is forced by law to keep — if you break it, nothing official happens to you.
  • Whistleblower — Someone who used to work inside a company or government and speaks up publicly about problems they saw there.

Six AI Giants Promised to Build Safely. Within Days, Doubts Set In.

The chief executives of Anthropic, Google, Meta, xAI and Nvidia, plus OpenAI's president standing in for Sam Altman, gathered at the White House to sign a pledge on building artificial intelligence safely. Within days, a well-known tech whistleblower said she doubts they'll keep their word, and one of OpenAI's most senior safety staff quit, calling the company's safety culture 'broken.' The episode has reopened a long-running argument: can the companies building the world's most powerful AI be trusted to police themselves?

Why this mattersThe AI tools you already use — chatbots that help with homework, image generators, voice assistants — are built by these same six companies, and the next versions will be more capable and more embedded in daily life. Whether those companies are checked by enforceable law or only by their own promises will shape how much you can trust what they build, long before most people have a say in it.
Read the whole story

Six of the most powerful people in artificial intelligence stood behind a new agreement called the 'Joint Commitment on Frontier Responsibilities.' 'Frontier AI' is industry shorthand for the most advanced systems being built — ones more capable than whatever chatbot or image generator the public is currently using, and closer to the edge of what's technically possible. The pledge, brokered by the White House, commits the signing companies to test their systems for serious risks before release, be more transparent about what those systems can do, and report dangerous capabilities when they find them.

There's a catch built into the word 'commitment.' This is a voluntary, non-binding agreement — meaning no regulator, court, or government agency can fine a company or shut down a product if it breaks the promise. It works more like a public handshake than a law. Supporters argue that reputational damage is still a real cost: a company caught breaking a promise it made at the White House, in front of cameras, risks losing public trust and business partnerships. Critics counter that reputational damage rarely outweighs the financial upside of releasing a powerful product before a competitor does.

That skepticism found a prominent voice almost immediately. Frances Haugen, the former Facebook employee who in 2021 leaked internal documents showing the company knew its products harmed teenagers' mental health, said publicly that she doubts these AI companies will honor the 'spirit' of their new pledge — as opposed to just the technical letter of it. Her point was that a company can follow the literal rules of an agreement while still behaving in a way that defeats the whole purpose, similar to how someone might not cheat on a test directly but still look up every answer the night before.

Then came a second, quieter but arguably more striking development. David Robinson, OpenAI's longest-serving safety staffer, resigned, saying the company's 'culture is broken' when it comes to choosing safety over speed. Robinson wasn't an outside critic or a journalist — he was inside the company that built ChatGPT, watching decisions get made for years. His resignation matters because it's one thing for an outsider to suspect a company is cutting corners; it's another for someone who spent years inside the building to say so on the way out the door.

Together, the two stories have sharpened a debate that's been simmering since AI chatbots went mainstream a few years ago: should the companies racing to build increasingly powerful AI be left to regulate themselves through pledges and internal review boards, or does the technology now need binding rules — enforceable by law, with real penalties — the way airlines, drug companies, and banks eventually got? The United States has so far leaned toward the voluntary approach, while the European Union has moved further toward binding legislation. This week's events are being read by each side as proof they were right all along.

Words worth knowing

  • Non-binding commitment — A promise with no legal force behind it, meaning no one can be fined, sued, or punished for breaking it.
  • Whistleblower — Someone who works or worked inside an organization and speaks publicly about problems or wrongdoing they witnessed there.

The US Is Sending More Warships and Marines Toward Iran

The United States is moving a third giant aircraft carrier and thousands more Marines toward the Middle East. People inside the government say President Trump might order new bombing strikes on Iran, but only after an election happening in the US on November 3. Nobody has said this plan out loud in public yet — it comes from officials who talked to reporters without giving their names.

Why this mattersWhen countries argue near oil-producing regions, the price of oil can jump around the world — and oil is what makes gasoline for cars and fuel for planes. If fighting starts again, prices for things your family buys, like gas or plane tickets, could go up. It also means thousands of soldiers and sailors might be sent far from their families, which affects real people, not just headlines.

For eight months, the United States and Iran have been in an off-and-on conflict. Sometimes it looks calm. Sometimes it gets tense again.

Now the US is sending a third aircraft carrier group to the region. That is a huge ship packed with fighter jets, plus smaller ships that protect it, like a floating airport surrounded by bodyguards. Along with the ship, somewhere between 2,000 and 9,000 more Marines — trained soldiers who fight on land and sea — are being sent too.

Talks between the two countries to solve their problems peacefully have stalled, meaning they are stuck and not moving forward. At the same time, Iran's money has dropped to its lowest value ever against the US dollar, which means things cost a lot more for people living there. The country of Qatar has been trying to help both sides talk, but so far nothing has been agreed.

Some officials say President Trump is thinking about bombing Iran again, but only after the November 3 elections are finished. The White House and the Pentagon, which runs the US military, have not confirmed this plan directly.

A Third US Aircraft Carrier Heads Toward Iran, and Washington Weighs Strikes After the Vote

The United States is sending a third aircraft carrier strike group, plus thousands more Marines, toward the Middle East, the biggest military buildup since tensions with Iran flared eight months ago. Officials who were not named, speaking to the Wall Street Journal and other outlets, say President Trump is considering a new round of bombing strikes on Iran once the November 3 midterm elections are over, though nobody in government will confirm that on the record. The buildup comes as Iran's currency has collapsed to its lowest value ever against the US dollar, and as quiet talks to defuse the standoff appear to have stalled.

Why this mattersIran sits next to the Strait of Hormuz, a narrow waterway that a huge share of the world's oil passes through, so a new US-Iran conflict tends to push oil and gas prices up worldwide, including at the pump for anyone who drives. It is also a reminder that decisions about war and peace can get tangled up with domestic politics like elections, which is worth noticing whether or not you think that's how it should work.
Read the whole story

An aircraft carrier strike group is not one ship but an entire floating military base: a carrier loaded with dozens of fighter jets, plus destroyers and submarines built to protect it and strike targets on land. The US normally keeps around three of these groups deployed worldwide at any given time, so moving a third one into the Middle East, alongside somewhere between 2,000 and 9,000 additional Marines depending on which report you read, is not a small gesture. It is the kind of force that could support a sustained bombing campaign, not just a show of strength.

This follows roughly eight months of on-and-off confrontation between the US and Iran, including previous rounds of US strikes on Iranian-linked targets and retaliatory attacks by Iran and groups it backs across the region. Neither side has declared this over, but neither has it turned into the kind of full war that reshapes borders. It has instead settled into a pattern: strikes, pauses, threats, talks that go nowhere, repeat. The reported plan now, according to officials who insisted on anonymity, is to wait until after the November 3 midterm elections in the US before deciding whether to resume bombing. Midterms are the elections held partway through a president's term that decide who controls Congress, and a major new war is the kind of event that can swing voters, which is one reason a president might want to avoid starting one in the final stretch of a campaign.

Inside Iran, the pressure is visible in a very ordinary way: money. Iran's currency, the rial, has fallen to its lowest value ever against the US dollar, meaning it now takes far more rials to buy the same dollar than it used to. For ordinary Iranians this shows up as imported goods, medicine, and food becoming more expensive almost overnight, because a weaker currency makes everything bought from abroad cost more. Economists read a currency crash like this as a sign of serious strain, usually caused by a mix of international sanctions restricting trade, lost oil revenue, and a loss of confidence that the government can turn things around.

Diplomacy has not stopped entirely. Qatar, a small, wealthy Gulf nation that has positioned itself as a go-between in regional conflicts it is not directly part of, has reportedly been involved in mediation, meaning it has been passing messages and proposals between the US and Iran since the two countries do not negotiate with each other directly. No breakthrough has been announced, and it is unclear from the outside whether these talks are close to anything or simply keeping a channel open in case either side wants an off-ramp.

Everything about the timing and the certainty of renewed strikes rests on officials who were not willing to put their names to it, relayed by the Wall Street Journal and picked up by other outlets. That matters for how much weight to put on the story: it may reflect a real plan taking shape inside the administration, or it may reflect officials testing the idea in public before anyone commits to it, a common way for governments to gauge reaction without making a formal announcement. Either way, the ships and the Marines are real and already moving, which is harder to walk back than a quote.

  • mediation — When a third party passes messages or proposals between two sides that are not talking to each other directly, trying to help them reach an agreement.
  • currency devaluation — A sharp drop in how much a country's money is worth compared to other currencies, which makes imported goods more expensive for people living there.

The Ocean Took a Break: No Hurricanes All September

September is usually the busiest month for hurricanes in the Atlantic Ocean. This year, for the first time in more than 30 years, not a single hurricane formed. Scientists say a strange weather pattern is to blame, and they're watching to see if this could be the first hurricane-free year ever recorded.

Why this mattersHurricanes can knock down trees, flood streets, and force families to leave their homes, so a quiet year is good news for people living near the coast in places like Florida or the Caribbean islands. But scientists warn this is a one-time pause, not a permanent change, because El Niño won't last forever and busier storm years are expected to return.
Read the whole story

Every year, big spinning storms called hurricanes form over the warm ocean near the Americas. September is normally their favorite month, like the middle of winter is the coldest time of year.

This year was different. Scientists say strong winds high in the sky kept chopping up storms before they could grow. Imagine trying to build a tall tower out of blocks while someone keeps blowing on it. The wind did that to the storms.

This wind pattern comes from something called El Niño, which changes ocean temperatures every few years and can push wind patterns around the whole world.

Now scientists wonder if the whole year could pass without one single hurricane. That has never happened since people started carefully counting hurricanes, about 70 years ago.

  • Hurricane — A huge, powerful spinning storm that forms over warm ocean water and can bring dangerous wind and flooding.
  • Wind shear — When winds blow in different directions at different heights in the sky, pulling storms apart before they can grow strong.

The Atlantic Just Went a Whole September Without a Single Hurricane

For the first time in more than 30 years, the Atlantic Ocean made it through September — normally the busiest month for hurricanes — without a single one forming. Scientists say an unusually strong weather pattern called El Niño is responsible, and it's now raising a strange question: could all of 2026 pass without a single Atlantic hurricane, something that has never happened since reliable records began in the 1950s?

Why this mattersIf you live somewhere hurricanes can reach — or know people who do — this year's unusual calm is a reminder that weather risk isn't constant from year to year, even though long-term danger doesn't disappear with one quiet season. It's also a case study in how scientists separate a weird, one-off event from a real trend, which is a skill worth having for any news about climate or weather.
Read the whole story

Every year, from June through November, the Atlantic Ocean goes through what meteorologists call hurricane season — a stretch when warm ocean water and favorable winds make it possible for tropical storms to grow into hurricanes. September is usually the peak: the ocean has had all summer to warm up, and conditions are typically ideal for storms to form and strengthen. This year, something unusual happened instead. Nothing formed at all.

The reason, scientists say, traces back to a climate pattern called El Niño — a periodic warming of ocean water in the Pacific that happens every few years and reshapes weather patterns across the entire planet, not just near the Pacific itself. This year's El Niño has been unusually strong, and one of its effects has been to crank up something called wind shear over the Atlantic: a difference in wind speed or direction at different heights in the sky. Picture trying to build a tower of blocks while someone keeps pushing the top half one way and the bottom half another — that's roughly what wind shear does to a budding storm. It rips the structure apart before it can organize into the tight, spinning system a hurricane needs to be.

The streak is notable enough that it's genuinely possible the entire 2026 season could end without a single hurricane forming anywhere in the Atlantic — not a near miss, not a weak one, but zero. That would be unprecedented since record-keeping began in the 1950s, when satellites and consistent tracking first made it possible to say with confidence how many hurricanes formed each year. It's worth being precise about what that would and wouldn't mean: tropical storms, which are weaker systems that haven't yet reached hurricane strength, can still form and cause flooding or wind damage. A hurricane-free season isn't the same as a storm-free one.

Scientists are quick to add a caution here, and it matters as much as the headline number: this is being driven by a specific, temporary pattern, not a permanent drop in hurricane risk. El Niño conditions come and go, typically fading within a year or two, and when they do, the wind shear that's been protecting the Atlantic this year is expected to ease off as well. Most forecasters expect more active, normal — or even unusually intense — seasons to return once that happens. Nobody studying this data thinks coastal communities can relax their preparations going forward.

Even so, people whose job is to plan around hurricane risk are watching closely. Insurance companies price policies for homes along the US Gulf Coast and the Caribbean based partly on how active they expect a season to be, and a quiet year changes the math, at least temporarily, on how much money gets paid out in claims. Coastal governments that spend money on storm shelters, evacuation planning, and emergency supplies are also paying attention, since a rare quiet year is a chance to study exactly what suppressed storm formation — knowledge that could sharpen forecasts for the far more typical, busier seasons still to come.

Words worth knowing
  • El Niño — A periodic warming of ocean water in the Pacific that happens every few years and shifts weather patterns across the whole planet, including wind conditions over the Atlantic.
  • Wind shear — A difference in wind speed or direction at different heights in the sky, which can tear apart a forming storm before it grows into a hurricane.
  • Hurricane season — The months — June through November in the Atlantic — when ocean and wind conditions make hurricanes most likely to form, with September usually the busiest point.
