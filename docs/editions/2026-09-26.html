Two Powerful Leaders Talked for 43 Hours — and Agreed to Keep Talking

The leader of China, Xi Jinping, visited the White House to meet with U.S. President Trump. They talked, ate, and exchanged gifts for about 43 hours — that's almost two full days. At the end, they agreed to pause their biggest disagreement for a while longer, but they didn't actually solve it.

Why this mattersWhen two of the world's biggest countries argue about trade, it can change how much things cost — including toys, sneakers, and electronics you might get as gifts. A pause in the argument means prices probably won't jump suddenly, but it also means the disagreement is still sitting there, waiting to be solved another day.

China and the United States have been arguing about trade for years. Trade means buying and selling things between countries — like the phone in your house, which might have parts made in China. The two countries have been putting extra charges, called tariffs, on each other's goods.

Xi Jinping flew to Washington, D.C. to meet President Trump in person — his first visit like this in more than ten years. They spent almost two full days together, talking, eating meals, and even trading gifts and kind words.

They agreed to keep their current pause on new tariffs going until early 2027, instead of letting prices jump right away. But they did not solve the bigger arguments — about Taiwan, an island China claims as its own, or about special materials called rare earths that are used in phones, cars, and batteries.

News reports don't agree on who did better. American news says both sides just bought more time without fixing anything. A newspaper based in Hong Kong, which tends to reflect what China's government wants people to think, says Xi Jinping came out ahead, especially on the Taiwan question.

Xi and Trump Meet for 43 Hours — and Mostly Agree to Keep Talking

Chinese leader Xi Jinping made his first state visit to the United States in more than ten years, spending roughly 43 hours at the White House with President Trump in late September. The two sides agreed to extend their trade truce — a pause on new tariffs — into early 2027, but they left without a real deal on tariffs, soybeans, or the rare minerals used in phones and missiles. Xi pushed hard on Taiwan and on U.S. limits on selling advanced tech to China; neither side got everything it wanted.

Why this mattersThe prices of phones, cars, and thousands of everyday products depend on whether the U.S. and China are taxing each other's goods or trading freely, so this truce affects family budgets even though no one signed anything dramatic. It also matters because Taiwan produces most of the world's advanced computer chips — the ones inside nearly every phone and laptop — so a serious dispute over the island could disrupt technology supplies everywhere, not just in Asia.

Two of the most powerful men in the world spent nearly two full days together, and the headline result was: nothing blew up. That sounds unimpressive until you remember what the alternative looks like. The U.S. and China have been locked in a trade war for years, each slapping tariffs — taxes on imported goods — on the other's products, which raises prices for ordinary shoppers and squeezes companies on both sides. Instead of letting that fight escalate, the two governments agreed to extend a truce that was set to expire, pushing the deadline to early 2027. That buys both sides time, but it settles nothing.

What didn't happen matters as much as what did. There was no final agreement on tariffs generally, no deal on China buying American soybeans (a trade that matters enormously to U.S. farmers), and no agreement on critical minerals — the rare earth elements China mostly controls and the world needs for everything from electric car batteries to fighter jets. Those were the three things analysts expected might get resolved. They didn't.

Xi used the visit to press Trump on two of Beijing's biggest grievances: Taiwan, the self-governing island China claims as its own, and America's export controls, which restrict the sale of advanced computer chips and other technology to Chinese companies. Beijing sees those controls as an attempt to keep China permanently behind in areas like AI and advanced manufacturing. Whether Xi actually got meaningful concessions on either point is exactly where the two sides' stories diverge.

American outlets covering the summit — Bloomberg, CNBC, CNN — describe the outcome as both leaders essentially buying time rather than resolving anything real, with warm gestures (gift exchanges, friendly remarks, talk of cooperating to manage the risks of artificial intelligence) papering over the fact that the hard disputes are still unresolved. Hong Kong's South China Morning Post, an outlet based in China whose reporting tends to reflect how Beijing wants events understood, told a different story: that Xi made real gains on Taiwan and on what it calls 'strategic stability.' Those two accounts can't both be fully right, and neither should be taken as the whole picture on its own.

That gap between the two versions is itself a piece of information. When a summit produces a friendly photo but no signed agreement, both governments have an incentive to describe it as a win for their own audience back home — Trump can claim he held the line, Xi can claim he made Washington bend. The truth, most likely, sits closer to the boring middle: two rivals who need each other's markets too much to fight outright, and disagree too deeply to actually make peace, kicking the argument another year down the road.

The President's Plane Left One News Channel Behind

President Trump flew to Knoxville, Tennessee, on his special plane, Air Force One. One news channel, CNN, was told it could not come along this time. A different channel, one that usually supports the president, took its seat instead.

Why this mattersImagine if you got to pick which of your friends were allowed to tell your class what happened at recess. You might pick the friend who always makes you look good. That is part of what people are arguing about here — who gets to tell the story of what a president does, and whether leaving out someone who might ask hard questions is fair.
Read the whole story

Reporters usually travel with the president in a small group called a press pool. They watch what he does and tell everyone else about it.

This time, the White House said CNN could not be in that group for the Knoxville trip. A channel called Real America's Voice News, which often speaks kindly about the president, took CNN's spot.

Just days before, the White House had also tried to stop CNN and two other news groups from even walking onto White House property. A judge, whose job is to decide what the rules allow, said that ban had to stop.

So instead of banning CNN from the whole building, the White House left it off just this one plane trip. Many people are asking if that is really any different.

White House Drops CNN From Air Force One Flight, Days After a Judge Ordered Its Access Restored

On Saturday, the White House removed CNN from the small group of reporters who travel with President Trump aboard Air Force One, replacing it with Real America's Voice News, a conservative outlet. The switch came just days after the administration had banned CNN, along with MS NOW and Politico, from the White House entirely — a decision a federal judge had briefly blocked, ordering their access restored. By targeting this one flight instead of general White House access, the administration appears to have found a way around that court order.

Why this mattersHow reporters get access to the people in power shapes what the public actually learns about decisions made in its name — not just for this president, but as a precedent for how future administrations treat outlets they dislike. If access to cover the government can be handed out or withdrawn based on how favorably an outlet reports, that changes what kind of scrutiny any president, of any party, has to face.
Read the whole story

When a president travels, there isn't room on Air Force One for every reporter who wants to be there. So news organizations rotate through something called a press pool — a small group that goes along, takes notes and photos, and then shares everything with every other outlet, print and TV alike. It's been treated for decades as a neutral system: your outlet's turn comes up, you go, you report, everyone else gets the material too. For Saturday's trip to Knoxville, Tennessee, the White House pulled CNN out of that rotation and put in Real America's Voice News, a network that openly supports the president, instead.

This didn't happen in isolation. Just days earlier, the administration had banned CNN, MS NOW and Politico from White House grounds altogether — meaning their reporters couldn't even walk in to cover briefings or events there. A federal judge stepped in and temporarily reversed that ban, ruling that the outlets' credentials (the official passes that let journalists routinely access the White House) had to be restored while the underlying lawsuit plays out.

The Air Force One switch looks like a way to keep the pressure on CNN specifically, without technically defying that judge. The court order was about access to White House grounds. Being included in the traveling press pool for a specific presidential trip is a separate arrangement, decided trip by trip. So CNN can walk back into the White House building under the judge's order, but the administration can still leave it off individual flights — which is exactly what happened here.

This isn't a story resting on one outlet's word. CNN reported it, but so did The Washington Post, NPR, PBS, NBC, Bloomberg, Al Jazeera and The Hill — a wide enough spread of newsrooms, including ones with no stake in defending CNN, that the basic facts aren't seriously in dispute. What's disputed is what it means: whether this is ordinary presidential discretion over who gets a seat on his plane, or a workaround designed to punish outlets a court just told the White House it couldn't punish.

The lawsuit over general White House access is still ongoing, so this isn't the end of the story — a judge may yet rule on whether pool assignments can be used this way too. For now, it's a live example of how a legal fight over press access can shift shape rather than simply end: block one door, and if a court forces it back open, look for another door nearby.

Words worth knowing
  • Press pool — A small, rotating group of reporters who travel with the president when there isn't room for every outlet, and who share their notes and footage with everyone else afterward.
  • White House press credentials — Official passes that give journalists routine, ongoing access to cover the president and White House events, as opposed to being invited to one specific trip.
  • Federal injunction — A judge's order that temporarily pauses a government action while a lawsuit over it is still being decided.

Microsoft Builds an AI Helper That Can Work All by Itself

Microsoft just rebuilt its AI helper, called Copilot, so it can work on jobs for hours without you checking in every minute. Instead of just answering one question and stopping, it can now write computer code and keep working on tasks like a tiny robot assistant. People who own pieces of Microsoft got excited about the news, and the company's stock price jumped up.

Why this mattersAI helpers like Copilot are showing up in more grown-ups' jobs every year, including maybe your parents' workplaces. As these helpers start doing bigger jobs on their own, without a person checking every single step, it changes what work looks like and who is watching to make sure things go right.
Read the whole story

Microsoft has an AI helper called Copilot. Until now, it worked like a friend you ask one question at a time. You ask, it answers, and that's it.

Now Microsoft has rebuilt Copilot to do much more. It can chat with you, write computer code, and send out little AI helpers called 'agents' that keep working on a task for a long time. Imagine a robot that keeps cleaning your room for hours while you're at school, only stopping to ask you a question if it gets stuck.

Microsoft is doing this because another company, called Anthropic, makes a similar AI tool. Lots of businesses have started using Anthropic's tool instead of Microsoft's, so Microsoft wants to win them back with something bigger and better.

After Microsoft showed off the new Copilot, its stock price jumped by about 4 percent. A stock price is like a price tag on a tiny piece of a company. When it goes up, it means people believe the company is about to do well.

Microsoft Rebuilds Copilot Into an AI 'Super App' That Works While You're Not Watching

Microsoft has relaunched its AI assistant Copilot with a major overhaul: instead of just answering questions when you type them in, it can now write software code and run as a 'persistent agent' that keeps working on a task over hours or days without you sitting there prompting it. Investors reacted immediately — Microsoft's stock jumped by roughly 3.6 to 4 percent after the announcement. The redesign is aimed squarely at Anthropic, a rival AI company that has been winning over business customers Microsoft wants to keep.

Why this mattersIf AI agents that work independently for days become normal in workplaces, it changes what kinds of tasks humans are needed for and how closely those tasks get checked. It's also a preview of a tool many of today's students may end up managing, or being managed by, at their first jobs.
Read the whole story

Until now, most people who have used an AI chatbot like Copilot, ChatGPT, or Claude have used it the same way: you type a question, it types back an answer, and the conversation ends when you close the window. Microsoft's new version of Copilot tries to break that pattern. It bundles chat, coding tools, and what the company calls 'agents' into one product — a so-called super app, meaning one piece of software that does many different jobs instead of several separate ones.

The agent part is the real shift. An AI agent doesn't just answer a single question; it can be given a goal — say, 'reconcile this spreadsheet every week' or 'monitor these contracts for renewal dates' — and then keep working on it over time, checking back in, adjusting, and reporting progress, closer to how a junior employee might operate than how a search engine does. That is a meaningfully different kind of trust to place in software: instead of reviewing every single output, a company is letting the AI run semi-independently in the background.

This matters commercially because the fight for AI customers has moved from consumers to businesses. Companies pay far more for AI tools than individuals typically do, and once a business builds its workflows — its daily processes, from expense approvals to customer support — around one company's AI, switching to a rival is expensive and disruptive. Anthropic, maker of the Claude chatbot, has been making real gains here, particularly by positioning its tools as strong at coding and reliable enough for serious enterprise (business) use. Microsoft's redesign is a direct answer to that threat.

Microsoft is not a startup gambling on a new idea; it is one of the most valuable companies in the world, and it already owns the software many businesses run on, including Windows, Office, and Teams. Wiring an agent-based Copilot into all of that gives Microsoft a distribution advantage no competitor can easily match — it doesn't need to convince companies to install something new, only to start using a feature that's already sitting inside software they already pay for.

The stock market's reaction — a jump of roughly 3.6 to 4 percent in Microsoft's share price, which for a company of its size translates into tens of billions of dollars in added market value within a single day — shows that investors read this as a credible strategic move, not just a marketing refresh. But a product announcement is not the same as proof the product works well at scale; how reliable these autonomous agents actually are when let loose on real company data, for weeks at a time, is something that will only become clear as businesses actually adopt it.

Words worth knowing
  • AI agent — An AI system that is given an ongoing goal and can keep working toward it over time, rather than just answering a single question and stopping.
  • Enterprise software — Software built and sold specifically for businesses to use in their daily operations, as opposed to apps made for individual consumers.
  • Market value (market capitalization) — The total worth of all of a company's shares added together, which rises or falls as investors buy or sell stock based on how they judge the company's future.
