When a country's leader says they'll punish anyone doing business with an enemy nation, it's usually a sign that other options have run out. That's roughly what happened here. The U.S. had already tried bombing Iranian targets and blockading Iran's coastline with warships — cutting off ships trying to enter or leave — to pressure Iran to stop fighting. Neither worked. So Trump turned to a different weapon: money. He called for a campaign of 'economic warfare,' meaning the U.S. would try to choke Iran's economy by punishing any country that keeps buying its oil.

Oil is the kind of thing that quietly touches almost everything. It's not just what goes in cars — it's used to make plastic, to ship goods across oceans, and to run factories. So when traders around the world got nervous that a bigger fight over Iranian oil was coming, the price of crude oil jumped to almost $94 a barrel, its highest point since late July. Higher oil prices tend to ripple outward into higher prices for gas, shipping, and everyday goods, everywhere, not just in the countries directly involved.

Here's where the story gets more complicated. Bloomberg's reporting points out that the country buying the overwhelming majority of Iran's oil isn't some small, easily-pressured nation — it's China. That means Trump's threat, even though it's framed as being about Iran, is really landing on the world's second-largest economy. Some analysts see this less as a move to punish Iran and more as a test of how far the U.S. is willing to go in a economic standoff with China, using Iran as the pressure point.

Iran's foreign minister wasn't impressed. He dismissed the threat as a distraction, pointing to a New York Times report that U.S. national debt — the total amount the American government has borrowed and still owes — has now passed $40 trillion, an almost unimaginable number. His argument was essentially: how credible is a threat of economic punishment from a country that owes that much money itself? Iran also separately warned that if the war resumes, it could use more 'destructive' weapons than it has so far, raising the stakes further.

Meanwhile, the United Arab Emirates, a close U.S. ally in the region, suspended its own trade dealings with Iran — a sign that the pressure campaign is already reshaping decisions well beyond Washington and Tehran. What isn't settled, and may not be for a while, is how much of this is genuinely about stopping Iran's war, and how much is about using Iran as leverage against China. Different observers read the same set of facts in very different ways.

Donald Trump posted the threat on social media in blunt, capitalized terms — "TREMENDOUS economic consequences" — aimed at any nation that keeps buying Iranian oil or otherwise trades with Tehran. It came after weeks in which U.S. airstrikes on Iranian targets and a naval blockade meant to choke off Iran's oil shipments had both failed to bring the fighting to an end. The post read less like a diplomatic statement than an ultimatum, and markets treated it that way.

Global crude oil jumped to nearly $94 a barrel within hours, its highest price since late July. Oil traders move fast on threats like this because a blockade or new sanctions regime can genuinely cut supply, and even the possibility of that is enough to make buyers pay more today rather than risk paying far more later. For anyone who drives a car, heats a home with oil, or buys anything shipped by truck or ship, a spike like this eventually shows up in ordinary bills.

Bloomberg's reporting on the threat pointed to an inconvenient detail: the countries that would actually feel "tremendous economic consequences" are not really Iran's smaller trading partners — they are dominated by one buyer. China purchases the overwhelming majority of Iran's oil exports, often through a network of independent refineries that get around existing sanctions. That makes any serious attempt to stop Iran's oil trade, in practice, an attempt to stop China's oil trade — turning a regional conflict into a test of how far Washington is willing to push Beijing.

Iran's foreign minister was dismissive, framing the threat as a distraction from America's own financial troubles rather than a serious danger to Iran. He pointed to a New York Times report that U.S. national debt — the total amount the U.S. government owes to its lenders — has now passed $40 trillion, arguing that a country in that much debt is in no position to lecture others about economic pain. It was as much a rhetorical jab as a policy argument, but it was clearly meant to be read in Washington as well as Tehran.

Iran did not stop at words of dismissal. It separately warned that it could deploy weapons it described as more "destructive" if the fighting resumes, a warning meant to signal that further military pressure carries its own risks. Meanwhile the United Arab Emirates, a close U.S. partner in the Gulf and a major regional trading hub, announced it was suspending its own trade dealings with Iran — a sign that regional governments are hedging against a wider economic and military confrontation rather than waiting to see how the standoff between Washington and Beijing plays out.

What remains genuinely unsettled is what this pressure campaign is actually for. One reading is that it is a conventional, if aggressive, attempt to squeeze Iran's economy until its government changes course on the fighting. The other reading, favored by much of the financial press, is that Iran has become the pretext for a much larger contest: whether the United States can force China, its biggest strategic rival, to give up a major and cheap source of oil. Both readings can be true at once, which is part of why the threat has rattled markets rather than reassured anyone.

特朗普在社交媒体上用直白、全部大写的语气发出这条威胁——“巨大的（TREMENDOUS）经济后果”——针对任何继续购买伊朗石油或与德黑兰进行贸易的国家。此前数周，美军对伊朗目标发动的空袭，以及旨在切断伊朗石油运输的海军封锁，都未能让战事平息。这条帖子读起来不像外交声明，更像最后通牒，市场也是这样反应的。

短短几小时内，全球原油价格跳涨至近每桶94美元，创下7月底以来的新高。石油交易员对这类威胁反应迅速，因为封锁或新一轮制裁确实可能真的切断供应，哪怕只是这种可能性，也足以让买家宁愿现在多付钱，也不愿冒着日后付出更高代价的风险。对于开车、用油取暖，或购买任何通过卡车或轮船运输商品的人来说，这样的价格飙升迟早会体现在日常账单里。

彭博社关于这一威胁的报道指出了一个尴尬的细节：真正会感受到“巨大经济后果”的，并不是伊朗那些规模较小的贸易伙伴——这个市场其实由一个买家主导。中国购买了伊朗绝大部分的石油出口，往往通过一批独立炼油厂来绕开现有制裁。这意味着，任何认真想阻止伊朗石油贸易的举动，实际上都等于想阻止中国的石油贸易——这就把一场地区冲突，变成了华盛顿愿意在多大程度上向北京施压的一次考验。

伊朗外长对此不以为然，将这一威胁定性为对美国自身财政困境的转移视线，而非对伊朗的真正威胁。他引用《纽约时报》的一篇报道称，美国国家债务——即美国政府欠债权人的总额——如今已突破40万亿美元，并借此反驳说，一个负债如此之重的国家没有资格教训别人经济上的痛苦。这与其说是政策论点，不如说是一记言辞上的反击，但显然既是说给德黑兰听的，也是说给华盛顿听的。

伊朗并未止步于言辞上的轻蔑。它还另外警告称，如果战事重启，可能会动用其所称“更具毁灭性”的武器，意在表明进一步的军事施压本身也带有风险。与此同时，美国在海湾地区的重要伙伴、区域贸易枢纽阿联酋宣布暂停与伊朗的贸易往来——这表明地区各国正在为一场更大范围的经济和军事对抗未雨绸缪，而不是坐等华盛顿与北京之间的对峙如何收场。

真正悬而未决的是，这场施压行动到底意欲何为。一种解读是，这不过是一次常规但强硬的手段，意在挤压伊朗经济，直到其政府在战事上改变立场。另一种解读——为许多财经媒体所倾向——是伊朗已经沦为一个更大博弈的借口：美国能否迫使其最大的战略对手中国，放弃一项重要且廉价的石油来源。这两种解读完全可以同时成立，这也正是为何这一威胁搅动了市场，却没能让任何一方感到安心。

For three decades, tens of thousands of people gathered every June 4th in Hong Kong's Victoria Park, holding candles to remember the hundreds, possibly thousands, of people killed when Chinese troops crushed pro-democracy protests in Beijing in 1989. It was a strange anomaly: nowhere else in mainland-controlled China could such a gathering happen without being shut down immediately. Hong Kong's semi-autonomous status, guaranteed under the "one country, two systems" arrangement negotiated when Britain handed the territory back to China in 1997, made the vigil possible. That arrangement has been steadily narrowed since Beijing imposed a national security law on the city in 2020, following mass pro-democracy protests the year before.

Chow Hang-tung, a human-rights lawyer who defended herself in court, and Lee Cheuk-yan, a former opposition lawmaker, led the Hong Kong Alliance in Support of Patriotic Democratic Movements of China, the group behind the vigil. Both were already in detention when this trial concluded; Chow has spent years in and out of custody on related charges, and much of the group's leadership has already been prosecuted or has fled the city. The vigil itself has not been held publicly since 2020, when authorities cited pandemic rules to ban it, then continued blocking it in subsequent years on national-security grounds even after those rules lapsed.

The charge against them, "inciting subversion," comes from the 2020 national security law, which criminalizes acts deemed to undermine the authority of the Chinese state — a category broad enough, critics say, to cover organizing a vigil, and specific enough, prosecutors argue, to distinguish protected memory from political agitation aimed at the government. The prosecution's theory was that the Alliance's continued public calls to remember Tiananmen, combined with its stated goal of ending one-party rule in China, amounted to subversive intent rather than simple commemoration. The defense argued that mourning the dead is not the same as inciting rebellion, and that no law should be able to turn grief into a crime.

The timing sharpens the stakes. Just days before the verdict, Xi Jinping made an unusually direct public remark defending the 1989 crackdown as the correct decision — breaking with decades of official silence in which the Chinese Communist Party neither fully justified nor apologized for the killings, preferring simply not to discuss them. Human-rights groups read the comment, paired with this conviction, as confirmation that Beijing has closed the door on any future reassessment of 1989, a possibility that some Chinese reformers had quietly held onto for years.
