A Chatbot Company Wants to Sell Pieces of Itself for Maybe $2 Trillion

Anthropic, the company that makes the AI chatbot Claude, told U.S. regulators this week that it wants to let ordinary people buy tiny pieces of the company, called shares. At the same time, it revealed it lost an almost unimaginable amount of money in just six months. Now everyone is watching to see if people will still want to buy in.

Why this mattersGrown-ups put their savings, and sometimes money meant for retirement, into companies like this one through the stock market. If a company like Anthropic succeeds, people who bought shares could make a lot of money, but if it struggles, they could lose a lot too. This story is really about a big bet on whether brand new technology is worth trusting with real money, even before it proves it can make a profit.
Read the whole story

Anthropic makes Claude, a computer program that can chat, write, and answer questions almost like a very smart robot friend. This week, the company filed papers with the SEC, a U.S. government group that watches over the stock market, saying it wants to become a 'public company.' That means soon, anyone with money could buy a small piece of Anthropic, called a share, once it starts trading on a stock exchange called the Nasdaq.

The papers also showed something surprising. Anthropic lost $80.7 billion in just six months. To picture that, imagine spending $1,000 every single day. It would take you more than 220,000 years to spend that much money.

Even though Anthropic is losing huge amounts, some investors think it could be worth $1.5 trillion to $2 trillion once it finally sells its shares. That is more money than the entire yearly earnings of many countries combined. People betting on Anthropic think its AI technology will become so useful and valuable that it is worth the risk, even though right now it spends far more than it earns.

Nobody knows yet exactly what price the shares will sell for, or exactly when. This sale is being watched closely because it will show whether people still believe in AI companies that burn through enormous piles of cash while chasing a huge future payoff.

  • Shares — Shares are tiny pieces of a company that people can buy, which let them own a small part of it and share in its profits or losses.

Anthropic Just Filed to Go Public — and Revealed It's Losing Money Faster Than Almost Any Company in History

Anthropic, the company behind the AI chatbot Claude, has taken the first formal step toward selling shares to the public. Buried in the paperwork was a staggering number: the company lost $80.7 billion in just the first six months of 2026, even as its revenue kept growing. Despite that loss, some estimates put its value at up to $2 trillion once it starts trading on the Nasdaq stock exchange under the ticker 'ANT.'

Why this mattersIf you have a retirement account, a pension, or even a parent with investments in index funds, there's a decent chance you'll end up owning a tiny sliver of Anthropic once it's public, whether you choose to or not. How this bet plays out will shape how much money flows into AI development over the next decade — which affects how fast the technology you already use keeps changing.
Read the whole story

On Monday, Anthropic filed what's called a Form S-1 with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), the government agency that oversees stock markets. An S-1 is essentially a company's formal application to sell its shares to the public for the first time — a process known as an IPO, or initial public offering. Before this, Anthropic was funded privately, by venture capital firms and investors like Amazon and Google. Going public means ordinary people, pension funds, and institutions will be able to buy pieces of the company on the open market.

The numbers in the filing are hard to picture at normal scale. An $80.7 billion loss in six months works out to roughly $440 million lost every single day. For comparison, that's close to what some mid-sized companies earn in total revenue in a year — Anthropic burned through an equivalent amount before lunch, most days. The spending goes mainly toward computing power: training and running AI models requires enormous numbers of specialized chips, which are expensive to buy and expensive to run.

Yet investors aren't necessarily scared off, because revenue is also growing fast — the filing shows it increasing sharply alongside the losses. The bet underlying a potential $1.5 trillion to $2 trillion valuation (the estimated total worth of the company, which won't be finalized until shares actually start trading) is that Anthropic is spending aggressively now to capture a dominant position in artificial intelligence later, the same way Amazon spent years losing money while it built out its retail and shipping empire.

That comparison is exactly what's being argued about. Skeptics point out that Amazon eventually became profitable because it sold physical goods people needed regardless of hype cycles, while AI's long-term profitability is still unproven — nobody fully knows yet whether companies and consumers will keep paying enough to cover these costs once the novelty wears off. Supporters counter that AI is already embedded in software millions of people use daily, and that whoever builds the most capable models first could lock in a durable advantage, the way Google did with search.

This IPO will be closely watched as a signal for the entire AI industry. Several other AI companies have also been spending far more than they earn, funded by private investors willing to bet on the future. If Anthropic's public listing goes well — meaning its stock holds value or rises — it tells other AI companies, and their investors, that public markets will tolerate this kind of spending. If it goes badly, it could cool enthusiasm and funding across the sector.

Words worth knowing

  • S-1 filing — A detailed legal document a company must submit to U.S. regulators before it can go public, disclosing its finances and risks to potential investors.
  • Valuation — An estimate of what a whole company is worth, usually calculated by multiplying its share price by the total number of shares.

Why this mattersThis story is about whether powerful people get treated the same way as everyone else when it comes to breaking rules. If you were ever accused of something, you'd want the process to feel fair and not rushed. This case asks a hard question: is the government punishing someone for actually breaking the law, or because that person made powerful people angry?

James Comey used to be the boss of the FBI. The FBI is the group of detectives who investigate big crimes across the whole country, like spying and terrorism. Comey led it until 2017, when President Trump fired him.

Now the Department of Justice, the part of the government that brings criminal charges, says Comey broke a law back in 2016. They say he shared secret information about national defense with someone who wasn't allowed to have it, and that he worked with other people to do it.

The deadline to charge someone for this kind of old crime was about to run out. So instead of asking a grand jury, a group of regular citizens who decide if there's enough evidence for a trial, prosecutors filed the charges themselves and skipped that step.

James Comey Charged Again — Third Time Since Trump Fired Him

The Justice Department has filed new federal charges against James Comey, the former head of the FBI, accusing him of illegally sharing national security information and conspiring with others to do so. It's the third time prosecutors have tried to bring a case against him since President Trump fired him from the FBI in 2017. The charges landed just hours after a federal judge ruled that the same Justice Department had illegally fired Comey's daughter from her job as a federal prosecutor.

Why this mattersThis isn't really about whether you like or dislike James Comey — it's about whether the government's power to charge someone with a crime is being used evenly, or aimed at people who criticize those in charge. That question matters for everyone, because the same tools used here could, in principle, be pointed at anyone, and how this case plays out will shape what future administrations feel free to do.
Read the whole story

James Comey used to run the FBI, the agency that investigates major crimes and threats to national security in the United States, until Trump fired him in 2017 — a firing that became one of the most argued-over moments of Trump's first term. Comey has remained a public critic of Trump ever since. Now the Justice Department, the government department that includes federal prosecutors, has charged him again: this time with knowingly passing along information related to national defense to someone not allowed to have it, and with conspiring with others to break the laws that protect that kind of information.

What's unusual is how the charges were filed. Normally, serious federal charges like this go through a grand jury — a group of ordinary citizens who review the evidence prosecutors have gathered and decide whether there's enough to justify a formal charge, called an indictment. This time, prosecutors skipped that step and used something called a 'criminal information' instead, a method that lets them file charges directly. Reports say the reason was timing: the alleged conduct dates back to 2016, and prosecutors were running up against the statute of limitations — the legal deadline after which someone can no longer be charged for an old alleged crime.

The timing of the announcement raised eyebrows for another reason too. Just hours earlier, a federal judge in New York had ruled that the Justice Department broke the law when it fired Comey's daughter, Maurene Comey, last year from her job as an assistant U.S. attorney — a federal prosecutor — in Manhattan. Her firing and the ruling against it had nothing to do with the new charges against her father on paper, but the sequence of events, one case against a father and a legal defeat over his daughter's firing arriving within hours of each other, is exactly the kind of detail that fuels arguments on both sides.

Comey's legal team is expected to fight the case hard, and nothing about its outcome is settled — it still has to survive legal challenges before any trial could happen. Critics, including many who are not close allies of Comey, point out that this is now the third attempt to charge him since his firing, and argue that repeatedly pursuing one outspoken critic looks less like ordinary law enforcement and more like a pattern. The administration and its supporters respond that the charges stand on their own evidence and that a person's politics, or who they've criticized, shouldn't shield them from prosecution if they broke the law.

Words worth knowing
  • criminal information — A way of formally charging someone with a crime that lets prosecutors file directly, without first getting a grand jury to review the evidence.
  • grand jury indictment — A formal criminal charge that only happens after a group of ordinary citizens reviews the evidence and agrees there's enough to proceed.
  • statute of limitations — A legal deadline that stops prosecutors from charging someone for an alleged crime once too much time has passed since it happened.

Measles Is Spreading Fast Across America Again

More than 4,580 people have caught measles in the United States this year. That's more than double last year's total. Pennsylvania has been hit hardest, with sick people found in 44 different counties.

Why this mattersMeasles can make kids very sick, causing high fevers and sometimes serious breathing problems. Getting vaccinated doesn't just protect you — it helps protect babies and other kids who can't get the shot yet, by stopping the disease from spreading in the first place.
Read the whole story

Measles is a disease that spreads very easily, like a cold but much more dangerous. It used to be mostly gone from the United States because so many children got a vaccine shot that protects against it.

Lately, fewer families have been getting that shot. When fewer people are protected, the disease can spread fast again, jumping from person to person in schools, stores, and airports.

Doctors and health officials say this is why cases have jumped so high this year. They're worried people traveling on planes could carry measles to other countries too.

There's also an argument happening between a top U.S. health official and Pennsylvania's governor. They disagree about how deaths from this outbreak are being counted and talked about.

Measles Cases in the US Have More Than Doubled This Year — And Officials Are Fighting Over the Numbers

More than 4,580 measles cases have been reported across the United States so far in 2026 — over double the total for all of last year. Pennsylvania has been hit hardest, with at least 1,338 confirmed cases spread across 44 counties. As the outbreak grows, the nation's top health official and Pennsylvania's governor are publicly clashing over how the state's measles deaths have been counted and described.

Why this mattersMeasles was supposed to be a disease of the past in the US, something your grandparents worried about but you wouldn't have to. Whether that stays true depends partly on vaccination choices made by people around you, not just your own — and on whether officials can agree on basic facts long enough to respond to it together.
Read the whole story

Measles was declared eliminated in the United States back in 2000, meaning the disease was no longer spreading continuously within the country — any cases that popped up came from travelers bringing it in from abroad, and they fizzled out quickly because so many Americans were vaccinated. That status is now in real danger. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the federal agency responsible for tracking and fighting disease outbreaks nationwide, reports that 2026's case count has already more than doubled 2025's full-year total, with cases still climbing as of late October.

Pennsylvania has become the center of the crisis, with at least 1,338 confirmed cases spread across 44 of its counties — meaning the outbreak isn't contained to one city or community, but has spread widely across the state. Public health officials point to the same underlying cause driving outbreaks in other states too: falling vaccination rates in specific communities, often where religious objections, misinformation, or general distrust of vaccines have led parents to skip or delay the MMR vaccine, the standard childhood shot that protects against measles, mumps, and rubella.

Measles is one of the most contagious diseases known — a single infected person can spread it to 12 to 18 others in an unvaccinated population, far more than viruses like the flu or even COVID-19. That's why public health relies on herd immunity: if roughly 95% of a community is vaccinated, the virus struggles to find new people to infect, which protects not just the vaccinated but also babies too young for the shot and people with medical conditions that prevent them from getting it. When vaccination rates slip below that threshold in pockets of the country, even by a few percentage points, outbreaks like this one become possible.

The outbreak has also become tangled up in a political fight. Robert F. Kennedy Jr., the Secretary of Health and Human Services (HHS) — the federal department that oversees the CDC and sets national health policy — has a long history of publicly questioning vaccine safety, a stance most medical researchers reject based on decades of evidence. He and Pennsylvania's governor, Josh Shapiro, have clashed openly over how the state's measles deaths have been reported and characterized, with each side accusing the other of distorting the picture. The dispute matters beyond politics: when the public hears two powerful officials disagreeing about basic facts during a health emergency, it becomes harder to know what to trust.

Health experts warn the danger isn't confined to Pennsylvania, or even to the United States. International travel means an outbreak that grows large enough can cross borders easily — a traveler infected in Pennsylvania could unknowingly carry the virus to another country within a day, potentially sparking outbreaks elsewhere. The CDC's most detailed report on the outbreak's scale was published October 25, just as this window of reporting began, but officials stress the situation remains active and unresolved — the case count is expected to keep rising.

Words worth knowing
  • CDC (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention) — The US federal agency responsible for tracking disease outbreaks nationwide and recommending how to respond to them.
  • Herd immunity — Protection that happens when enough people in a community are vaccinated that a contagious disease can't easily spread, shielding even those who aren't vaccinated.
  • MMR vaccine — The standard childhood shot that protects against measles, mumps, and rubella, usually given in two doses before school age.
