Five TV News Channels Are Being Investigated for Teaming Up Against the White House

Five big TV news channels — ABC, CBS, CNN, NBC and Fox News — stopped sharing their usual video of the president for a day, to protest other reporters being kicked out of the White House. Now the government says that teamwork might have broken a law, and it has opened an investigation to find out. No one has been charged with anything yet — this is just the start of looking into it.

Why this mattersReporters are the people whose job is to find out what the government is doing and tell everyone else — like a messenger between leaders and the public. When the government can punish news channels for teaming up to protest, it raises a question about how free those messengers really are to do their jobs.

Last month, the White House banned more than 70 journalists from its briefing room, including reporters from CNN and two other news outlets. A judge quickly stepped in and ordered their access restored for now, while a bigger decision is still coming.

U.S. Justice Department Investigates Five TV Networks Over Joint News Boycott

The U.S. Justice Department has opened a formal antitrust investigation into ABC, CBS, CNN, NBC and Fox News, accusing them of illegally coordinating when they jointly suspended their shared White House coverage in protest. The protest began after the administration barred reporters from CNN, Politico and MS NOW from the press briefing room. It follows an earlier decision to strip press credentials from more than 70 journalists altogether, a move a federal judge has temporarily blocked while a fuller ruling is expected soon.

Why this mattersHow governments treat journalists who report on them shapes what the public ends up knowing — and an administration that can legally punish news outlets for cooperating with each other has gained real leverage over what gets covered and how. Whatever you think of any particular network, the underlying question — can the government use an ordinary business law to discourage coordinated protest by the press — will outlast this specific dispute.

For decades, television networks covering the White House have relied on something called a press pool: a small rotating group of reporters and camera crews who get close access to the president, then share their footage and notes with every other outlet, so the Oval Office isn't crowded with fifty separate camera crews every time something happens. It only works because the networks trust each other and cooperate. Last month, after the administration banned reporters from three specific outlets from the briefing room, the major networks did something unusual: they agreed together to suspend the pool entirely, as a protest.

The Justice Department's response was to open an antitrust investigation — the kind of legal tool normally used to punish companies that secretly agree to fix prices or divide up a market instead of competing with each other, which is illegal because it cheats customers out of the benefits of competition. Applying that same law to news organizations deciding, together, how to cover a political dispute is a significant stretch of what antitrust law has traditionally been used for, and press freedom groups say that's exactly the point: it turns an ordinary business law into a weapon against unified journalism.

This didn't happen in isolation. The month before, the administration had stripped press credentials — the official passes that let reporters into government buildings and briefings — from more than 70 journalists at once. A federal judge issued a temporary stay, a short-term court order pausing an action while a case is still being argued, requiring that access be restored for now. A more permanent ruling on whether the credential removals were lawful is expected by Tuesday evening.

Bruce Brown, who leads the Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press, a nonprofit that provides legal help to journalists, called the antitrust investigation 'another and even more brazen attempt to try to intimidate journalists.' Press freedom advocates broadly agree that using antitrust law this way is aggressive and unusual — there's no obvious precedent for treating a newsroom's editorial protest as the kind of market manipulation the law was built to catch.

One detail matters for how you should read coverage of this story: CNN is itself one of the five networks under investigation, which means its own reporting on the probe comes with an obvious conflict of interest — it's reporting on a case it's a defendant in. That's why this account draws instead on The Washington Post, Al Jazeera and NPR, none of which are targets of the investigation.

No criminal charges have been filed, and the Justice Department has not announced any findings — this is an investigation, which is a formal step toward possibly building a case, not proof that any law was actually broken.

Two Americans Win Top Prize for Explaining Why Prices Go Up and Down

Two professors from the United States, Robert Hall and Michael Woodford, just won one of the world's biggest prizes for figuring out how a country's economy speeds up and slows down. Their ideas help explain why your allowance sometimes buys more snacks than other times. The prize is called the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences, and it comes with money the two scientists will share.

Why this mattersWhen prices rise too fast, the same amount of pocket money buys less candy or fewer toys than before. When too many people lose their jobs, families have less money to spend on fun things, or even on things they need. The ideas these two scientists studied are part of why grown-ups argue about interest rates and prices on the news.

Robert Hall teaches at Stanford University and Michael Woodford teaches at Columbia University. Both are in the United States. This week, they won the Nobel Prize in Economics for studying how people decide what to buy and how businesses decide what to charge.

Their research helped explain something tricky: why an economy sometimes grows too fast, causing prices to rise quickly, and sometimes grows too slowly, causing people to lose their jobs. Think of an economy like a bike you're learning to ride. Go too fast and you might crash. Go too slow and you might tip over. Hall and Woodford studied how to help a country's economy find a steady speed.

Their ideas became part of a set of theories economists call 'New Keynesian' thinking. For decades, these ideas have guided how central banks, like the U.S. Federal Reserve, decide whether to make borrowing money more expensive or cheaper. That decision affects things like how easy it is for your parents to get a loan for a car or a house.

The Economics Prize is a little different from the other Nobel Prizes, like the ones for Peace or Science. Alfred Nobel, the man who created the original prizes, didn't actually include Economics in his will. Sweden's central bank created this prize later, in 1968. Even so, most people today treat it with the same respect as the original Nobel Prizes.

Two American Economists Win Nobel for Work Behind Every Fed Interest Rate Decision

On Monday, the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences awarded this year's Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences to Robert Hall of Stanford University and Michael Woodford of Columbia University. Their decades of research explain why economies swing between booms and busts, and how central banks can try to limit the damage from both. The ideas they developed now sit quietly behind nearly every interest rate decision made by the U.S. Federal Reserve and similar institutions around the world.

Why this mattersThe next time interest rates go up or down, affecting the cost of a car loan, a mortgage, or a parent's credit card, the reasoning behind that decision likely draws on work like this. It's also a reminder that the price of everyday things — and whether people keep their jobs — is shaped as much by economic theory as by politics or luck.
Read the whole story

Every economy moves in cycles: periods of growth and hiring, called booms, followed by slowdowns and layoffs, called busts. This pattern, known as the business cycle, isn't caused by any single villain — it emerges from millions of separate decisions by households and companies about what to buy, what to charge, and whether to hire or fire. Left alone, these cycles can swing hard enough to wreck lives: unemployment spikes during a bust, or prices race ahead of wages during a boom, eating into what people can actually afford.

Hall and Woodford spent their careers studying something deceptively simple: how real households and businesses actually make decisions about prices and spending, rather than how economic textbooks assumed they did. Earlier models often treated prices as adjusting instantly and perfectly to changing conditions, the way water finds its level. Hall and Woodford showed that prices are 'sticky' — a company doesn't change its prices every time costs shift even slightly, because changing prices is itself costly and confusing for customers. That stickiness means the economy doesn't snap back to balance on its own; it needs a nudge.

That insight became a foundation for what's called New Keynesian economics, a school of thought that borrows from older ideas (that markets sometimes need help) while using rigorous, modern mathematical tools to show exactly how and when that help works best. In plain terms: because prices and wages don't adjust instantly, a central bank — the institution that controls a country's money supply and interest rates, like the Federal Reserve in the U.S. — can smooth out booms and busts by carefully raising or lowering the cost of borrowing money.

This isn't an abstract academic debate. When the Federal Reserve decided how fast to raise interest rates after inflation surged in 2021 and 2022, or how aggressively to cut them during the 2008 financial crisis, the models guiding those choices trace directly back to work like Hall's and Woodford's. Raise rates too slowly, and prices spiral upward, eroding savings and wages. Raise them too fast, and businesses stop hiring, throwing people out of work. Getting that balance right, according to this research, depends on central banks being predictable and credible — so that households and firms trust it enough to adjust their own behavior in advance.

The award itself carries its own quiet controversy. Unlike the Nobel Prizes for physics, chemistry, medicine, literature and peace — all specified in Alfred Nobel's 1895 will — the economics prize was created in 1968 by Sweden's central bank, nearly seventy years after Nobel's death, and is formally called the Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel. Critics have long argued this makes it a different kind of honor altogether, borrowing prestige from a name rather than being part of Nobel's original vision. In practice, though, it is announced alongside the other Nobels, administered by the same foundation, and treated by the public and by economists themselves as carrying equal weight.

Words worth knowing
  • Business cycle — The natural pattern of an economy expanding (a boom) and then contracting (a bust), which affects how many people have jobs and how fast prices rise.
  • New Keynesian economics — A school of economic thought showing that because prices and wages adjust slowly rather than instantly, governments and central banks can act to smooth out economic swings.
  • Central bank — A country's official institution for managing its money and interest rates, like the U.S. Federal Reserve, usually designed to operate independently of elected politicians.

No Driver, No Problem: Self-Driving Trucks Get a Special Pass

A U.S. government agency just made a new rule just for trucks that drive themselves. Normally, if a truck breaks down, the driver has to walk out and set up orange warning triangles so other cars see it. But a self-driving truck has no driver to do that job. So officials said two companies, Aurora Innovation and Kodiak AI, can use flashing lights on the truck instead, for the next five years.

Why this mattersTrucks carry almost everything you use, from the cereal in your kitchen to the sneakers on your feet. If trucks start driving themselves, it could change how fast things get to stores, who gets jobs driving trucks, and what you see on the highway during your next car trip.
Read the whole story

Picture a truck as long as four cars parked end to end. If it breaks down on a highway, the driver has always had to hop out and place reflective triangles on the road behind it, so other drivers spot it in time and don't crash.

But some trucks today don't have a driver at all. They steer themselves using cameras, computers, and sensors, like a very careful robot. If one of these trucks breaks down, there's nobody inside to walk out and set up triangles.

A government agency in the United States, called FMCSA, just said two companies can skip that triangle rule. Instead, their trucks can use bright flashing lights built onto the front of the truck. This permission lasts for five years.

People who watch this industry say this was one of the last big rules standing in the way of driverless trucks spreading across American highways.

Driverless Trucks Get a Legal Green Light to Skip a 70-Year-Old Safety Rule

America's truck safety regulator has agreed to let two self-driving truck companies, Aurora Innovation and Kodiak AI, skip a rule that has applied to every truck on US highways for decades: when a truck breaks down, someone has to walk out and place reflective warning triangles on the road behind it. For a truck with no driver, that's impossible — so regulators are letting these companies use flashing beacons mounted on the cab instead. It's a small-sounding fix for a problem that was actually blocking driverless trucking from expanding nationwide.

Why this mattersTrucking moves most of what you buy — groceries, clothes, furniture — across the country, and the industry has struggled for years to find enough drivers for long, often grueling highway routes. Whether driverless trucks become common on the roads you or your family drive on depends on exactly these kinds of unglamorous regulatory decisions, made one rule at a time, long before any flashy robot-truck headline.
Read the whole story

Picture a semi-truck breaking down on a dark highway shoulder. Federal law has long required the driver to walk out onto the road and place three reflective warning triangles — one close to the truck, two further back — so oncoming traffic sees the hazard in time. It's a rule written for trucks that have drivers. A driverless truck doesn't.

That gap in the rulebook has been one of the quiet but real obstacles stopping companies from running fully driverless trucks — no human in the cab at all — on public highways at scale. You can build the sensors and the software, but if you can't legally handle a breakdown without a human stepping outside, you can't operate without someone on board, which defeats much of the point.

The Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration (FMCSA), the US agency that writes and enforces safety rules for trucks and buses, has now granted Aurora Innovation and Kodiak AI a five-year exemption from the triangle requirement. In its place, their trucks can use beacons — warning lights built into or mounted on the truck itself — that switch on automatically when the truck stops. No one needs to get out.

This is a narrow, technical-sounding change, but it removes a specific legal roadblock rather than just a symbolic one. Both companies have been testing driverless trucks on selected highway routes, often in Texas, for a while — but mostly with a safety driver on board or under tightly limited conditions. An exemption like this is part of what a company needs before it can argue, to itself and to regulators, that its trucks are ready to run with genuinely nobody aboard.

It's worth being honest about how solid this particular piece of news is. Only one outlet, TechCrunch, has reported on this specific exemption in detail — no second, independently owned newsroom has yet published its own account of it. The broader pattern, though, is well documented elsewhere: US regulators and lawmakers have been steadily adjusting rules written for human drivers to accommodate trucks that don't have any, and this exemption fits that pattern rather than standing apart from it.

  • Exemption — Official permission to skip a rule that would normally apply, usually granted for a specific reason and a limited time.
  • Driverless trucking — Trucks that drive themselves on public roads using sensors and software, without a human operator on board.

  1. Should regulators grant exemptions like this before driverless trucks have years of real-world safety data, or only after?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Approve exemptions early

    • Companies can't gather meaningful real-world data at scale until outdated rules like the triangle requirement stop forcing a human to be on board anyway.
