The Night Before America's Big Vote: A Court Says No, A Politician Switches Sides, and A President's Popularity Drops

Tomorrow, millions of Americans vote in elections that decide who controls Congress, the group that writes the country's laws. Right before voting day, three big things happened almost at once. A top court refused to block some votes, a politician surprised everyone by backing the other team, and a new poll showed the president's popularity sinking lower than ever.

Why this mattersThe people chosen in these elections decide things that reach into everyday life, like school funding, roads, and healthcare. Fights over who gets to vote, and whether politicians stick with their own team, show how messy and important it is to make sure everyone's voice gets counted fairly.

Every two years, Americans vote for Congress, kind of like picking the team that makes the rules for the whole country. This year's vote, called the midterms, could shift who is in charge.

Right before voting started, the Supreme Court — the highest court in the country — said no to a request from the government. The government wanted California to set aside maybe thousands of ballots, saying some might have been cast by people who aren't allowed to vote. But every court that looked at the claim so far said there wasn't enough proof, so the ballots will be counted.

In Michigan, something unusual happened. A Democratic politician named Haley Stevens said she would support the Republican candidate, Mike Rogers, instead of her own party's candidate, Abdul El-Sayed. The two had a disagreement over an ad she felt was unfair, and his campaign asked her to stop appearing with him.

Meanwhile, a new poll found that 60 out of every 100 Americans disapprove of how President Trump is doing his job — the highest that number has ever been for him. He and Vice President Vance were still traveling and giving speeches right up to the final hours before voting.

On the Eve of the Vote: A Court Says No, a Democrat Breaks Ranks, and Trump's Numbers Hit a Low

Hours before Tuesday's elections that will decide which party controls Congress, three unrelated events converged. The Supreme Court turned down a last-minute request from the Trump administration to let California toss out ballots it suspected came from noncitizens. A Democratic congresswoman in Michigan publicly backed the Republican Senate candidate over her own party's nominee. And a new poll showed President Trump's disapproval rating at its highest point of either of his presidential terms.

Why this mattersWhichever party wins control of Congress on Tuesday will shape what laws can pass for the next two years — on everything from taxes to immigration to how future elections are run. The Supreme Court's refusal to let California set aside ballots on short notice also signals how courts will likely treat similar last-minute requests in future elections: skepticism, unless the evidence is solid.
Read the whole story

Late on the night before the vote, the Supreme Court rejected an emergency request from the Trump administration and the Justice Department, which wanted California to set aside what it claimed could be thousands of ballots cast by people who are not U.S. citizens. An emergency request like this asks the Court to act within hours or days, skipping the months of argument a normal case takes — it's the legal equivalent of pulling a fire alarm. But every lower court that looked at the government's evidence had already called its methodology for identifying these supposed noncitizen voters 'uncertain,' and none had found it solid enough to act on. The Supreme Court's refusal, issued just as the window to change anything before Election Day was closing, meant the ballots stayed in the count.

In Michigan, a state whose Senate race could help decide which party controls that chamber, Democratic Representative Haley Stevens did something her own party doesn't see often: she endorsed the Republican, Mike Rogers, over her party's own nominee, Abdul El-Sayed. The break came after a dispute over a political ad — Stevens called it antisemitic, and El-Sayed's campaign, instead of patching things up, asked her to stop appearing at events with him. Crossing party lines to back the other side's candidate is rare because it hands the opposing campaign a powerful talking point ('even people in her own party won't vote for him') and can cost a politician trust within her own ranks for years afterward.

Meanwhile, a new NBC News poll landed showing President Trump's disapproval rating — the share of people who say they don't approve of the job he's doing — at 60%, higher than at any point in either of his two terms in office. Vice President Vance spent the final hours of the campaign alongside Trump making stops around the country, trying to shore up support in close races. A disapproval number that high, this close to a national vote, tends to weigh on candidates from the president's own party, since voters often use midterm elections to send a message about how the sitting president is doing.

None of these three things caused each other. But they landed in the same 48 hours for a reason: campaigns, courts, and pollsters all move fastest right before an election, because that's when the incentives to act — or to delay acting until it's too late to matter — are strongest. A losing side in a lawsuit wants a ruling before the deadline, not after. A candidate weighing a risky endorsement wants it to land while it can still change minds. And a pollster wants the last word on where the public stands before the ballots are actually counted.

Taken together, the three stories describe the pressure building around a single vote: a legal fight over who gets to count which ballots, a party struggling to hold its own candidates together, and a president whose public standing was sinking just as his party's control of Congress hung in the balance.

Words worth knowing

  • Crossing party lines — When a politician publicly supports a candidate from the opposing party instead of their own party's nominee.
  • Disapproval rating — The percentage of people surveyed who say they do not approve of how a political leader is doing their job.

Bombs Hit a Food Center in Ukraine, and the Death Count Is Still Unclear

Russia dropped ten guided bombs on a town called Komyshuvakha, in Ukraine. The bombs hit a school building where people were handing out food and supplies, and a shelter right next to it. Ukraine's leader, President Volodymyr Zelenskyy, says more than twenty people died, though different news groups report slightly different numbers. The attack happened just as a short, local pause in fighting nearby was about to end.

Why this mattersThis happened at a place where people were going to get food and safety, not to fight. When something like this happens, it's harder for anyone to trust that an agreement to pause fighting will actually hold. It also shows how even the facts of something this serious, like how many people died, can be hard to pin down right away.

Komyshuvakha is a small town in Ukraine, a country that Russia has been attacking for years. Russia sent ten guided bombs there. A guided bomb is a bomb that can be steered toward one exact spot, like a remote-control car aimed at a target, instead of just falling and landing wherever gravity takes it.

The bombs hit a school building. People were using it to give out food, water, and other aid to families who needed help. Right next door was a shelter, a safe place where people go to get away from danger. Both buildings were hit.

A Strike on an Aid Site in Ukraine Reignites the Question: Does Anyone Actually Want This War to Slow Down?

Russian forces fired ten guided bombs into the town of Komyshuvakha in southern Ukraine, hitting a school building used to hand out humanitarian aid and a shelter next to it. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said dozens of people were killed and called the strike proof that Russia has no interest in calming the war down. The attack happened on the same day a separate, local ceasefire near the city of Kherson was due to expire, and as cyberattacks knocked out air-raid warning apps in both Russia and Ukraine.

Why this mattersStories like this are hard to read cleanly because the facts themselves are contested in real time, and that is exactly the situation much of the world's information about this war is produced in — which is worth noticing the next time a single headline number gets repeated as if it were certain. It also matters for anyone following whether this war might wind down soon: strikes on aid sites right as truces expire are the kind of evidence both sides use to argue the other one isn't serious about peace, and those arguments shape whether other countries keep sending weapons, money, or pressure toward a ceasefire.
Read the whole story

Komyshuvakha is a small town in Ukraine's Zaporizhzhia region, a part of the country that has seen heavy fighting since Russia's full-scale invasion began in 2022. According to Zelenskyy, Russian forces dropped ten guided bombs on an area that included a building being used to distribute humanitarian aid — food, medicine, and other basic supplies sent to help civilians get through the war — and a shelter nearby where people had taken cover. A guided bomb is a weapon that uses satellite or laser targeting to hit a specific point, rather than falling freely like an old-fashioned bomb, which makes claims that it was an accident harder to accept at face value, though not impossible given how chaotic and imprecise war can still be even with modern weapons.

Zelenskyy posted about the strike on social media, describing it as 'further proof that Russia wants no de-escalation' — meaning no slowing down or softening of the war, the opposite of an escalation. This is a claim about intent, not just about damage, and it matters because the world has spent months watching on-and-off talk of peace negotiations and partial truces between Russia and Ukraine. If a strike like this was deliberate and aimed at a known aid site, it suggests one side is not interested in the kind of goodwill gestures that usually come before real negotiations. Russia has not offered its own detailed account of the strike in the reporting available, which is itself part of the story: in an active war, the side being accused often says little, or says something that contradicts what the other side and outside observers report.

How many people actually died is disputed, and that gap matters. NBC News and Al Jazeera's own headline both cited 26 deaths, while Bloomberg, Al Jazeera's full article text, and Hong Kong's South China Morning Post cited 24. A two-person difference might look small, but it shows something important: nobody doing the reporting has an independently verified final count. In an active war zone, journalists usually cannot walk through rubble themselves, count bodies, or cross-check hospital records in real time, so even careful outlets end up relying on different officials, rescue workers, or local sources who may not agree with each other. That is not the same as anyone lying — it is what happens when information has to travel out of a dangerous place quickly.

The strike also landed right as a much smaller, local ceasefire around the city of Kherson was expiring. Unlike a full ceasefire, which would pause fighting across the whole war, this was a limited, local arrangement — the kind sometimes agreed to let aid in, let civilians leave, or simply reduce fighting in one specific area for a set period. Its expiration on the same day as a bombing elsewhere does not prove the two events are connected, but it adds to a pattern that worries diplomats: these narrow, local pauses keep collapsing or ending right as larger violence continues, which raises the question of whether they do much good at all, or whether they are only ever symbolic.

On top of the physical attack, cyberattacks disrupted the smartphone apps both countries use to warn people when an air raid is coming. These alert apps are a basic piece of wartime infrastructure now — something like a fire alarm for an entire city, giving people minutes to get to shelter before missiles or drones arrive. Knocking them out on both sides at once suggests this wasn't a one-directional sabotage attempt by only one country, but some kind of exchange or coincidence of disruption affecting both populations, which makes an already dangerous moment more dangerous: people relying on a warning system that briefly wasn't working.

Words worth knowing
  • guided bomb — A bomb that uses satellite or laser targeting to hit a specific spot, rather than falling freely and landing wherever gravity takes it.
  • ceasefire — An agreement between fighting sides to stop attacking, which can cover an entire war or, as in this case, just one small area for a limited time.
  • de-escalation — A reduction in the intensity of a conflict — fewer attacks, smaller ones, or steps toward talking instead of fighting — the opposite of escalation.

Tech Stocks Take a Wild Ride on Wall Street

Last week, shares in some of the biggest computer and internet companies in the world swung up and down like a rollercoaster. Companies like Nvidia, Meta, Oracle, and Marvell Technology all saw their stock prices drop suddenly. By Monday, some prices were climbing back up again, especially for tech companies.

Why this mattersMany of these companies make the chips and computers inside phones, game consoles, and the AI tools people use every day. When their stock prices swing wildly, it can affect people's savings, retirement funds, and even the prices of the gadgets we use. It's also a reminder that even giant, successful companies can have uncertain, bumpy weeks.
Read the whole story

Imagine a company is like a pizza, cut into millions of tiny slices. Each slice is called a 'share,' and people can buy and sell them. When lots of people want to sell their slices at once, the price of each slice drops. That is what happened last week to companies like Nvidia and Meta — their share prices dipped about 1%. Oracle's dropped a lot more, 8%, after Oracle said it wants to borrow and raise $20 billion to build giant warehouses full of computers for artificial intelligence, or AI.

Here's how big $20 billion really is. If you counted one dollar every single second, non-stop, day and night, it would take you more than 600 years to count that much money. That's how much Oracle wants to spend building AI computer centers.

Two other companies had an even bigger shake-up. Marvell Technology announced it is buying another company called Lumentum for $115 billion — an amount so large it would take you over 3,600 years to count out loud. Marvell's own stock price fell almost 13%, and Lumentum's jumped around too, falling 18% at one point.

All these wobbles have people on Wall Street — the famous street in New York where lots of stock trading happens — asking a big question. Has everyone gotten too excited about AI, the way people sometimes get too excited about a new toy before realizing it's not quite as amazing as they thought? Some experts think prices just needed a small correction. Others wonder if this is the beginning of a bigger drop.

Words worth knowing

Wall Street Gets Nervous About Its Favourite Bet: Artificial Intelligence

Shares in some of the biggest names in artificial intelligence took a hit this week, with chipmaker Marvell dropping nearly 13% and software giant Oracle falling 8% in a single day. The sharp swings have traders asking an uncomfortable question: has the stock market gotten too excited about AI, and is the excitement now starting to wear off?

Why this mattersIf you have a pension, a parent with a 401(k) retirement account, or money in almost any stock market index fund, you likely have some exposure to these AI companies, since they now make up such a large share of the market's total value. A real slowdown in AI investment wouldn't just affect tech workers in California — it could ripple through retirement savings, job markets, and the broader economy well beyond Silicon Valley.
Read the whole story

For most of this year, a small group of companies tied to artificial intelligence has been doing most of the heavy lifting for the entire US stock market. Chipmakers like Nvidia, cloud companies like Oracle, and tech giants like Meta have all poured enormous amounts of money into building the computing power AI needs — and investors have rewarded them by buying up their shares, pushing stock prices to record highs. This week, that story wobbled.

The trigger was partly about debt. Oracle announced it wants to raise $10 billion by selling new shares of stock (which dilutes, or shrinks, the ownership stake of existing shareholders) and another $10 billion by issuing bonds — essentially IOUs that pay investors back with interest over time. The money is meant to fund more data centres, the giant warehouses full of computers that run AI systems like ChatGPT. Investors didn't love it: Oracle's stock fell 8% on the news, a sign that some worry the company is borrowing heavily to chase a bet that might not pay off as quickly as hoped.

A separate shock hit the chip side of the AI industry. Marvell Technology announced it's buying a smaller company called Lumentum for $115 billion — a huge sum, even by Silicon Valley standards. Rather than cheering the deal, Marvell's own stock dropped almost 13%, while Lumentum's shares, strangely, fell 18% too, even though it was the one being bought. That's unusual: when a company gets bought out, its stock price typically rises toward the purchase price. The drop suggests investors are uneasy about whether the deal makes financial sense, or whether it's a sign that AI-related companies are scrambling to combine forces because the ground beneath them feels shakier than it looked a few months ago.

Nvidia and Meta, the two companies most associated with the AI boom, only slipped about 1% each — a mild dip compared to Marvell and Lumentum's tumble, but still notable, given how much of the stock market's overall gains this year have depended on just a handful of these companies continuing to rise. When the companies at the centre of a boom start to wobble, even slightly, the effect can ripple outward to everything connected to them.

By Monday, the mood had shifted again. US stock futures — bets made before the market officially opens that signal which direction prices are likely to move — turned higher, led by technology shares. Falling oil prices helped, since cheaper oil tends to ease worries about inflation and interest rates, which in turn makes investors more willing to take risks on growth stocks like tech. Whether that bounce is the start of a real recovery or just a brief calm before more turbulence is exactly what traders are arguing about.

The bigger debate running underneath all of this is whether the AI investment boom is fundamentally sound or badly overextended. Some analysts think this week's selloff is a normal, healthy correction — a pause after prices climbed too fast, not a warning that something is broken. Others think it's the first crack in a bubble: a sign that companies have been spending and borrowing on the assumption that AI will generate enormous profits soon, and that reality might take much longer to catch up to the hype.

  • Market correction — A correction is a noticeable drop in stock prices after a period of rapid gains, generally seen as a normal cooling-off rather than a crisis.

    • Being first or fastest to build AI capacity could lock in customers and market share before competitors catch up.
    • Interest rates and borrowing costs could rise later, making debt more expensive if a company waits.
    • Moving slowly risks falling permanently behind rivals who are willing to take the financial risk now.

    No, it's too risky

    • Debt has to be repaid regardless of whether AI demand grows as quickly as companies are betting it will.
    • Diluting shareholders and piling on bonds simultaneously signals financial strain, which can spook investors and raise future borrowing costs.
    • A more cautious, gradual buildout would let companies adjust if AI profits take longer to materialize than expected.
