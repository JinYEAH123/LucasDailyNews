The U.S. Wants to Charge More Money on Things Bought From Other Countries

On Wednesday, the United States government said it wants to add new fees on goods coming from places like China, Japan, Brazil, and more than a dozen other countries. These fees are called tariffs, and they make foreign products cost more. The government says it's doing this because those countries help their factories unfairly. But a top court already said an earlier version of this plan wasn't allowed, so people are watching to see if this new one will work.

Why this mattersWhen tariffs go up, stores usually pass that extra cost on to shoppers — so things your family buys, like sneakers, phones, or toys, could get more expensive. Other countries might also add their own tariffs on things America sells them, which could affect jobs here at home too.
Read the whole story

Imagine a toy made in China costs $10 at the store. Right now, the store already pays a small fee, called a tariff, to bring that toy into America. The government wants to raise that fee a lot — maybe 20% more, or even 40% if talks with China don't go well.

It's not only China. The plan also adds new fees on things from Europe, Japan, Brazil, South Korea, Taiwan, and about a dozen other countries.

The government says this is fair because those countries give their factories special help, called subsidies, which lets them sell things cheaper than American factories can.

But many experts say this new plan looks a lot like an earlier one. The Supreme Court — the highest court in the country — already said that earlier plan gave the president too much power. Now people are asking if this new version will survive being challenged in court too.

  • Subsidy — A subsidy is money a government gives to help its own companies, sometimes letting them sell things for less.

Trump Tries Tariffs Again — This Time Through the Back Door

On Wednesday the Trump administration proposed a fresh set of tariffs — taxes on imported goods — hitting roughly 14 countries, including China, the European Union, Japan, Brazil, South Korea and Taiwan. Chinese goods could face duties up to 20%, rising to 40% if trade talks break down. The move comes just months after the Supreme Court struck down the administration's previous tariffs for going beyond what a president is legally allowed to do, and this new round is widely seen as an attempt to get to the same destination by a different legal road.

Why this mattersIf these tariffs take effect, everyday items — electronics, clothing, cars, appliances — could get more expensive, since much of what's sold in the US and used by families is imported from the countries being targeted. It also matters beyond prices: this is a live argument over how much power a president has to set economic policy without Congress or the courts signing off, which shapes how the US makes major decisions for years to come.
Read the whole story

A tariff is a tax a government charges on goods coming in from another country. When the United States puts a 20% tariff on Chinese steel, for example, an American company importing that steel pays 20% extra to the government — a cost that usually gets passed on to whoever buys the steel next, all the way down to the price of a car or a washing machine on a store shelf. Tariffs can protect domestic industries by making foreign goods more expensive and less competitive, which is the administration's stated goal here.

This time, the legal justification is different from before. The Commerce Department — the federal agency that handles trade and industry — ran an investigation into what the administration calls "unfair manufacturing practices": foreign governments subsidizing their own factories and producers, letting them sell goods more cheaply than American competitors can. Subsidies are payments or tax breaks a government gives to its own companies, effectively helping them compete abroad. The administration argues this amounts to cheating, and that tariffs are a fair response.

The reason this needs a new legal footing at all is that the Supreme Court already rejected the administration's last attempt. Those earlier tariffs were imposed using emergency-powers law — legislation meant to let a president act fast during genuine crises, like a war or a sudden economic shock. The Court ruled that broad, long-running tariffs on dozens of countries didn't fit what the emergency law was written for; the power to set tariffs generally belongs to Congress, not the president acting alone. So the administration is now trying a different legal route: not "emergency," but "unfair practices" uncovered by a formal investigation.

Whether this new approach holds up is genuinely unsettled. Some legal experts think an investigation-based process gives the tariffs a sturdier footing, because it follows procedures Congress has actually written into trade law for exactly this kind of situation. Others think it's the same move in a different costume — a way to reach the same sweeping result the courts already said was too broad, just through a different door. That disagreement will likely play out in court challenges over the coming months.

Reaction from trading partners and economists has been sharp. Governments in the EU, Japan, South Korea and elsewhere are warning they may respond with retaliatory tariffs of their own — taxes aimed back at American exports, like farm goods or machinery, which would raise costs for American producers trying to sell abroad. Economists point out that tariffs are ultimately paid by importers and, usually, by consumers through higher prices, not by the foreign country directly, which complicates the administration's framing of this as foreign nations footing the bill.

Words worth knowing
  • Tariff — A tax a government charges on goods imported from another country, usually making those goods more expensive for buyers.
  • Subsidy — Money or tax breaks a government gives to its own companies to help them produce goods more cheaply or compete better abroad.
  • Retaliatory tariffs — Tariffs one country imposes specifically in response to another country's tariffs, often targeting that country's exports.

  1. Should governments be allowed to charge extra fees on goods from other countries to protect their own factories?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes, it protects jobs

    • Making foreign goods pricier might push people to buy things made at home, keeping local factory jobs safe.
    • If other countries help their factories unfairly, tariffs can even out the competition.
    • Protecting important industries, like steel or electronics, could matter a lot if there's ever a shortage or emergency.

    No, it hurts shoppers

    • Tariffs usually make everyday things like clothes, toys, and electronics cost more for families.
    • Other countries can strike back with their own tariffs, which can hurt businesses that sell things abroad.

    No, presidents need to act fast

    • Trade problems can change quickly, and waiting for lawmakers to agree might be too slow to protect jobs.

  1. Should the US use tariffs to protect American manufacturing, even if it means higher prices for consumers?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes, protect manufacturing

    • Factories and manufacturing jobs provide stable, long-term employment that a service-based economy doesn't always replace.
    • Relying heavily on foreign manufacturing, especially from a single country, creates risks if relations sour or supply chains break down, as seen during recent global disruptions.
    • If foreign governments are genuinely subsidizing their industries to undercut American competitors, matching tariffs can be seen as leveling an already uneven playing field.

    No, prioritize low prices

    • Higher prices from tariffs hit lower-income families hardest, since essentials take up a bigger share of their budgets.
    • Tariffs often trigger retaliation, hurting American exporters like farmers and manufacturers who sell abroad.
    • Protecting inefficient industries with tariffs can reduce the pressure on companies to innovate or become more competitive on their own.

  2. Should a president be allowed broad authority to set tariffs through investigations and executive action, or should this power rest mainly with Congress?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Executive authority is appropriate

    • Trade conditions can shift quickly, and a president can act faster than a divided Congress debating legislation for months.
    • Formal investigations, like the Commerce Department's, provide a documented, procedural basis rather than an arbitrary decision.
    • Many other countries give their leaders similar flexibility in trade policy, and matching that speed can be a strategic advantage.

    Congress should hold this power

    • The Constitution assigns tariff and trade power to Congress specifically because it affects the whole economy and should reflect broad democratic input, not one person's judgment.
    • Letting presidents repeatedly find new legal workarounds after courts reject one approach undermines the idea that any branch of government has real limits.
    • Major economic decisions affecting millions of consumers and businesses deserve the debate and compromise that legislative process is designed to provide.

The Robot-Brain Company Had a Very Wobbly Week

The companies that make the computer chips powering AI had a scary day. Their stock prices — basically how much people think they're worth — dropped fast, some by more than one in every ten dollars. At the same time, the President said he might forgive the boss of the biggest chip company for a crime nobody has even accused him of yet.

Why this mattersThe chips Nvidia makes are inside the AI tools lots of grown-ups use at work, and inside video game consoles and phones too. When companies like this wobble, it can shake retirement savings and jobs that depend on them, even for families who've never bought a single AI chip.
Read the whole story

Nvidia makes the special computer chips that let AI chatbots and robots think fast. For months, people have been paying huge amounts of money for pieces of Nvidia and companies like it, betting that AI will keep growing bigger and bigger.

Then, suddenly, lots of people got worried at once. A Japanese company called SoftBank, which owns pieces of AI companies, lost as much as 15 cents of every dollar it was worth in just one day. Nvidia lost about 8 cents of every dollar too. It's like if your favorite trading cards were worth $10 each yesterday, and today everyone decided they're only worth $9.15.

On the very same day, something odd happened. Nvidia's boss, a man named Jensen Huang, was removed from his job for a few days and then put back. People say it happened because of worries he had talked with people connected to China's government in a way that worried U.S. officials. Huang has to answer questions about it in Congress next week.

President Trump said he'd be willing to pardon Huang — meaning officially forgive him — even though no one has charged Huang with any crime yet. Trump also said the government might buy a piece of Nvidia itself, like becoming a part-owner.

Words worth knowing
  • bubble (in money terms) — When something's price gets pushed way higher than what it's really worth, like a soap bubble that looks huge and shiny right before it pops.
  • pardon — An official forgiveness from a leader that cancels a punishment for a crime — even, sometimes, before anyone has proven a crime happened.

AI Stocks Plunge on Bubble Fears — Same Day Trump Offers to Pardon Nvidia's CEO

Tech and AI-related stocks crashed across global markets, with Japan's SoftBank falling as much as 15% and U.S. chip giant Nvidia sinking around 8.5% at one point during the trading day. On the very same day, President Trump said he'd be willing to pardon Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang if it became necessary — just days before Huang is due to testify to Congress about an alleged contact with Chinese intelligence. No charges have actually been filed against Huang, which makes the pardon offer unusually hard to explain.

Why this mattersIf AI stocks really are in a bubble, the fallout wouldn't stay confined to Wall Street — pension funds, retirement accounts, and index funds that millions of ordinary people hold are often loaded with these same tech stocks. And the unusual political attention on Nvidia's CEO raises a separate question that matters regardless of where you live: how should governments treat powerful companies that sit at the center of both national security and the stock market?
Read the whole story

For months, money has poured into companies connected to artificial intelligence — the chipmakers that build the processors AI needs, the software firms writing the programs, and the data centers storing it all. Investors bet that AI would reshape nearly every industry, and stock prices climbed accordingly. Then, in a single trading session, a lot of that confidence evaporated. SoftBank, the Japanese investment giant with major stakes in chip and AI companies, fell by as much as 15%. Nvidia, whose chips power most of the world's advanced AI systems, dropped roughly 8.5% intraday — meaning at its lowest point during the day, before partially recovering. Even Anthropic, a major AI company, saw declines before the U.S. markets had properly opened (known as premarket trading).

The underlying fear driving the sell-off is that AI stocks have become what's called a valuation bubble — a situation where a company's stock price reflects excitement and hope about the future far more than its actual current profits or realistic prospects. Bubbles can keep inflating for a long time, but when enough investors start doubting the story at once, prices can fall fast, because everyone tries to sell before everyone else does. Nobody can say for certain whether this was the moment the AI bubble finally burst or just a rough day that recovers soon — reasonable analysts disagree, and that uncertainty itself is part of what's making markets jumpy.

Layered on top of the market panic was a stranger, more political story. Jensen Huang, Nvidia's CEO, was briefly forced out of his job and then reinstated days earlier, reportedly connected to an allegation that he had some kind of contact with Chinese intelligence services. Huang is expected to testify before Congress next week about exactly what happened. Into that tense moment stepped President Trump, who told reporters he'd be willing to pardon Huang "if necessary."

A presidential pardon normally forgives someone for a specific crime they've been convicted of, or are at serious risk of being charged with. The odd part here is that no charges have been announced against Huang at all — so it's genuinely unclear what crime a pardon would even cover. Critics, including some legal commentators, called the offer an unusual and premature intervention by a sitting president into a company currently under investigation, especially given that Huang hasn't been formally accused of anything. The administration hasn't explained the legal reasoning behind the idea, or what specifically prompted it.

Trump also floated a second idea the same day: that the U.S. government could take an equity stake in Nvidia — meaning the government would become a part-owner of the company, much like a shareholder who buys stock. This would be a significant departure from how the U.S. government typically relates to private companies, and no details were given about how it might work, what it would cost, or what the government would get in return. Combined with the pardon comments, it left Nvidia at the center of two separate controversies on the same day its stock price was already tumbling.

Words worth knowing
  • valuation bubble — A situation where a company's stock price is driven mainly by excitement and expectation rather than its actual current profits, making a sudden price crash more likely.
  • pardon — An official forgiveness from a government leader that cancels a criminal charge or conviction, usually offered after someone has been accused or convicted of a specific crime.
  • equity stake — Partial ownership of a company, usually bought by purchasing its stock, which entitles the owner to a share of its profits and value.

A Good Report Card Made the President Angry

The U.S. government just announced that the country's economy grew even faster than first thought. Instead of celebrating, President Trump said the new, better number was fake and wants to investigate his own team for publishing it.

Why this mattersBanks, business owners, and the people who decide how much it costs to borrow money all depend on these numbers being true. If people stop trusting official numbers, it becomes harder for everyone to know what is really happening, a bit like trying to ride a bike without trusting your own eyes.
Read the whole story

Every few months, a group called the Commerce Department measures how much bigger the whole U.S. economy got. This number is called GDP, short for Gross Domestic Product. Think of it like measuring how much taller you grew this year, except it's measuring every business, job, and sale in the entire country.

First, the Commerce Department said the economy grew by 3.4% over the summer. Then they did more careful counting and said it actually grew by 4.3%, which is even better news than before.

Updating a number later is completely normal. It happens all the time, kind of like when a teacher re-checks a stack of tests and finds a few kids actually scored higher than first marked.

But President Trump called the new, improved number fake. He wants to investigate the Commerce Department, even though he is the one who chose the people running it. So far, he has not shown any proof that anyone did the math wrong.

Words worth knowing
  • GDP — A number that measures how much bigger a country's whole economy grew, by adding up everything people built, sold, and worked on.
  • revised estimate — An updated number that replaces an earlier guess once more careful counting has been done.

Trump Calls His Own Government's Good Economic News 'Fake'

The U.S. Commerce Department updated its estimate of how fast the economy grew over the summer, raising the number from 3.4% to a stronger 4.3%. That is normally a non-event — a routine statistical correction. But President Trump reacted by accusing the agency, which he appointed, of deliberately publishing false figures to hurt Republicans in the midterm elections, and said he wants to investigate it.

Why this mattersThe number itself barely changes anyone's life, but trust in official statistics quietly underpins things that do — mortgage rates, job markets, how much your family's savings or a parent's business might be worth. If people stop believing the government's own numbers, even for no clear reason, it gets harder for everyone to make basic financial decisions with confidence.
Read the whole story

Three times a year, the Commerce Department publishes an estimate of GDP — gross domestic product, the total value of everything the country produced in a given period. The first estimate always comes out fast, using incomplete data, and gets revised as more complete numbers roll in over the following weeks and months. This time, the revision moved growth up, from 3.4% to 4.3% at an annualized rate — meaning if the economy kept growing at that pace for a full year, that's roughly how much bigger it would get.

Upward revisions like this happen constantly and are built into the agency's publicly announced release schedule; nobody at the Commerce Department decides on a whim to change a number, and economists who track the process could see this one coming. That is what makes President Trump's reaction unusual. He did not dispute a number that made him look bad — this one was good news, showing the economy growing faster than first thought. He called it 'fake' anyway, offered no evidence that anyone had manipulated it, and said he wants to investigate the very department whose job is to produce these statistics.

It is worth sitting with who is accusing whom here. The Commerce Department is part of the executive branch, meaning its leadership answers to the president. The current secretary was Trump's own pick. So this is not one political party accusing the other of lying — it is a sitting president publicly saying his own administration's official economic data cannot be trusted, with no explanation of what, specifically, he thinks was faked or why.

That distinction matters because of who relies on these numbers. Investors decide where to put money based partly on GDP figures. Businesses use them to plan hiring and spending. The Federal Reserve, the central bank that sets interest rates for the whole economy, leans on this data to decide whether to raise or lower rates. None of those decisions work well if the people producing the numbers are publicly accused, by their own boss, of cooking them — even if, as in this case, nothing suggests they did.

Governments throughout history have sometimes falsified economic data to make themselves look better; it is a well-documented tactic in countries where no free press or independent agency can check the claim. What makes this episode notable is closer to the reverse: a leader attacking statistics that happened to be favorable, undermining confidence in the system that produces them for reasons that remain unexplained.

Words worth knowing
  • GDP (Gross Domestic Product) — A measure of the total value of everything a country produces in a given period, used as the main scorecard for how fast an economy is growing or shrinking.
  • Revision — An update to an earlier economic estimate once more complete data becomes available — a routine, scheduled part of how statistics agencies work, not a sign something went wrong.
  • Federal Reserve — The United States' central bank, which sets interest rates for the whole economy and depends on accurate government data, like GDP, to make those decisions.

  1. Should statistics agencies like the Commerce Department be made independent from the president, the way the Federal Reserve already is, to prevent this kind of political interference?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes, make them independent

    • Markets, businesses, and the Fed all need confidence that economic data is untouched by politics, regardless of who happens to be president.

    Yes, investigate it

    • A proper investigation costs little and would either expose a real problem or clear the agency's name — both useful outcomes.
    • Dismissing every claim outright because a leader has said false things before risks ignoring the one time it happens to be true.

    No, treat it as unfounded

    • There is already a public, pre-announced technical process for GDP revisions that independent economists and journalists can and did verify on their own.
    • Launching an investigation into every unsupported claim lends it legitimacy it has not earned and spreads distrust that isn't warranted.
    • Trusting the long-established, well-understood statistical process is more reasonable than acting on suspicion with no evidence behind it.
