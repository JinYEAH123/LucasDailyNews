America's Money Chief Declares an 'Economic D-Day' Against Iran

The United States' top money official, Scott Bessent, announced a big new plan to squeeze Iran's economy. He gave it a dramatic name — 'Operation Economic Outcast' — and compared it to D-Day, one of the biggest military operations in history. The plan tries to stop money, oil, and gold from reaching Iran, even when that money passes through other countries like China.

Why this mattersOil is used to make gasoline for cars and buses, and even shows up in some of the plastic in your toys. When countries fight over oil and shipping routes, prices for things your family buys can go up or down. If ships really get stopped in the Strait of Hormuz, it could make gas more expensive everywhere, including at the pump your family uses.
Read the whole story

Imagine you get in trouble at school, and the principal tells every other kid: 'Don't trade snacks with him, or you're in trouble too.' That is kind of what the US is doing to Iran. Iran is a country in the Middle East. The US and Iran have been fighting — not just with words, but with missiles — for six months.

The new plan is called 'sanctions.' Sanctions are rules that stop people or countries from trading with someone. The US wants to stop Iran from selling oil, gold, and even using computer money called crypto. Iran needs to sell oil to get money, like a farmer needs to sell vegetables to buy shoes.

The tricky part: China buys about 9 out of every 10 barrels of oil Iran sells. That's a huge amount — like if a neighborhood bake sale sold almost every single cookie to just one family. The US says China must stop too, or face punishment. President Trump has even been calling world leaders directly, asking them to stop buying from Iran.

Iran is angry. It has threatened to block ships in a narrow sea passage called the Strait of Hormuz, where a huge share of the world's oil ships travel through. Grown-ups disagree on what happens next: some think China will keep buying Iranian oil no matter what, because it needs the oil and doesn't like being told what to do. Others think the pressure could work, especially if banks and shipping companies get scared of being punished too.

Words worth knowing
  • sanctions — Rules one country makes to stop people from trading with another country, like a time-out for buying and selling.
  • Strait of Hormuz — A narrow strip of ocean near Iran where many oil ships must pass through, like a hallway everyone has to walk down.

The US Declares an 'Economic D-Day' on Iran — and This Time China's a Target Too

The US Treasury Secretary, Scott Bessent, just announced a giant new sanctions campaign against Iran — and he gave it a war-sized name: 'Operation Economic Outcast.' He called it an 'economic D-Day,' comparing it to the massive Allied invasion that helped turn World War II. The target isn't just Iran. It's every country and company still doing business with Iran, including its single biggest oil customer: China.

Why this mattersEven if you've never thought about Iran or sanctions, this affects the price of gas your family pays and how stable global trade feels, because a fifth of the world's oil passes through the waterway Iran is threatening to block. It's also a preview of how the US and China — the two biggest economies on Earth — might handle disagreements without going to war, using money and trade as the battlefield instead.

The US Treasury Secretary, Scott Bessent, announced a huge new sanctions push against Iran, and gave it a name that sounds like a military operation: 'Operation Economic Outcast.' He called it an 'economic D-Day' — a reference to the massive Allied invasion of Nazi-occupied France in 1944, one of the turning points of World War II. It's a big claim: Bessent is saying this campaign is meant to be just as decisive, only fought with financial rules instead of soldiers.

The plan targets the networks that keep Iran's economy connected to the rest of the world — the ships that carry its oil, the gold it uses to store value, the planes it flies, and even the cryptocurrency transfers it uses to move money around sanctions. Those networks run through places like the United Arab Emirates, Hong Kong, mainland China, Singapore, and Switzerland — financial hubs that Iran has used as pass-throughs to keep trading despite years of existing sanctions.

The tool doing the heavy lifting is called a 'secondary sanction.' Normal US sanctions only stop American companies from doing business with a target. Secondary sanctions go further: they threaten any foreign bank or company, anywhere in the world, with being cut off from the US financial system if it keeps trading with Iran. Because so much of global trade and banking runs through US dollars, that threat carries real weight — even for companies that have never set foot in America.

The most striking target is China, which buys roughly 90 percent of Iran's oil exports and is by far its biggest customer. Bessent said flatly that China would not get an exemption this time — a notable statement, since China is also the world's second-largest economy and a country the US usually treats carefully. President Trump has reportedly been personally calling other world leaders, urging them to cut their own ties with Tehran.

The timing matters too. This sanctions push comes about six months after a period of open conflict between the US and Israel on one side and Iran on the other. A deadline for a ceasefire — an agreement to stop the fighting — passed without both sides reaching a deal. In response, Iran has threatened to seize ships passing through the Strait of Hormuz, a narrow strip of water between Iran and the Arabian Peninsula that carries about a fifth of the world's oil every single day. Blocking or threatening that passage would ripple through gas prices worldwide.

What nobody can say for certain yet is whether any of this will actually work. China has weathered plenty of US pressure before, and both Iran and China have spent years building workarounds — disguised shipping routes, renamed tankers, non-dollar payment systems — specifically designed to dodge sanctions like these. Some analysts think this round, because it targets the entire supply chain at once, could genuinely bite. Others think it will mostly add cost and hassle without stopping the oil from flowing.

Words worth knowing
  • Secondary sanctions — Rules that punish foreign companies and banks — not just Americans — if they keep doing business with a country the US has sanctioned.
  • Strait of Hormuz — A narrow sea passage between Iran and the Arabian Peninsula that roughly a fifth of the world's oil ships through every day.
  • Ceasefire — An agreement between fighting sides to stop shooting, at least for a while — though it doesn't necessarily end the underlying conflict.

Chip Companies Get Nervous Before the Big Report Card

Nvidia, a company that makes tiny computer chips used for AI, is about to announce how much money it made — like handing in a report card. Right before that happened, the stock prices of other chip companies dropped. On the other side of the world, a Chinese tech giant called Alibaba saw its stock drop too, after it said it made way less money than before.

Why this mattersThe apps and games you use, and the computers your school or your parents' workplace relies on, are increasingly built using AI chips like the ones these companies make. If companies decide AI spending isn't paying off, they might slow down building new things — or prices for tech gadgets could go up to cover the cost.
Read the whole story

Nvidia makes special chips that help computers do AI things, like writing stories or recognizing pictures. Every few months, Nvidia tells everyone how much money it earned. This week, right before that announcement, three other chip companies — Intel, AMD, and TSMC — saw their stock prices fall. Intel dropped about 5%, like a $100 toy suddenly being worth $95.

People who buy pieces of these companies, called stock, got worried. AI has been the exciting new thing everyone wants to build. But building it costs a huge amount of money, and some people are asking: is it actually worth that much? Making things worse, reports say Nvidia might raise the price of its AI computers by more than 15% for its biggest customers.

Across the ocean, Alibaba, a giant Chinese tech company, announced it would sell $10 billion worth of new stock to raise money for AI projects. That is such a big number that if you saved one dollar every single second, it would take you more than 300 years to save that much. This came right after Alibaba said its profit — the money left over after paying its bills — had dropped by 75%, like a piggy bank that used to hold $100 now only holding $25.

Both stories show the same worry, just in two different countries. People are asking whether all this AI spending will really pay off, or whether companies are spending too much, too fast, on something still new and unproven.

Words worth knowing
  • stock — A tiny piece of ownership in a company that people can buy; when the company does well its stock is worth more, and when it struggles the stock is worth less.
  • profit — The money a company has left over after it pays for everything it needs, like materials, rent, and workers' pay.

The Market Gets Nervous Right Before Nvidia Speaks

This week, shares in some of the world's biggest chipmakers took a hard hit, just days before Nvidia — the company at the center of the AI boom — was set to report its earnings on Wednesday. Intel dropped about 5%, AMD about 4%, and Taiwan's TSMC about 3%, all within days of each other. On the other side of the world, Chinese tech giant Alibaba's stock also fell after it said it would sell $10 billion worth of new shares to keep funding its AI ambitions. Investors on both sides of the Pacific seem to be asking the same nervous question: is all this AI spending actually going to pay off?

Why this mattersIf you've ever used a phone with an AI assistant, played a game with AI-generated content, or watched a video recommended by an algorithm, you've relied on the exact chips these companies make. Whether AI spending pays off affects more than stock prices — it shapes what gets funded, which jobs get created or cut, and how fast (or slowly) AI tools show up in daily life.

Chips are the tiny computer brains inside everything from your phone to the servers that run ChatGPT, and right now, companies are racing to build faster, more powerful ones for artificial intelligence. Nvidia makes the chips most AI companies depend on, so when Nvidia reports how its business is doing, the whole industry watches closely. Ahead of that report, investors got jittery — Intel, AMD, and TSMC all saw their stock prices drop in the days before Nvidia's numbers came out.

Part of the anxiety comes from something investors call the 'AI trade' — the pattern of buying up tech stocks because everyone expects AI to be hugely profitable in the future. The problem is that stock prices for these companies have climbed so high that some investors worry they're priced for a perfect future that might not arrive. Adding to the nerves: reports suggested Nvidia might raise prices on its AI servers — the powerful computers that run AI programs — by more than 15% for its biggest customers, which could squeeze profits for everyone buying that equipment.

Meanwhile in China, Alibaba made its own AI-related move that spooked investors. The company announced it would sell $10 billion worth of new shares — a move called a share placement, where a company creates and sells more of itself to raise cash quickly, but it also means each existing share is worth a slightly smaller piece of the company. Alibaba said the money would go toward building AI infrastructure, the data centers and networks needed to run AI systems. That announcement landed just days after Alibaba revealed its quarterly profit had fallen 75% compared to a year earlier — a steep drop that made investors question whether spending even more on AI right now was wise.

Put together, these two stories point to the same underlying worry, just from opposite sides of the world. American chipmakers are watching to see if Nvidia's massive AI bet will keep paying off, while Alibaba is asking its shareholders to trust that pouring more money into AI infrastructure will eventually be worth it, even as current profits shrink. Neither side has a clear answer yet — investors are essentially betting on a future that hasn't happened.

It's worth noting that most of the reporting on the American chip stocks comes from a single source, and only one other outlet has separately covered the Alibaba side, so the full picture is still filling in. But the pattern itself — nervous investors, huge sums of money, and a technology that hasn't fully proven its payoff — is clear enough to matter.

Words worth knowing
  • AI trade — The pattern of investors buying up tech company stocks because they expect artificial intelligence to become hugely profitable in the future.
  • share placement — When a company creates and sells new shares of itself to raise money quickly, which slightly shrinks the ownership stake of existing shareholders.
  • valuation — How much investors believe a company is worth, based on its stock price compared to its actual profits and growth.

29 US States Say Instagram Was Designed to Be Hard to Stop Using

A big trial started up again in a courtroom in California. Lawyers for 29 US states say Meta, the company that owns Instagram and Facebook, built features on purpose to keep kids scrolling and scrolling. They say this hurt teenagers' mental health. The states want Meta to pay a huge amount of money.

Why this mattersIf you or someone in your family uses Instagram or a similar app, this case is really about you. The court is deciding whether apps can be built to grab your attention on purpose, or whether companies must design them more carefully for kids. Whatever the judge and jury decide could change how the apps on your phone actually work.
Read the whole story

Meta is the company behind Instagram and Facebook. Lawyers from 29 different US states — that's more than half of all 50 states — say Meta made these apps on purpose to be hard to put down. Things like endless scrolling and buzzing notifications, they say, were designed to keep kids using the app as long as possible, even if it hurt them.

The states say this harmed many teenagers' mental health. They took Meta to court in Oakland, California, in front of a judge and jury. Meta tried to delay the trial before it even started, but a judge said no — the trial has to happen now.

The amount of money on the table is enormous. The states say Meta could owe as much as $1.4 trillion. If you paid one dollar every single second without stopping, it would take you more than 44,000 years to pay that back. Meta says the real number, if they lose, is closer to $200 billion — still enough money to build hundreds of hospitals.

Grown-ups on both sides are watching closely. This case could decide how apps are allowed to be built for young people all across the country.

29 States Are Suing Meta Over Instagram's Effect on Teens

A major trial is underway in a federal court in Oakland, California. Attorneys general from 29 US states say Meta, the company behind Instagram and Facebook, built features on purpose to keep teenagers hooked, even when it hurt their mental health. The states want penalties that could reach $1.4 trillion; Meta says the real number, if it loses, would be closer to $200 billion. Either way, the case could change how every social media app designs features for young users.

Why this mattersIf you have Instagram, TikTok, or any app with a never-ending feed, this case is about the very screen you might be scrolling on right now. Whatever the jury decides could change whether apps keep the addictive-style features you use every day, or whether companies are forced to redesign them for anyone under 18.
Read the whole story

Picture an app that never really lets you stop. No natural ending, no 'game over' screen — just an endless scroll of videos and photos, interrupted by little red notification bubbles designed to pull you back the second you look away. That is what 29 US states argue Meta built into Instagram and Facebook on purpose, and they say the company did it knowing it would harm teenagers who were still figuring out how to manage their own attention and emotions.

The lawsuit, now back in court after being delayed once already, was brought by attorneys general — the top lawyers who represent each state's government and can sue companies on behalf of their residents. They argue Meta used specific design choices, like infinite scroll (a feed with no bottom, so you can keep swiping forever) and variable reward notifications (alerts that arrive unpredictably, similar to how a slot machine keeps people pulling the lever), to keep teens opening the app again and again. Meta denies it built these features specifically to cause harm.

The money at stake is almost impossible to picture. The states say penalties could reach $1.4 trillion — for comparison, that is more than the entire yearly economic output of a country like Belgium or Switzerland. Meta pushes back hard on that number, arguing that if it actually loses, the realistic total would be closer to $200 billion. That gap exists because the final penalty depends on exactly which claims a jury believes and how many individual violations they count.

What makes this trial matter beyond the dollar figure is the word precedent — a legal decision that later courts look to when deciding similar cases. If the states win, it could give lawmakers and other state governments a clear legal path to force tech companies to redesign apps aimed at kids, not just here but potentially across the whole industry. If Meta wins, it becomes harder for anyone to argue in court that addictive-feeling app design alone counts as illegal harm.

Meta's side of the story deserves a fair hearing too. The company argues that plenty of adults use the same features without becoming addicted, that parents have tools to limit screen time already, and that blaming app design ignores everything else going on in a teenager's life — school stress, friendships, family, all of it. Meta already tried to delay this trial back on August 10 and a judge said no, so the company is now defending its design choices in open court, in front of a jury that will decide who is right.

Words worth knowing
  • Attorney general — The top government lawyer for a state, who can bring lawsuits on behalf of everyone who lives there.
  • Infinite scroll — A feed design with no natural stopping point, so a user can keep swiping through content forever.
  • Precedent — A legal decision that later courts look at and often follow when they face a similar case.
