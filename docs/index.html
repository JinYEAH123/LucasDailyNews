A Government Office Tries to Stop an Election Before It Even Happens

In the state of Alaska, people are about to vote for a U.S. Senator. But before anyone has even voted, a powerful government office called the Justice Department has asked a judge to maybe block the result. They say two candidates have such similar names that voters might get confused and pick the wrong one.

Why this mattersImagine if, before your class election for class president even finished, the principal said the winner might not count because two kids had similar names. You would wonder if that was really about being fair, or about someone not liking who might win. This case matters because it asks a big question: who gets to decide if your vote counted?
Read the whole story

Alaska is about to hold an election for one of its two seats in the U.S. Senate, which is part of the group that makes laws for the whole country. The current senator, Dan Sullivan, is running again. But there is another candidate with a very similar name running too.

Nine days before anyone even voted, the U.S. Justice Department filed a lawsuit. They asked a court to stop the state from officially counting the results if Sullivan loses. Their reason was that voters might mix up the two similar names and pick the wrong person by mistake.

Normally, states decide their own elections, and the U.S. Constitution, the country's rulebook, gives that job to the states and to Congress, not to the president's government. Many lawyers and election experts say this lawsuit breaks that rule and have asked a judge to throw it out completely.

The attorney general, who is the person in charge of the Justice Department, said on television that his office was just doing research. But because this election could help decide which political party controls the Senate, many people worry this is really about winning, not about confused voters.

The Justice Department Is Trying to Block an Election Result Before Anyone Has Voted

Nine days before Alaska's U.S. Senate election, the Justice Department filed a lawsuit asking a federal court to stop the state from certifying its results — but only if Republican incumbent Dan Sullivan loses. The department says a similarly named independent candidate could confuse voters and skew the count. Election-law scholars and Democratic lawyers call the move unconstitutional, since the Constitution gives states, not the executive branch, the power to run and certify their own elections.

Why this mattersWhichever side is right, the case tests a basic rule most people take for granted: that elections are decided by voters and certified by states, not pre-emptively contested by whoever happens to run the federal government at the time. If a court allows this kind of lawsuit to proceed, future administrations of either party could use the same tactic whenever a close race doesn't go their way — which would change how much anyone can trust an election result to actually stick.
Read the whole story

The lawsuit, filed by the U.S. Department of Justice, asks a federal court to block Alaska from certifying its Senate election results if Sen. Dan Sullivan, the Republican incumbent, loses his re-election bid. The department points to a second candidate running as an independent whose name closely resembles another candidate's, arguing this could cause enough 'voter confusion' to distort the outcome. The lawsuit was filed before a single vote had been cast — the election is still nine days away as of the filing.

To understand why this is unusual, it helps to know who normally controls this process. The U.S. Constitution gives states the authority to run and certify their own elections, with Congress playing a limited role afterward in counting results. The Justice Department is part of the executive branch — the part of government led by the president — and has no constitutional role in deciding whether a state's election results are valid. Legal experts describe this lawsuit as the first time a sitting administration's DOJ has tried to pre-emptively block a specific state certification before the vote even happens.

The 'voter confusion' argument centers on the second, similarly named candidate running as an independent, which the DOJ claims could pull votes away from Sullivan without voters realizing it. Critics note that this argument only produces a lawsuit if Sullivan loses — if he wins, the same alleged confusion presumably wouldn't matter enough to challenge. That timing is part of why Democratic lawyers and several election-law scholars, including some who don't usually agree on much, see the suit as a backup plan rather than a genuine concern about ballot clarity.

Attorney General Todd Blanche defended the lawsuit on CBS's Face the Nation on Sunday, October 25th, saying the department was 'doing our own research under the law.' He did not detail what specific legal violation the department believes occurred, beyond the voter-confusion claim already in the filing. The statement left open exactly what evidence, if any, supports the idea that the naming overlap would meaningfully change the outcome.

Democratic lawyers have asked the judge to dismiss the case outright, arguing it has no basis in law and that allowing it to proceed would hand the executive branch a tool to contest any election it might lose. Several election-law scholars, writing independently of either party, have said the same thing: nothing in the Constitution or federal election law gives the DOJ authority to block a state's certification before the state has even finished voting and counting. The case remains unresolved, and the outcome could matter well beyond Alaska, since the race could help determine which party controls the U.S. Senate.

Words worth knowing
  • Certification — The official process by which a state confirms and finalizes election results, making them legally binding.
  • Department of Justice (DOJ) — The federal agency that enforces U.S. law and represents the government in court, typically led by officials appointed by the sitting president.
  • Incumbent — A politician who currently holds an office and is running in an election to keep it.

    It's reasonable to focus here

    • Raising the issue only nine days before the election, and only in a scenario where Sullivan loses, suggests the concern is about the outcome rather than about voters.
    • If ballot confusion were the real worry, the DOJ could have flagged it when the second candidate first filed to run, long before the vote neared.

A Church Will Pay $800 Million to People It Failed to Protect

A big Catholic church organization in New York has agreed to pay $800 million. The money will go to about 1,300 people who say adults working for the church hurt them when they were children. The case took more than seven years to settle.

Why this mattersThis story is about grown-ups whose job is to keep kids safe, like teachers, coaches, and religious leaders. It shows that even big, respected organizations can be held responsible when they fail at that job. It also shows that speaking up, even many years later, can lead to real consequences.
Read the whole story

The Archdiocese of New York is a large Catholic organization that runs many churches and schools. About 1,300 people said that adults who worked there hurt them a long time ago, when they were children. That kind of harm is called abuse.

The people who were hurt took the church to court. That process took over seven years. Now the church has agreed to pay $800 million instead of going through a trial. This is called a settlement.

The money will come from the church's own funds, insurance payments, and other sources. It is the second-biggest payment like this ever made by a Catholic organization in the United States. Only one other, in Los Angeles in 2024, was bigger.

Lawyers for the people who were hurt say this is a moment that was long overdue. Church officials say they hope it helps people heal. The settlement does not end similar cases against other Catholic organizations elsewhere in the country.

Words worth knowing
  • archdiocese — A large group of Catholic churches in one area, led by a senior leader called an archbishop.
  • settlement — An agreement to end a legal dispute, often by paying money, instead of having a trial.

New York's Catholic Church Agrees to Pay $800 Million Over Decades of Abuse Claims

The Archdiocese of New York has agreed to pay $800 million to settle claims from about 1,300 people who say they were sexually abused by priests and other church workers over many decades. The deal ends more than seven years of lawsuits and is the second-largest payout ever made by a Catholic diocese in the United States. It will be funded using church property, savings, and insurance money.

Why this mattersThis settlement is part of a pattern playing out across American institutions — churches, schools, youth organisations — where survivors are using changed laws to seek accountability decades after the fact. It raises a question that applies far beyond one church: when an institution you trust lets you down badly, what does it actually owe you, and is money ever enough to answer that?
Read the whole story

For more than seven years, roughly 1,300 people have been suing the Archdiocese of New York, the Catholic Church organisation that runs parishes, schools, and charities across the New York City area, over sexual abuse they say happened as far back as the 1940s. This week that fight ended with an $800 million settlement, an agreement to pay money to close the lawsuits without the archdiocese being found guilty in court. It's the second-largest payment a US Catholic diocese has ever agreed to, behind only the $880 million deal the Archdiocese of Los Angeles reached last year.

Most of these claims only became possible to file because New York changed its law. For a long time, a rule called the statute of limitations blocked people from suing over something that happened too many years ago — the idea being that evidence fades and memories blur with time. In 2019, New York passed the Child Victims Act, which opened a temporary 'lookback window': a stretch of time during which people could sue over childhood abuse no matter how long ago it occurred, even if the usual deadline had passed decades earlier. Thousands of cases against the church, scouting organisations, and other institutions flooded in during that window, and this settlement resolves the largest single batch of them.

Paying $800 million is not as simple as writing one cheque. The archdiocese says the money will come from a mix of its own savings and property, insurance payouts, and other financial arrangements — not from money currently being used to run individual parishes' day-to-day religious services, according to officials involved in the talks. Even so, a sum this large is likely to mean selling some property or restructuring how certain schools and charities are funded, since the archdiocese's total resources are not unlimited.

The two sides describe the deal differently, and both framings matter. Lawyers for the survivors call it long overdue — proof, after years of denial and delay by church officials in earlier decades, that the people harmed are finally being taken seriously. The archdiocese describes the settlement as part of a 'healing' process and points to changes it has made since the scandal broke nationally in the early 2000s, including background checks and reporting requirements for anyone working with children. Both things can be true at once: a settlement can be a genuine institutional reckoning and still arrive only because survivors forced the issue through courts.

What this deal does not do is close the broader story. Other dioceses around the country, along with Catholic religious orders that run their own separate finances, still face abuse lawsuits of their own, and some have filed for bankruptcy protection while negotiating settlements. Criminal charges are rare in these cases, too — many of the accused are dead, and for the rest, criminal statutes of limitations (separate and usually stricter than the civil ones changed by laws like the Child Victims Act) often still block prosecution no matter how clear the evidence.

Words worth knowing
  • Archdiocese — A large district of the Catholic Church, covering many parishes, schools, and charities in one region, led by a senior bishop called an archbishop.
  • Settlement — An agreement to end a lawsuit by paying money, reached without a court formally ruling on whether the accused side broke the law.
  • Lookback window — A temporary change in the law that lets people sue over abuse that happened long ago, even though a normal time limit would usually stop them.

  1. When an institution owes an enormous settlement, should survivors be paid in full right away, even if that means cutting money from schools and charities that serve people today who had nothing to do with the abuse?

The Company That Makes AI Chips Is Also Lending It Billions of Dollars — And People Are Starting to Worry

Nvidia makes the computer chips that power most AI programs. But Nvidia is also lending huge sums of money to companies that build the giant computer warehouses AI needs to run. Now some of those borrowing companies look shaky, and investors are getting nervous about the whole setup.

Why this mattersLots of families have savings for retirement or college tucked into the stock market, and AI companies are now a big part of that market. If this AI spending is built on too much borrowed money and people suddenly lose confidence, stock prices could fall for all sorts of companies, not just AI ones. It's a reminder that exciting new technology doesn't automatically mean quick, guaranteed profits for everyone involved.
Read the whole story

Imagine a bike company that doesn't just sell bikes. It also lends money to bike shops so they can buy even more bikes to sell. That's a bit like what Nvidia is doing. Nvidia makes the special chips that let AI chatbots and other AI programs work. It has also been lending billions of dollars to companies that build data centers, giant warehouses packed with computers that run AI.

Some of the companies borrowing this money are nicknamed 'neoclouds.' Lately they've looked shaky. Their stock prices have dropped, and the cost of borrowing more money has gone up for them, a bit like a bank saying, 'We're not sure you'll pay us back, so we're charging extra just in case.'

At the same time, a financial expert looked at how much money AI is actually making for companies. He found that a small group of giant tech companies, nicknamed the 'Magnificent Seven,' are earning much more money per worker than before. But the other 493 companies in a famous list of big American businesses, called the S&P 500, haven't made any extra money from AI at all.

Put together, these two signs have people asking a worried question. Is all this AI spending a bubble, something that looks huge and solid but could pop fast if people stop believing in it? Nvidia hasn't said much publicly about these worries yet.

Words worth knowing
  • data center — A giant warehouse full of powerful computers that run AI programs and use huge amounts of electricity.
  • bubble — When prices or excitement grow very fast, often fueled by borrowed money, and can suddenly crash if people stop believing in it.

Nvidia Isn't Just Selling the Picks and Shovels of the AI Boom — It's Lending the Money to Buy Them Too

Nvidia makes the chips that power almost every major AI system in the world, which has made it the most valuable company on the planet. Less noticed is that Nvidia has also become one of the AI industry's biggest lenders, pouring money into the very companies that buy its chips. This week, cracks appeared in that arrangement: shares in several of those borrower companies fell, the cost of their debt rose, and a prominent Wall Street economist published data suggesting that outside a handful of giant tech firms, AI has produced no measurable financial payoff at all.

Why this mattersIf you or your parents have money in a pension fund, a 401(k), or almost any ordinary stock market index fund, you already own a slice of this story — the Magnificent Seven tech companies now make up a huge share of the overall stock market's value. A serious wobble in AI financing wouldn't just hit tech investors; it could ripple through retirement accounts and household savings far beyond Silicon Valley.
Read the whole story

For the past two years, Nvidia's business has looked almost too simple: it makes the specialised chips that train and run AI systems, and everyone building AI — from OpenAI to Amazon to Chinese tech giants — needs as many of those chips as they can get. But Nvidia has quietly built a second business alongside the first. It invests in and sometimes lends to the data-center operators and AI labs that are its own customers, helping them afford the enormous computing setups that run on Nvidia hardware. The arrangement helps Nvidia in two ways: it locks in demand for its chips, and it gives Nvidia a financial stake in its customers' success.

A large chunk of this financing has flowed to so-called 'neoclouds' — newer companies that exist almost entirely to rent out AI computing power, built from warehouses full of Nvidia chips, to AI labs that don't want to build their own data centers. These companies have grown explosively, but mostly by borrowing heavily rather than using their own cash, betting that demand for AI computing will keep rising fast enough to cover the debt. It's a bit like a landlord taking out a huge mortgage to build an apartment block before confirming anyone will actually rent the units — reasonable if demand keeps growing, risky if it slows.

This week brought the first real test of that bet. Shares in several neocloud companies dropped, and the yield on their bonds — essentially the extra interest rate investors demand to lend them money — rose. A rising bond yield is a signal that investors see more risk of not getting their money back, which makes borrowing more expensive at exactly the moment these companies need to keep borrowing to build more data centers. None of this means a neocloud has defaulted on its debt, but it's the kind of warning light that tends to appear before trouble, not after.

Adding to the unease, Torsten Slok, chief economist at the investment firm Apollo Global Management, published figures arguing that AI has delivered what he called a 'zero payoff' for corporate America outside the tech industry itself. He pointed to revenue per worker — a rough measure of how much money each employee is generating for a company — which has jumped sharply for the 'Magnificent Seven,' the nickname for the seven giant tech firms (including Nvidia, Microsoft, and Google's parent Alphabet) that dominate the AI buildout. For the other 493 companies in the S&P 500, the stock index that tracks America's largest public companies, that same measure has barely moved.

Put together, the two stories feed the same worry: that an enormous amount of money — much of it borrowed — is being poured into AI infrastructure on the assumption that profits will eventually catch up, while the evidence that they're catching up outside a tiny circle of tech giants is thin. Nvidia has not responded in detail to the reporting on its financing exposure, and it's worth saying plainly that nobody knows yet whether this is the early tremor of a serious correction or just a rough patch in an otherwise sound expansion. Markets have survived scares like this before without the underlying technology turning out to be fake; they have also, at other times, been the first sign that a boom had run ahead of reality.

Words worth knowing

  • bond yield — The extra interest a company must pay to borrow money by selling bonds; a rising yield usually means investors see the loan as riskier.
  • Magnificent Seven — A nickname for the seven giant U.S. tech companies — including Nvidia, Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet, Meta, and Tesla — that dominate the stock market and the AI buildout.

  1. Is it risky for a company to lend money to the very customers who buy its products?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    It's fine

  1. Is the massive, debt-fueled spending on AI infrastructure a smart long-term investment or a bubble waiting to burst?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    This is a bubble

    • Revenue outside the handful of top tech firms hasn't moved, suggesting the spending isn't yet producing real economic value for most companies.
    • Much of the buildout is financed with borrowed money rather than cash, meaning losses could spread quickly through credit markets if confidence drops.
    • Past transformative technologies, like railroads and the early internet, were genuinely important but still produced financial bubbles and crashes along the way.

    This is necessary infrastructure investment

    • Major technological shifts — electricity, railroads, the internet — have always required huge upfront spending before the broader payoff became visible.
    • The companies at the center of this buildout, including Nvidia, are already extremely profitable, not speculative startups with no revenue.

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    It's a troubling conflict of interest

    • Nvidia profits whenever customers buy more chips, giving it an incentive to keep financing even risky borrowers just to sustain demand.
    • If a financed company fails, Nvidia risks losing both the loan and a customer, creating hidden risk that outside investors can't easily see.
    • Money that flows from Nvidia to a customer and back to Nvidia as chip purchases can make real demand for AI look stronger than it actually is.

    It's a normal and unremarkable business practice

    • Vendor financing is extremely common — equipment makers, carmakers, and other tech companies have long helped customers afford expensive purchases.
    • Nvidia's balance sheet is strong enough to absorb losses from a few failed bets without serious damage to the company itself.
    • Nvidia choosing to help fund data centers can be read as genuine confidence in future demand, not a sign of desperation to prop up fragile customers.
