Three Powerful Leaders Will Meet Together for the First Time Ever

On November 19, something that has never happened before will happen. The leaders of the United States, China, and Russia will sit down together at the same table. President Trump, President Xi Jinping, and President Putin will meet in a Chinese city called Shenzhen.

Why this mattersNuclear weapons are so powerful that just a few of them could hurt huge numbers of people. When the leaders in charge of almost all of these weapons talk to each other, it can make the whole world a little safer, or sometimes show new tensions between them. Even though this is happening far away, decisions these three leaders make can affect how safe everyone on Earth feels.

These three leaders run the three countries that own almost all of the world's nuclear weapons. A nuclear weapon is the most powerful bomb ever invented, much stronger than any other weapon humans have made.

The meeting will happen at a big event called the APEC summit, where many countries' leaders gather to talk. This is the first time Trump, Xi, and Putin have ever all met together at once.

Trump says he wants to talk about cutting down the number of nuclear weapons each country has. He also plans to bring up the war happening in Iran. But he is not expected to talk about the war in Ukraine during this meeting, even though that war is still going on.

Nobody has said exactly how the meeting will work. It might be a quick side chat, or it could be a longer, more formal sit-down. No country has released a plan or list of topics yet, so a lot is still unknown.

Trump, Xi and Putin to Sit Down Together for the First Time — With Ukraine Off the Table

On November 19th, the leaders of the United States, China and Russia will meet together for the first time ever, on the edges of a major economic summit in Shenzhen, China. The White House says President Trump wants to talk about cutting down nuclear weapons stockpiles and also plans to raise the situation in Iran. What he reportedly won't discuss, despite sitting across from the man who ordered it, is Russia's ongoing war in Ukraine.

Why this mattersThe choices these three leaders make about nuclear weapons affect everyone, because a serious miscalculation between nuclear-armed states is one of the few risks that could touch the whole planet at once. It also matters that a war killing people right now can get quietly set aside at a summit meant to look historic — that's a real tension in how diplomacy gets done, not a side issue.
Read the whole story

Three men who each control one of the world's largest stockpiles of nuclear weapons have never before sat at the same table. That changes this week. Donald Trump, Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin will hold what's being called a trilateral meeting — a conversation between exactly three parties, as opposed to the usual one-on-one summits — on the sidelines of APEC, a yearly gathering of 21 Pacific Rim economies that includes the US, China, Japan and most of Southeast Asia. 'Sidelines' is the key word: the big official APEC summit will be happening anyway, and this three-way sit-down is being slotted in around it, near the end of Trump's broader trip through Asia.

Nobody has released an agenda, and none of the three governments have said whether this will be a brief side conversation in a hallway or a formal session with aides and interpreters around a table. What officials have confirmed is the headline subject: reducing nuclear arsenals, meaning the actual number of nuclear warheads each country keeps ready to use. The US and Russia hold by far the largest stockpiles in the world, built up during the Cold War and only partly reduced since by treaties that have mostly expired or lapsed. China's arsenal is much smaller but growing quickly. Getting all three in the same room to even discuss the subject, let alone agree on anything, has never happened before.

What's missing from the announced agenda is just as notable as what's in it. Trump is expected to bring up the war in Iran, but officials say he does not plan to discuss Russia's war in Ukraine during the trilateral session — a war now in its fourth year, which has killed or wounded hundreds of thousands of people and which Putin started. Leaving it off the agenda doesn't make it disappear from the room; it just means the one person at the table who could end the war tomorrow won't be pressed to do so in front of the other two. Whether that's a deliberate bit of diplomacy or an awkward dodge is something reasonable people read differently.

Coverage of the meeting varies depending on who's doing the covering. Regional outlets, including some funded directly by governments in the region, have framed the summit partly around whether Trump will use it to take a tougher public line against Beijing, given separate trade and security tensions between the US and China. That's a plausible angle, but it's worth being clear that it's a framing added by commentators and state-backed media, not something the White House itself has confirmed as the meeting's purpose. State-funded outlets have their own interests in shaping how a story like this gets told, which is a reason to read their framing as one perspective rather than as the official account.

What makes this moment unusual isn't just the content of the talks — it's the format. Trump has built a reputation for favoring direct, personal dealmaking over the slower, more scripted diplomacy that usually surrounds nuclear policy. Putting Xi and Putin in the same room, informally, on the edge of a trade summit rather than at a dedicated arms-control conference, is itself a kind of experiment in how major-power diplomacy can work. Whether it produces anything concrete — a joint statement, a working group, even just an agreed next meeting — won't be clear until the day itself, since no format or outcome has been locked in publicly by any of the three sides.

Measles Is Spreading Again — America Loses a 25-Year Record

For the first time since the year 2000, the United States is no longer officially free of ongoing measles spread. Health experts say the disease has been spreading nonstop in some communities since 2025 and hasn't stopped. That breaks a record the country had held for 25 years.

Why this mattersIf you've had your shots, you're very likely protected from measles yourself. But babies too young for the vaccine, and kids with certain illnesses who can't get it, depend on the people around them being vaccinated to stay safe. When vaccination rates drop in a community, it becomes easier for outbreaks like this one to start and keep going.
Read the whole story

Measles is a disease that spreads through coughing and sneezing. It can give you a fever, a cough, and a rash of red spots all over your body. In the year 2000, the United States earned something called 'elimination status.' That means health officials tracked the disease for over a year and found no ongoing spread happening on its own inside the country.

Now that record is broken. In 2024, the US had its worst year in three decades, with 2,242 measles cases — about enough kids to fill 80 classrooms. But in 2026, that number has more than doubled to 5,412 cases, enough to fill nearly 200 classrooms. About 590 of those people got sick enough to go to the hospital. Health officials have confirmed at least 6 deaths from measles, though news reports tracking state records say the real number may be closer to 10.

Doctors say the outbreaks are happening mostly in places where fewer children get a shot called the MMR vaccine, which protects against measles, mumps, and rubella. When most kids in a community are vaccinated, the disease has nowhere to spread — this is sometimes called herd protection, because the group protects its weakest members. But where vaccination rates have dropped, the virus finds unprotected kids and keeps moving from one to the next.

Losing elimination status doesn't mean measles is suddenly everywhere in America. Most towns still have no cases at all. But it does mean the chain of spreading never fully broke, somewhere in the country, for more than a year — something that hadn't happened since before most kids' parents were born.

Words worth knowing
  • elimination status — A title a country earns when a disease stops spreading on its own inside its borders for more than a year, even if a few sick travelers still bring cases in.
  • MMR vaccine — A shot given to children that trains the body to fight off measles, mumps, and rubella before they ever catch the real diseases.

The US Just Lost a Disease-Free Status It Held for 25 Years

The CDC has confirmed that the United States no longer qualifies as having eliminated measles, a status the country held since 2000. Case counts this year have more than doubled last year's 30-year high, driven by outbreaks that started in 2025 and never stopped spreading. Officials say the core problem is simple: not enough children are getting vaccinated in certain communities.

Why this mattersMeasles is one of the most contagious diseases known, and it mostly hospitalizes and kills young children, so a drop in vaccination rates in even a few communities can bring back a disease most families haven't had to think about in decades. This story is also a window into how public health statistics actually get made — official counts can lag real events by days or weeks, which matters whenever you're trying to judge how serious something is in real time.
Read the whole story

For 25 years, the United States held something called 'measles elimination status.' That doesn't mean zero measles ever — it means no chain of infection inside the country lasted longer than 12 months. Cases could still arrive from travelers, but public health systems were strong enough, and enough people were vaccinated, to snuff out any spread before it took hold. That streak just broke. The CDC confirmed this week that ongoing outbreaks, some tracing back to 2025, never actually stopped, which under the technical definition means the US no longer qualifies as having eliminated the disease.

The numbers tell the story of how fast this escalated. In 2024, the US recorded 2,242 measles cases, already the highest total in three decades. In 2026 so far, that number has more than doubled to 5,412, with over 590 people hospitalized. About a third of this year's cases are directly linked to the outbreaks that began last year and simply kept finding new unvaccinated people to infect, town by town, rather than burning out.

The CDC officially attributes at least six deaths to measles this year. But news organizations tracking state health department reports — including newly confirmed deaths in Wisconsin and Pennsylvania reported just this past Friday — put the real total closer to 10. That gap exists because state and federal reporting systems update on different timelines; a death might be confirmed by a local medical examiner weeks before it shows up in the CDC's official tally. It's a reminder that even official statistics are often provisional, especially during a fast-moving outbreak, and two correct-sounding numbers can disagree simply because they were counted at different moments.

Public health officials point to one cause above others: declining vaccination rates for MMR, the combined vaccine that protects against measles, mumps, and rubella, given routinely to young children. Measles is extraordinarily contagious — far more so than flu or COVID — so protecting a community requires roughly 95% of people to be immune, whether through vaccination or prior infection. When vaccination rates dip even a little in a specific school, neighborhood, or religious community, that threshold can slip below the level needed to stop transmission, and the virus finds the gap.

Losing elimination status won't change much for most Americans overnight — hospitals still know how to treat measles, and vaccines still work exactly as well as they did last year. What it signals is structural: a public health achievement built over 25 years through sustained vaccination campaigns has started to erode, and reversing it will take the same kind of sustained effort, community by community, rather than a single policy fix.

Words worth knowing
  • Measles elimination status — An official designation meaning no uninterrupted chain of measles transmission has lasted more than 12 months within a country, even if isolated cases still occur.
  • MMR vaccine — A single vaccine given to young children that protects against measles, mumps, and rubella together.
  • Herd immunity threshold — The percentage of a population that needs to be immune to a disease — through vaccination or past infection — to stop it from spreading; for measles this is unusually high, around 95%.

Nvidia's CEO Resigns Over a Spy Allegation — and Washington's Chip War Loses Its Biggest Critic

Jensen Huang, the man who built Nvidia into the company that makes the chips powering most of the world's AI, resigned on Thursday. Days earlier he had admitted to a romantic relationship with a woman that unnamed US officials and his own company allege — but have not publicly proven — was working for Chinese intelligence. The resignation lands at the exact moment Washington is deciding whether to tighten the rules on selling advanced chips to China, and Huang had been the loudest voice arguing against doing so.

Why this mattersThe chip inside your phone, laptop, or any AI tool you've used was likely designed by Nvidia or a company competing directly with it — so decisions made in this fight shape what gets built, how fast, and how much it costs. It's also a clean example of how national security, corporate interest, and unverified allegations can tangle together in ways that are genuinely hard to untangle, even for the officials involved.
Read the whole story

Jensen Huang didn't just run Nvidia — he was, in large part, the reason it became the most valuable chipmaker on Earth. Under him, Nvidia's processors went from powering video games to powering nearly every major AI system, from chatbots to self-driving cars to the data centers behind them. That made Huang one of the most powerful individual voices in American tech policy, and he used that voice loudly and specifically against export controls — US government rules that restrict which advanced technologies, including Nvidia's most powerful AI chips, American companies are allowed to sell to China. He argued the restrictions mostly just pushed China to build its own chips faster, while costing American companies billions in lost sales.

The allegation that ended his tenure is murkier than the resignation itself. Huang acknowledged an affair with a woman whom officials — speaking anonymously, not on the record — and Nvidia itself have alleged was an operative for Chinese intelligence, meaning someone working, openly or secretly, to gather information for the Chinese government. No public evidence has been produced to confirm this beyond those statements. It's entirely possible the allegation is true, entirely possible it's exaggerated, and entirely possible officials who wanted Huang gone — for policy reasons having nothing to do with the affair — found this a convenient way to make it happen. All three readings are circulating, and none has been settled.

What's clearer is the effect. With Huang gone, the chip industry loses its single most effective and well-resourced opponent of tighter export rules. Congress has kept up pressure this week, with lawmakers demanding more detail on what the security risk actually was. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick — whose department enforces export controls — and other administration officials are reported to be weighing how far new restrictions should go, now that the executive who fought hardest against them is off the board.

This sets up a fight with consequences well beyond one company's leadership. If Washington tightens the rules further, it could accelerate something already underway: China's own drive toward semiconductor self-sufficiency, building its own advanced chip factories so it no longer needs American technology at all. Export controls were partly designed to slow China down; a decade of them has also given Chinese companies and the government a very strong reason to stop depending on the US entirely. Whether that trade-off has been worth it is one of the most argued questions in US-China policy.

Underneath the geopolitics sits an uncomfortable fact: a huge swing in US chip policy may trace back to an allegation that has never been independently verified, built on statements from the very company whose business interests are most affected by what happens next.

  • semiconductor self-sufficiency — A country's effort to design and manufacture its own advanced computer chips so it no longer depends on other nations for them.
  • intelligence operative — A person working, secretly or openly, to gather information for a government's spy agency.
