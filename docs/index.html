Russian Missiles Hit Poland for the First Time — And Nobody Agrees on What It Means

Overnight, Russia fired one of its largest missile barrages yet at Ukraine — and some of those munitions landed inside Poland, a member of the NATO military alliance. It is the first time in nearly four years of war that Russian weapons have struck NATO territory directly. Poland's government called it deliberate. President Trump called it a mistake.

Why this mattersNATO's entire deterrent power rests on everyone believing that an attack on one member really would bring in all of them — if that promise ever looks optional, it stops working, for Poland and for every other member, including wherever you live if your country is one of them. How leaders decide to describe this moment — accident or test — will shape whether the war stays contained to Ukraine or edges closer to involving the alliance directly.

Russia has been firing missiles and drones at Ukraine for almost four years, but this barrage was unusually large, and for the first time, wreckage or munitions came down on the Polish side of the border. Poland is not just any neighbor — it belongs to NATO, an alliance of 32 countries, including the United States, that have promised to treat an attack on one member as an attack on all of them. That promise is the whole point of the alliance: it is supposed to make attacking any member too dangerous to attempt.

Poland's Prime Minister, Donald Tusk, said the strike looked like a deliberate provocation, not an accident. NATO's leadership called it a 'grave violation' of allied airspace — serious language that opened the door to two different emergency procedures. One is 'Article 4 consultations,' where member countries sit down together because a member feels its security is threatened, but nobody is obligated to do anything beyond talk. The other, far more serious, is 'Article 5,' the collective-defense clause — the one that could, in theory, pull the entire alliance into direct conflict with Russia.

Hours after the strike, President Trump held a nearly two-hour press conference at the White House. He had just hosted a trilateral summit — a three-way meeting — with Russia's Vladimir Putin and China's Xi Jinping, and he spent part of the briefing defending that relationship and praising his recent trip to Asia. Asked about the strike on Poland, he called it 'a mistake,' suggesting the missiles had simply gone astray during the broader bombing of Ukraine rather than being aimed at NATO on purpose.

Tusk publicly disagreed with that characterization, and the disagreement matters enormously. If the strike was accidental, it is a dangerous but understandable side effect of a huge barrage flying close to a border. If it was deliberate, it is Russia testing whether NATO's collective-defense promise is real — probing to see what the alliance will actually do if provoked. Those two readings point toward completely different responses: one calls for an apology and tighter safety margins, the other calls for the alliance to show it means what it says.

For now, no country has invoked Article 5, and it is unclear whether even Article 4 consultations will happen. The episode has left NATO in an uncomfortable position: it must decide how seriously to treat the first direct hit on its own territory in this war, while its most powerful member's president is publicly downplaying the incident just hours after sitting down with the man whose military fired the missiles.

Words worth knowing
  • NATO — A military alliance of 32 countries, including the US and most of Europe, built around the promise that an attack on one member is treated as an attack on all.
  • Article 5 — The part of NATO's founding treaty that says an armed attack on one member country is considered an attack on every member, potentially triggering a collective military response.
  • Barrage — A large, concentrated burst of missile or artillery fire, often many weapons launched at once rather than a single strike.

AI Stocks Take a Big Tumble on Wall Street

Last week, the prices of lots of American companies' stocks dropped fast. Stock is a tiny piece of a company that people can buy and sell, like a trading card for a business. The drop was worst for companies that make or use artificial intelligence (AI), the computer technology that can write, draw, and answer questions like a person.

Why this mattersLots of grown-ups have retirement savings or other money tucked into the stock market, often without even picking the stocks themselves. When AI company stocks drop this fast, it can shrink those savings overnight. It also matters because AI is in things you might use already, like apps that answer questions or help with homework, and this shows people aren't sure how much that technology is really worth yet.
Read the whole story

Imagine a toy that everyone suddenly wants to buy. Its price goes way up, even before anyone knows if it's actually as good as people hope. That's what happened with AI companies over the last two years. Investors (people who buy stocks hoping to make money) kept paying more and more for them.

Then, last week, many investors got worried. They started asking: are these AI companies really going to make enough real money to be worth all this? One company, Nvidia, which makes special computer chips for AI, saw its stock price drop by about 14% in a single week. That's its worst week since 2008.

A brand new AI company called Anthropic had just sold its first stock two days earlier, for a stunning $1.76 trillion in total value. To picture that number: if you shared it equally among every single person in the United States, each person would get about $5,000. After the selling started, Anthropic's stock dropped too.

The worry spread around the world, hitting stock markets in Japan, South Korea, and Europe. Nobody knows for sure yet if this is just a short scare, or if it's the beginning of AI companies losing their shine for a longer time.

AI Stocks Crash Just Days After the Biggest Market Debut in History

The US stock market just had its worst week since the pandemic crash of March 2020. The main trigger was a sudden wave of doubt about artificial intelligence companies — the very companies that have driven almost all of the market's gains for the past two years. Nvidia, the chipmaker at the centre of the AI boom, lost nearly 14% of its value in a single week.

Why this mattersIf you, a parent, or a grandparent has money in a pension fund, retirement account, or index fund, you very likely own some of these AI companies without ever having chosen them individually — they now make up a huge slice of the overall stock market. A sustained AI downturn wouldn't just hurt tech executives; it could shrink retirement savings, slow hiring at companies that supply or service the AI industry, and test whether the technology everyone has been promised is actually ready to pay for itself.
Read the whole story

On Friday, the S&P 500 — a index tracking 500 of the largest US companies, widely used as a stand-in for "the stock market" as a whole — fell 3.7% in one day and officially entered what traders call a correction: a drop of 10% or more from its recent peak. The Dow Jones Industrial Average, an older and narrower index of 30 big companies, fell nearly 950 points. The Nasdaq, which is heavy with technology stocks, plunged 5.28% and is now down for the year overall.

The panic centred on artificial intelligence. Nvidia, whose chips power most of the world's AI systems and which briefly became the most valuable company on Earth, fell about 13.8% over the week — its worst week since 2008, the year of the global financial crisis. AMD, a rival chipmaker, fell more than 13%. And Anthropic, the AI company behind the chatbot Claude, had gone public on the stock market just two days earlier in the largest IPO (initial public offering — when a private company first sells shares to the public) ever recorded, valuing it at $1.76 trillion. Its stock then fell another 8.5 to 9%, an unusually fast reversal for a company that had barely started trading.

The worry spreading through markets is simple to state and hard to answer: have investors been paying for a future that hasn't arrived yet? Over the past two years, AI companies have attracted enormous sums of money on the bet that the technology will transform entire industries and generate huge profits. Building that future has required spending hundreds of billions of dollars on computer chips, data centres, and electricity — much of it before anyone has proven the profits will actually show up. When a widely read analysis questioned whether some of that spending made financial sense, investors who had been assuming the good times would continue suddenly started selling instead.

The sell-off did not stay in the United States. Japan's SoftBank, an investment firm with large AI holdings, fell 14.5%. South Korea's main stock index, the Kospi, dropped 5.2%. European markets slid too. This matters because it shows how tightly connected the world's financial markets have become around a handful of American technology companies — when confidence in Nvidia wavers, pension funds and savings accounts on the other side of the planet feel it within hours.

Nobody agrees on what happens next. Some analysts argue this is a healthy, overdue correction — a moment where reality catches up with hype, prices reset to something more reasonable, and the AI industry continues growing from firmer ground. Others think it's the first crack in something much bigger: a bubble, where prices rise mainly because everyone expects them to keep rising, until something — a disappointing earnings report, a change in interest rates, a skeptical research note — breaks the spell. Both readings agree on the facts; they disagree on what the facts mean for the months ahead.

Words worth knowing
  • Correction — A drop of 10% or more in a stock or market's value from its recent peak, a term traders use to describe a sharp but not catastrophic decline.
  • IPO (initial public offering) — The first time a private company sells shares of itself to the public, allowing anyone to buy ownership in it through the stock market.
  • Valuation — An estimate of what an entire company is worth, usually calculated by multiplying its share price by the total number of shares that exist.

Two Different Proofs Show Up on the Same Day for a 167-Year-Old Math Mystery

Mathematicians have been stuck on one giant puzzle about prime numbers for 167 years — that's older than your great-great-great-grandparents. This week, a mathematician named Levent Alpöge, working with an AI called Claude, said he had solved it. On the very same day, someone else turned up with a completely different solution, written by a human alone with no AI help.

Why this mattersIf either proof holds up, it solves a problem that stumped brilliant people for over a century — the kind of achievement kids study in school forever after. It also matters because it's a test of whether AI can do deep, original thinking, not just answer questions it's seen before, which could change how all kinds of hard problems get solved in the future.
Read the whole story

The puzzle is called the Riemann Hypothesis. It's a guess about prime numbers — numbers like 2, 3, 5, 7, and 11 that can only be divided evenly by themselves and 1. The guess is about exactly how these special numbers are scattered among all the other numbers. Mathematicians have used this guess for over a century, but nobody had ever proven for certain that it was true.

Levent Alpöge works at Anthropic, a company that builds AI. He says he and the AI Claude worked together and found a proof — a step-by-step argument that leaves no doubt a math statement is true. Strangely, another proof of the exact same puzzle appeared from someone else that same day, written entirely by a person with no AI involved.

Now other mathematicians are checking both proofs line by line, including at least one winner of the Fields Medal, which is like the Olympic gold medal of math. By the end of the first day, some news reports said the AI-assisted proof looked almost certainly correct. But nobody has finished the full check yet.

It's still not settled which proof will turn out to be right. It could be one of them, both, or neither. Until mathematicians finish checking every single step, nobody can be completely sure.

Two Rival Proofs Claim to Crack a 167-Year-Old Math Mystery — One With an AI Co-Author

A mathematician working at the AI company Anthropic says he and its chatbot, Claude, have solved the Riemann Hypothesis, a problem that has stumped mathematicians since 1859. On the exact same day, a completely different, entirely human-written proof of the same problem appeared from someone else. Now the world's top mathematicians are racing to check both, and nobody yet knows if either one actually holds up.

Why this mattersIf you've used an AI chatbot for homework help, this is the same basic technology being pushed toward the hardest kind of thinking humans do — original proof, not just summarizing or explaining things already known. Whichever way this resolves, it will shape how much mathematicians, scientists, and eventually employers trust AI as a genuine research partner rather than just a faster search engine.
Read the whole story

The Riemann Hypothesis is a statement about prime numbers — the numbers like 2, 3, 5, 7, 11 that can't be divided evenly by anything except themselves and 1. Primes look scattered and random, but a 19th-century mathematician named Bernhard Riemann proposed a precise rule for how they're distributed, hidden inside a mathematical object called the zeta function. If his hypothesis is true, it would explain something fundamental about why primes show up where they do. It's one of seven "Millennium Prize Problems," with a million-dollar reward for a verified solution, and in 167 years nobody has managed to prove it.

This week, that apparently changed twice in one day. Levent Alpöge, a mathematician employed by Anthropic — the company that makes the AI chatbot Claude — announced a proof he says he produced working together with Claude itself, treating the AI less like a calculator and more like a thinking partner. Hours later, a second, unrelated proof surfaced, written entirely by a human mathematician with no AI involvement at all. Two strangers, using completely different methods, claiming to have cracked the same 167-year-old puzzle on the same day.

That kind of coincidence is unusual enough to make mathematicians suspicious rather than celebratory. A real proof of the Riemann Hypothesis has to survive brutal scrutiny: every single logical step checked by experts who are actively trying to find the flaw, because history is full of proposed proofs that looked solid for days before someone found the hole. At least one Fields Medalist — the holder of what's often called the Nobel Prize of mathematics — has joined the effort to check both claims line by line.

By the end of the first day, some news outlets reported that the AI-assisted proof looked "almost certainly true" based on early checks, though full formal verification — the slow, unglamorous process of confirming every step is airtight — was still underway. That's a meaningfully different thing from confirmed. Mathematics has no shortcuts for this part: a result isn't accepted because it sounds promising or because respected people are excited about it, but because independent experts have failed to break it after genuinely trying.

What makes this episode bigger than one math problem is the Claude angle. AI companies have spent billions of dollars and years of effort trying to show their models can do more than answer questions well — that they can produce genuinely new, correct, rigorous scientific reasoning no human had found before. If Alpöge's proof survives verification, it becomes one of the strongest pieces of evidence yet that an AI model contributed meaningfully to solving a problem that had defeated humans alone for over a century. If it doesn't survive, it becomes a cautionary tale about how easily an impressive-sounding AI collaboration can be mistaken for an actual breakthrough.

Either way, the verification process will likely take weeks or months, not hours. Mathematicians who announce major proofs sometimes wait years for the community to fully agree they're correct — the first proof of Fermat's Last Theorem, a comparably famous problem, took over a year of checking before a hidden gap was found and had to be patched. So right now, the honest answer to "has the Riemann Hypothesis been solved?" is: maybe, by one of two people, or maybe not at all.

  • Fields Medal — An award often called the Nobel Prize of mathematics, given to only a handful of mathematicians every four years.
  • formal verification — The slow process of checking every single logical step of a proof to make sure there are no hidden mistakes or gaps.
