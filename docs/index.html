For 60 days, American and Iranian officials worked under a memorandum — a written agreement to keep talking — meant to end a war between the two countries without a deal being reached. It expired this past Monday with no lasting agreement in place. Almost immediately, oil markets reacted: Brent crude, the price used to set oil costs across Europe and much of the world, rose to $88.85 a barrel, and WTI, the main U.S. benchmark, climbed to $82.42.

The reason oil prices move at all is a narrow strip of water called the Strait of Hormuz, which sits between Iran and Oman. About a fifth of the world's oil passes through it on tankers every single day. If that strait were ever closed or blocked, gas prices could jump almost everywhere, because so much of the world's fuel supply squeezes through this one channel, the way traffic backs up for miles when a single bridge on a busy highway shuts down.

President Trump said Iran wants a deal but won't accept the terms he's offering, and he raised the idea of declaring the Strait of Hormuz U.S. territory — an extraordinary claim, since it borders Iran and Oman, not the United States. He also confirmed something unusual: his administration has a direct backchannel, a private, informal line of communication, with Iran's Revolutionary Guard, the elite military force that answers to Iran's top religious leader rather than its regular government. 'They're good poker players, but they're dying,' Trump said, adding that he has 'no time schedule.' In the same stretch of days, he threatened to bomb Oman — a country that isn't even part of the dispute — if it interfered with the U.S. blockade, a military effort to stop ships from moving in or out of Iranian waters.

Iran did not back down quietly. A senior Iranian official told the news agency Reuters that after talks stalled, the country would shift to a 'fully offensive' military posture and carry out a 'timely and precise' attack. That kind of language, from a government official rather than a random commentator, is taken seriously by intelligence agencies and markets alike, because it suggests a decision has already been made somewhere inside the government.

By the next day, the picture got even murkier. CNN reported Trump saying no talks were actually underway, contradicting the sense of ongoing negotiation from just a day earlier. Meanwhile, officials in the United Arab Emirates said they had detected a 'missile threat.' When world leaders can't even agree publicly on whether diplomacy is happening, it becomes harder for anyone — allies, enemies, or ordinary people trying to plan their lives — to know what happens next.

On Monday, Aug. 17, a 60-day negotiating window the Trump administration had set for reaching a lasting deal with Iran ran out without one. Nothing detonated immediately, but the market reaction was itself a signal: Brent crude, the global oil-price benchmark, climbed to $88.85 a barrel, and the U.S. benchmark, West Texas Intermediate, rose to $82.42. Traders read the expired deadline as another tick upward in the odds of disruption to the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway between Iran and the Arabian Peninsula through which roughly a fifth of the world's oil passes every day.

The memorandum had set out terms meant to end months of on-and-off confrontation between the U.S. and Iran over its nuclear program and its naval activity in the Gulf. Trump said publicly that Iran wants an agreement but won't accept his conditions, without specifying which ones. He also confirmed something unusual for a formal diplomatic standoff: his administration maintains a direct backchannel, an informal line of communication outside normal diplomatic channels, with Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps — the branch of Iran's military that answers to its supreme leader rather than its elected government, and that controls much of the country's missile and naval forces. "They're good poker players, but they're dying," Trump said of Iranian officials, adding he has "no time schedule" for a deal.

He floated an idea that alarmed people who track international law: declaring the Strait of Hormuz U.S. territory. The strait sits in Iranian and Omani waters under international law, not on American ground, and no U.S. president has previously treated a foreign shipping lane as sovereign American soil. It isn't clear whether Trump meant this as literal policy or as a way of signaling that the U.S. Navy would treat any interference with oil shipping there as an attack on the United States itself. Either reading raises the stakes, since forces defending "our territory" operate under different rules of engagement than those merely protecting freedom of navigation.

The threats didn't stop at Iran. Trump warned he would "bomb the shit out of" Oman if it interfered with the American blockade of Iranian shipping — a striking target, since Oman sits on the other side of the strait and has long served as a trusted go-between hosting U.S.-Iran diplomacy. Oman has generally tried to stay neutral, mediating rather than taking sides, which made the threat land as pressure on a country trying to keep the door to talks open rather than on an adversary. On the Iranian side, a senior official told Reuters that with negotiations stalled, Iran would shift to a "fully offensive" military posture and carry out a "timely and precise" strike — language that leaves open both timing and target while committing to some form of retaliation.

By Tuesday, Aug. 18, the picture had shifted again. CNN reported Trump saying no talks were currently underway at all, and that the United Arab Emirates had detected a "missile threat" — a claim without public confirmation of what it referred to or where it originated. The sequence itself is the story: a deadline expiring, threats fired in multiple directions, a reported missile detection, all inside roughly 48 hours. Officials on both sides are making high-stakes public statements faster than anyone outside the room can verify or walk them back.

Courts have sometimes sided with speakers in jawboning cases, reasoning that the government doesn't need to issue a written command if the implicit threat does the same job.
