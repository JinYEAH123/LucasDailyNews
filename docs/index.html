Tech Stocks Tumble as Intel Has Its Worst Day Since 1974 — and Apple Becomes the World's Priciest Company

On Friday, big American tech companies had a rough day on the stock market. Investors got worried that companies like Amazon, Google's parent Alphabet, and the chip maker Intel are spending too much money on artificial intelligence without it paying off yet. Intel's stock price crashed by almost a third in a single day — its worst drop in more than fifty years.

Why this mattersMany things you use every day — search engines, shopping websites, phone apps — come from these very companies, so how they're doing matters for the technology you'll grow up with. It also matters because lots of grown-ups, including parents and grandparents, have savings tied up in the stock market, so a bad day like this can genuinely affect people's futures.
Read the whole story

Imagine a scoreboard that shows how much people think big companies are worth. On Friday, that scoreboard took a big tumble. Two major measures of the US stock market, called the Nasdaq and the S&P 500, had their worst day since June. The reason? Investors got nervous about how much money tech companies are pouring into artificial intelligence (AI) — smart computer programs — and whether all that spending will actually pay off.

Intel, a company that makes the computer chips found inside laptops and phones, had it worst. Its stock price fell by nearly a third in just one day — the sharpest drop since 1974, before most kids' grandparents were even born! That wiped out tens of billions of dollars in value. The US government actually owns a small slice of Intel, about one-tenth of the company. That slice alone lost about $12 billion in value in a single day — roughly enough money to buy 200,000 brand-new cars.

The day before, Alphabet (the company that owns Google) had its own worst single-day stock drop ever, for the same reason: worries about AI spending. In the middle of all this, something interesting happened. Apple, the company that makes iPhones, became the most valuable company on Earth, worth $5.03 trillion. That pushed Nvidia, a chip company that had been on top, down to second place at $4.93 trillion. If you tried to count to a trillion, saying one number every second without stopping, it would take you about 32,000 years.

Here's an important twist: the US economy itself is actually doing fine. Just the day before this stock drop, new numbers showed the economy had grown strongly. So this wasn't a sign that everything is going wrong — it was investors specifically worrying about whether AI spending is smart or risky.

Wall Street's AI Bet Wobbles: Intel Crashes, Apple Overtakes Nvidia

On Friday, three of the world's biggest tech companies — Amazon, Alphabet, and Intel — gave investors reasons to worry about how much money is being poured into artificial intelligence. Stock markets had their worst day since June, Intel's shares fell nearly 30% in a single day, and Apple overtook Nvidia as the world's most valuable company. It was the sharpest sign yet that some investors think the AI spending boom has gotten ahead of itself.

Why this mattersIf you, your parents, or your school has money in a retirement account, pension fund, or index fund, there's a good chance it includes shares of Amazon, Alphabet, Intel, Apple, or Nvidia — meaning swings like this one aren't just abstract numbers on a news ticker. More broadly, this is the first real test of whether the AI boom reshaping jobs, products, and the economy is built on solid ground or on spending that investors are starting to question.

Friday, October 30, 2026, was a rough day to own tech stocks. The Nasdaq Composite, an index that tracks mostly technology companies, fell 2.86%. The S&P 500, a broader index covering 500 large American companies, fell 1.82%. Both numbers represented the worst single day for each index since June. The trigger was simple: Amazon, Alphabet (Google's parent company), and Intel had all just reported their latest earnings, and investors didn't like what they heard about how much these companies are spending on artificial intelligence — and what they're getting back for it.

Intel took the hardest hit. Its stock price collapsed by roughly 29% in a single day, the company's worst drop since 1974, wiping out tens of billions of dollars in value almost instantly. That mattered to more than just Intel's usual shareholders, because the US government owns a 10% stake in the company — a slice of ownership the government took on earlier as part of an effort to keep advanced computer-chip manufacturing based in America. When Intel's stock cratered, the value of that government stake fell by about $12 billion in a single day, meaning American taxpayers effectively lost money on paper right along with everyone else.

Alphabet had already had its own bad day on Thursday, October 29 — its worst single-day stock drop ever as a public company. Like Intel's, it was tied to worries about the cost of AI infrastructure spending: the enormous sums tech companies are pouring into data centers, specialized chips, and the electricity needed to run them, all in a race to build and run powerful AI systems. Investors increasingly want to see proof that this spending is turning into profit, not just promises about the future.

In the middle of the rout, something symbolically striking happened: Apple passed Nvidia to become the world's most valuable company, with Apple's total market value — called its market capitalization, or market cap, the share price multiplied by the number of shares — closing at $5.03 trillion against Nvidia's $4.93 trillion. Nvidia had ridden the AI boom to become the most valuable company on Earth by selling the chips that power AI systems, but on a day when investors were nervous about AI spending, many moved their money toward Apple instead, seeing it as a steadier, less AI-exposed bet. Reports vary on exactly how much Amazon's stock moved that day, with estimates ranging from about 1.4% to nearly 6% down — a reminder that even on a dramatic news day, the numbers reported by different outlets aren't always identical.

What makes this notable is what it isn't about: the broader US economy. Just a day earlier, the government had reported that the economy grew at a strong annualized rate of 3.4% in the third quarter. So this wasn't investors panicking about jobs, prices, or growth generally — it was a much more specific kind of doubt, aimed squarely at whether the hundreds of billions of dollars being spent on AI will pay off as quickly, or as surely, as markets had been betting.

Hackers Steal $6.3 Billion in Just 20 Minutes

Over one weekend in late October, hackers broke into Binance, the world's biggest place to buy and trade cryptocurrency, and stole about $6.3 billion. It took them only about 20 minutes. It is the biggest crypto theft ever recorded, and experts think a hacking group connected to North Korea did it.

Why this mattersMore and more people, maybe even someone in your family, keep money in cryptocurrency instead of a regular bank. This story shows that even a company as big and careful as Binance can lose billions of dollars in minutes if a clever enough hacker gets in. It matters because it makes everyone ask: how do you keep something safe when it only exists as numbers on a computer?
Read the whole story

Imagine a huge digital vault holding billions of dollars in cryptocurrency, a kind of money that only exists on computers, with no coins or paper bills. Binance is like the biggest bank for this kind of money. One weekend, hackers broke in and grabbed about $6.3 billion in about 20 minutes, faster than it takes to watch half a cartoon.

Companies that track stolen crypto say the hackers look like they belong to a group linked to North Korea, a country run by a government that has used hacking before to get money. Binance and these tracking companies worked fast afterward, and managed to freeze about $3 billion of the stolen money, meaning the hackers can see it but cannot actually spend it.

Two big US agencies, the FBI and the Secret Service, are now investigating what happened. They want to find out exactly who did it and whether any more money can be recovered.

This isn't the first time something like this has happened, but it is now the largest. It has made a lot of people wonder whether money kept on these digital exchanges can ever truly be kept safe from thieves with a lot of skill and backing, like those who may work for a government.

Hackers Stole $6.3 Billion From the World's Biggest Crypto Exchange in 20 Minutes

Sometime between October 29 and 30, 2026, hackers broke into Binance, the largest cryptocurrency exchange on the planet, and walked away with about $6.3 billion in roughly twenty minutes. It is the biggest crypto theft ever recorded, beating the previous record set just the year before. Security firms and Binance itself say the evidence points to a hacking group linked to the North Korean government. The FBI and the US Secret Service are now investigating.

Why this mattersIf you or your family ever buy or hold cryptocurrency, this is the risk sitting underneath the whole system: even the biggest, most trusted platform can lose billions in minutes to hackers who may be working for a government. It also shows how cybercrime has become a tool of international politics — money stolen from an exchange can end up funding a state on the other side of the world.
Read the whole story

A cryptocurrency exchange works like a bank crossed with a stock trading app: people deposit their digital coins there, trade them, and trust the exchange to keep the keys — the digital passwords that control the money — safe. Binance is the biggest exchange in the world, handling trades for tens of millions of users. For hackers, that makes it an extraordinarily rich target, because instead of breaking into one person's wallet, breaking into the exchange's systems can mean access to the holdings of millions of people at once.

What makes this theft remarkable is not just the size but the speed. Every cryptocurrency transaction is recorded permanently on a blockchain — a shared, public ledger copied across thousands of computers around the world, designed so no one can quietly erase or alter a transaction after it happens. That transparency usually makes stolen crypto easier to trace than stolen cash, but it does nothing to stop the theft itself. In this case, whoever got in had already worked out exactly how to move the money before they struck, and they drained accounts faster than most people take to make a coffee.

Blockchain-intelligence firms — companies that specialize in following the trail of transactions across these public ledgers — say the pattern of the attack matches techniques used before by a hacking group tied to North Korea's government. North Korean state-linked hackers have been blamed for a string of major crypto thefts over the past several years, and intelligence agencies and researchers believe the country uses stolen cryptocurrency to fund its government, including its weapons programs, partly to get around international sanctions that block it from the normal financial system. Binance has backed this assessment, though it is worth noting that attributing a hack to a specific government is extremely difficult to prove beyond doubt — it relies on matching digital fingerprints and past behavior rather than catching anyone in the act.

Not all of the money got away. Partner firms working with Binance managed to freeze around $3 billion of the stolen funds before it could be fully laundered — though one widely cited report puts the frozen amount at closer to $2.54 billion, a gap that shows how quickly moving, hard-to-verify numbers get reported differently across outlets in the chaos right after a hack like this. Freezing crypto generally means getting other exchanges, where the stolen coins eventually need to land to be converted into usable money, to block those specific coins from being cashed out — a kind of digital dragnet that depends on cooperation across companies and countries.

The FBI and the Secret Service have both opened formal investigations, which signals the US government takes this seriously as both a financial crime and, potentially, a national security matter. But recovering the rest of the money, let alone catching anyone, is a long shot if a hostile state really is behind it — there is no extradition treaty that is going to hand over North Korean hackers.

For an industry that has spent years trying to convince regulators and ordinary savers that it is mature enough to be trusted with real money, a $6.3 billion heist in twenty minutes is a hard story to spin. It raises the same question that followed last year's record-breaking theft: if the single biggest, best-funded exchange in the world can be robbed this fast and this cleanly, what does safety even mean in an industry built on the promise of security through math?

Words worth knowing
  • cryptocurrency exchange — A company's website or app where people buy, sell, and store digital currencies like Bitcoin, similar to how a bank holds and moves regular money.
  • blockchain — A shared, public record of every cryptocurrency transaction, copied across thousands of computers so that no single person can secretly change or erase it.
  • state-linked hacking group — A team of hackers believed to work for, or with the approval of, a national government, often to steal money or information that benefits that country.

China's Leader Shakes Up His Top Team Before Seeking a Fourth Term

China's ruling Communist Party held a big meeting in Beijing last week. Leaders kicked 33 senior officials out of the party for corruption, and promoted a top general to their most powerful decision-making group for the first time in over 30 years outside a normal schedule. The meeting also set the date for 2027's huge party gathering, where Xi Jinping is expected to ask to stay in charge for a fourth time.

Why this mattersImagine if your school had one rule-making club, and the same person had been in charge of it for 14 years with no plan for who comes next. That uncertainty matters for a country of 1.4 billion people, because decisions made by China's leaders affect things you might use every day, like phones, toys, and even how much some foods cost.
Read the whole story

China is run by one political party, the Communist Party. It has about 100 million members, which is like everyone in a country the size of Germany belonging to one single club.

Every so often, the party's most senior leaders meet to make big decisions. This meeting is called a 'plenum', and the one last week was the fifth since the last five-year planning cycle began.

At this plenum, 33 high-ranking officials were thrown out for corruption, which means using their power to take money or favors unfairly. A general named Zhang Shengmin, who investigates corruption in the army, was promoted into the Politburo, the small group of about 24 people who make China's biggest decisions.

The party also announced that its next huge congress will happen in 2027. Xi Jinping, who has led China since 2012, is expected to ask to lead the country for a fourth term. No one knows who might lead China after him, and nobody is allowed to say so publicly.

China's Military Corruption Chief Gets a Rare Promotion as Xi Eyes a Fourth Term

China's Communist Party wrapped up a major closed-door meeting in Beijing on Friday, expelling 33 senior officials and promoting the military's top corruption investigator to its inner circle of power. The move, unusual enough that it hasn't happened outside a regular five-yearly congress in more than 30 years, comes as Xi Jinping prepares to seek a fourth term as party leader in 2027. No successor to Xi has emerged, leaving one of the world's biggest open questions about who runs China next entirely unanswered.

Why this mattersChina is the world's second-largest economy and a central player in global trade, technology competition, and tensions over Taiwan, so who controls its military and government affects decisions that reach supply chains, phones, and international conflicts far beyond its borders. A system with no clear succession plan for its most powerful country carries risks — around instability, miscalculation, or abrupt change — that can ripple outward in ways ordinary people elsewhere end up feeling.
Read the whole story

China is run by the Communist Party, and the party's real decisions get made by a small, closed group called the Politburo — about two dozen people who sit above the government itself. Several times a year, the party holds a bigger meeting called a plenum, where hundreds of senior officials gather to approve decisions already settled in private. This week's plenum, the fifth since the last party congress, ended Friday in Beijing having confirmed something already reported — the expulsion of 33 senior party members — and added something new: General Zhang Shengmin, the military's chief corruption investigator, was promoted straight into the Politburo.

That promotion matters more than it sounds. Membership in the Politburo is normally decided only once every five years, at a national congress, when the whole party apparatus turns over together. Bloomberg notes this is the first time anyone has been added to it outside that five-year cycle in more than three decades. Doing it now, mid-cycle, signals that Xi wants loyal, trusted people with him in the room without waiting for the next scheduled reshuffle — and Zhang's specific job, catching corrupt officers inside the military, tells you which institution Xi is most focused on controlling.

The expulsions and the promotion are two sides of the same tool: Xi's long-running anti-corruption campaign, which has removed tens of thousands of officials since he took power in 2012. Supporters say it has genuinely cleaned up a system where bribery and favor-trading were routine. Critics, including many outside analysts, say it has doubled as a way to remove rivals and enforce personal loyalty — since the party controls the investigations, it can choose whose corruption gets punished and whose doesn't.

The timing lands just before a bigger decision. The plenum also set the date for the next national congress in 2027, the meeting where China's top leadership formally changes hands — or doesn't. Xi is widely expected to use it to claim a fourth term as party leader, something no leader since Mao Zedong has done, because China abolished term limits on its presidency in 2018. Yet despite reshuffling the Politburo and rewarding a military loyalist, Xi has still not signaled who, if anyone, might eventually succeed him — a gap analysts describe as one of the most consequential unanswered questions in Chinese politics.

Put together, the picture is of a leader managing power through discipline and personnel rather than through any public contest or vote. There is no election where rivals campaign for Xi's job; there is only the party's internal machinery, which Xi has spent over a decade reshaping so that the people inside it owe their positions to him. Whether that makes China's system more stable or more fragile — because so much depends on one person, with no clear plan for what happens after him — is exactly what outside observers are now arguing about.

  1. Is it better for a country to concentrate power in one strong, long-serving leader, or to spread it out and limit how long anyone can hold it?

    • If the campaign genuinely reduces bribery and misconduct, the motive behind individual cases may matter less than the overall result.

    Abuse of power

    • When the same authority investigates, prosecutes, and judges, there's no independent check to ensure accusations are fair rather than convenient.
    • Using corruption charges to remove rivals erodes trust in the law itself, since punishment becomes about loyalty rather than guilt.
    • A campaign that quietly reshapes who holds power, without public debate or a vote, denies ordinary citizens any say in the outcome.
