More People Say They Don't Like How Trump Is Doing His Job

A new survey asked Americans if they like the job President Trump is doing. Most people said no. This comes right before a big election where Americans pick who sits in Congress, the group that helps make the country's laws.

Why this mattersWhen many people disagree with a leader, it can change who wins the next election and who makes decisions for the whole country. It is a bit like how if enough kids in a class disagree with a class rule, the teacher might have to change it, or get voted out as class president next time.

Imagine your class voted on whether your teacher was doing a good job. If only 33 out of 100 kids said yes, that would be a big deal. That is basically what happened with a poll about President Trump, done by a newspaper called the Washington Post.

A poll is when you ask a big group of people a question and use their answers to guess what everyone thinks. This poll asked Americans if they approve of how Trump is leading the country. Only about a third said yes. Two-thirds said no, and over half of those said they really disagree with him.

Two-Thirds of Americans Disapprove of Trump, New Polls Show, as Midterm Elections Near

A new Washington Post poll finds just 33% of Americans approve of how President Trump is handling his job, while 66% disapprove — more than half of them strongly. Other major pollsters, using different methods, land in similar territory. The numbers are landing two weeks before the November 3 midterm elections, when every seat in the House of Representatives and a third of the Senate is up for grabs.

Why this mattersMidterm elections decide who controls Congress for the next two years, which shapes what laws can actually pass — on everything from the economy to student debt to healthcare — regardless of who's president. Learning to read a poll properly, including why two pollsters can disagree and both be right, is a basic tool for understanding any news about elections you'll encounter for the rest of your life.
Read the whole story

Thirty-three percent approve, sixty-six percent disapprove, and more than half of that disapproving group say they feel it strongly rather than mildly. Those are the headline numbers from a new Washington Post poll of President Trump, and they land at an awkward moment for his party: two weeks before the November 3 midterm elections, when control of Congress is on the line.

The Post isn't alone. A Fox News poll — run by a network usually seen as friendly to Trump, which makes this particular result notable — puts his approval at 36%, close enough to count as the same story told with a different sample of people. An NBC News poll, meanwhile, finds a broader mood of anxiety and pessimism among Americans about how he's handling the economy and other issues, not just a number but a temperature reading on how people feel about the direction of the country.

Why does one poll say 33% and another say 36%? This is normal, not a sign that someone got it wrong. Pollsters call a group of a few hundred or a thousand people and use statistics to estimate what the whole country thinks, and every such estimate carries a margin of error — a built-in range of uncertainty, usually a few percentage points — because you're never actually asking everyone. Different pollsters also choose their samples differently: who they call, how they weight the results by age or location, which days they call on. Two honest, careful polls can disagree by a few points and both be doing their job correctly. What matters more than the exact number is that independent pollsters, with different methods and different owners, are all pointing the same direction.

That direction shows up in another number worth understanding: the generic congressional ballot. This is a poll question that doesn't ask about any specific candidate by name — it asks people whether they'd vote for "the Democratic candidate" or "the Republican candidate" in their district if the election were held today. It's a rough but useful gauge of which party might end up controlling Congress nationally, and right now Democrats are gaining ground on it, a structural signal that worries Republican strategists more than any single approval poll does, because it connects directly to how many seats each party might win.

On the ground, that national mood is colliding with specific, hard-fought races. In Texas, a closely watched Senate contest between Republican Ken Paxton and Democrat James Talarico is drawing rallies from both parties as early voting opens, the kind of race where a few points of national swing can decide who actually wins a seat. Races like this are where approval ratings and generic ballot numbers either translate into real seats changing hands, or don't — which is exactly why both parties are spending money and energy on the ground rather than just watching the polls.

Words worth knowing
  • Approval rating — The percentage of people in a poll who say they think a leader is doing a good job, tracked regularly to show how public opinion is shifting.
  • Generic congressional ballot — A poll question asking voters whether they'd pick "the Democrat" or "the Republican" in their district without naming anyone, used to estimate which party might win control of Congress overall.
  • Margin of error — The built-in range of uncertainty in any poll, since it questions a sample of people rather than everyone, which is why two careful polls can report slightly different numbers and both be correct.

  1. Should a president with an approval rating this low be seen as having lost the public's backing for his agenda, or can a president still govern with full authority regardless of poll numbers?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Low approval means lost backing

    • A president's authority ultimately rests on public consent, and when two-thirds of the country disapproves, that consent looks thin.
    • Sustained disapproval, especially the 'strongly' kind, tends to translate into real election losses that limit what a president can actually do next.
    • Ignoring sustained unpopularity treats elections as a one-time mandate rather than an ongoing relationship with voters.

    Elected authority doesn't expire between elections

    • The president was elected for a fixed term specifically so that governing wouldn't depend on constantly shifting day-to-day popularity.
    • Approval ratings move for all sorts of reasons — short-term news cycles, economic jitters — that don't necessarily reflect lasting judgment on policy.
    • Many past presidents governed through low approval periods and were later judged well, suggesting polls are a weak guide to legitimacy in the moment.

  2. Is it responsible for news outlets to prominently report presidential approval polls right before an election, or does that risk shaping the very outcome it's measuring?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Voters deserve this information

    • Polling is public data about public opinion, and withholding it would leave voters less informed, not more neutral.
    • Transparency about where the country stands helps people decide how urgently to vote and what's at stake.
    • Suppressing poll coverage would hand an advantage to whichever side benefits from voters staying in the dark.

    Heavy poll coverage can distort the vote

    • Research suggests some voters are swayed by a 'bandwagon' effect, joining what looks like the winning side, or by a sense that their vote won't matter if the race looks decided.
    • Constant poll coverage can crowd out reporting on actual policies and records, turning elections into a horse race story.
    • Given how often polls differ from each other and sometimes miss the final result, treating them as settled fact before votes are even counted can mislead rather than inform.

China's Economy Grows Faster Than Expected — But Not Everywone's Winning

China, the country where more people live than anywhere else on Earth, said its economy grew 4.5% from July to September. That's a little faster than experts thought it would, and better than the slower growth from the months before. Much of that growth came from selling robots and computer chips used for artificial intelligence to other countries. On the very same day, China's government announced new rules that will make it harder to sell drones and factory machines abroad, starting in November.

Why this mattersA lot of the electronics, toys, and gadgets your family owns were probably made in China, so how its economy is doing affects what things cost and how easy they are to get. When a country uses trade rules as a pressure tool, it can also affect prices and supplies far beyond its own borders, including things that end up in stores near you.

Imagine your allowance grew a little more than you expected one month. That's a bit like what happened to China's economy. It grew by 4.5% compared to a year earlier — a small but real jump from the 4.3% growth reported just three months before.

A big reason for that jump was exports — things China makes and sells to other countries. Lots of those exports were computer chips and machines used for artificial intelligence, the technology that powers things like smart assistants and self-driving cars. Countries all over the world are buying more of this AI equipment, and China is one of the biggest sellers.

But not everything inside China is growing. Chinese businesses are spending less money building new factories and buying new equipment for themselves — something called fixed-asset investment. When businesses slow down spending on themselves, it can mean they're less confident about the future, even while money is coming in from selling abroad.

On the same day the growth numbers came out, China's government announced new export controls — rules that will make it harder for China to sell drones and industrial machines to other countries starting November 18. Governments sometimes use rules like this as a kind of pressure tool in disagreements with other countries, especially the United States.

China's Economy Grew Faster Than Expected — But the Good News Has a Catch

China's economy grew 4.5% in the third quarter compared with a year earlier, beating what analysts had predicted and improving on the 4.3% growth recorded the quarter before — the slowest pace in three years. Almost all of that extra growth came from a surge in exports tied to artificial intelligence technology. On the very same day the figures were released, China's government announced new restrictions on exporting drones and industrial machinery, a move that takes effect next month and raises the stakes in its ongoing trade disputes.

Why this mattersChips and components made in China for AI data centers end up inside products and services used worldwide, including things your family might use every day, so a boom or slump in that trade ripples outward. And when a major economy starts using export controls as a political weapon, it can mean higher prices or supply shortages for ordinary buyers far from the dispute itself, the same way sanctions or tariffs eventually show up in store prices.
Read the whole story

China's National Bureau of Statistics, the government agency that compiles the country's official economic data, reported that gross domestic product — the total value of everything produced in the country over a given period, usually shortened to GDP — rose 4.5% in the third quarter compared with the same three months last year. That beat forecasts from financial analysts, who had expected the slowdown from earlier in the year to continue. Two outlets, CNBC and Al Jazeera, reported the figures independently, both drawing on the same official release.

The main engine behind the better-than-expected number was exports connected to artificial intelligence: chips, components, and equipment that other countries are buying up as they race to build their own AI infrastructure. Chinese factories and tech firms that supply this global boom have been shipping product at a pace strong enough to lift the entire economy's growth figure on its own, even while other parts of the economy struggled.

Those other parts tell a less encouraging story. Fixed-asset investment — spending by businesses and local governments on things like factories, equipment, and infrastructure that are meant to pay off over years, not months — kept falling inside China. This matters because it's one of the clearest signals of whether a country's own businesses believe the future looks good enough to justify spending now. When exports are strong but domestic investment is weak, it suggests the growth is coming from selling to the rest of the world, not from confidence at home.

On the same day the GDP figures came out, China's Ministry of Commerce announced it would impose new export controls — government limits on what companies are allowed to sell abroad — on drones and industrial machinery, starting November 18. This is part of a pattern: Beijing has increasingly used control over exports of goods the world depends on, from rare-earth minerals to now drones and machinery, as a bargaining chip in trade negotiations, particularly with the United States. Restricting what leaves the country can hurt foreign buyers who have no easy substitute, giving China leverage even as its own factories and ports keep moving.

Put together, the day's news told two stories at once. One is a China that's outperforming expectations, powered by its position at the center of the global AI supply chain. The other is a China where businesses closer to home are pulling back, and where the government is leaning harder on trade restrictions as a tool of pressure rather than relying purely on open competition. Economists are divided over which story will matter more for China's future — whether export strength can eventually translate into renewed domestic confidence, or whether weak investment at home is the more reliable signal of where the economy is actually headed.

Words worth knowing
  • GDP (Gross Domestic Product) — The total value of all goods and services a country produces in a given period, used as the main scorecard for how fast an economy is growing or shrinking.
  • Fixed-asset investment — Money businesses or governments spend on long-term things like factories, machinery, and infrastructure, which economists watch as a sign of confidence in the future.
  • Export controls — Government rules that limit or ban selling certain goods to other countries, often used to protect national security or to gain leverage in trade disputes.

  1. Is it fair for a government to use export controls, like China's new limits on drones and machinery, as a bargaining tool in trade disputes?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes, it's legitimate

    • Every country has the sovereign right to decide what crosses its own borders and under what conditions.
    • It's a nonviolent way to pursue national interests, far less costly than military conflict or outright sanctions on people.
    • It can protect strategic industries and national security concerns that a country believes matter more than open trade.

    • It invites retaliation, which can spiral into broader trade wars that leave everyone worse off, including the country that started it.

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    It counts as real strength

    • Exports reflect genuine global demand for what a country produces, which is real economic value, not a trick of the numbers.
    • Profits from strong exports can eventually flow into domestic investment, so today's export boom may fuel tomorrow's confidence at home.
    • Focusing on what you're best at selling to the world, rather than forcing growth everywhere at once, is standard economic strategy, not a flaw.

    It's a warning sign

    • Export-driven growth depends on decisions made by other countries and can vanish quickly if trade policies or global demand shift.
    • Weak domestic investment shows that a country's own businesses lack confidence in their own future, which matters more for growth that lasts.
    • An economy too reliant on exports is exposed to shocks — like tariffs or a slowdown abroad — that a more balanced economy could absorb.

Even After the Fighting Stopped, Food Is Still Scarce in Gaza

A year ago, Israel and a group called Hamas agreed to stop fighting in a place called Gaza. But the leader of the World Food Programme, a part of the United Nations that helps feed hungry people, says things there are still 'absolutely brutal.' About 1.4 million people don't have enough food to eat.

Why this mattersImagine agreeing to stop a fight, but still going to bed hungry every night afterward. This story shows that just stopping a war doesn't automatically fix everything that war breaks — people still need food, homes, and help getting back on their feet. It also shows how much daily life depends on organizations having enough money to do their jobs.
Read the whole story

Gaza is a small, crowded strip of land by the sea. For a long time, Israel and a group called Hamas were fighting there, and many people had to leave their homes.

About a year ago, the fighting stopped. The United States helped arrange this pause, called a ceasefire. Everyone hoped that once the fighting ended, food and medicine could reach people easily again.

But the head of the World Food Programme says that hasn't happened. Around 1.4 million people — picture every single person in a big city — still don't have enough to eat each day.

Aid groups that bring in food say they're running out of money. That means they're being forced to give out less, even though people need just as much help as before.

  • Ceasefire — An agreement between two fighting groups to stop shooting or attacking each other, at least for a while.
  • Humanitarian aid — Food, water, medicine, and other help given to people who are suffering, usually after a war or disaster.

A Ceasefire Stopped the Bombs in Gaza. It Didn't Stop the Hunger.

A year after the United States brokered a ceasefire between Israel and Hamas, the head of the UN's World Food Programme says conditions in Gaza are 'absolutely brutal.' About 1.4 million people are facing acute food shortages, and aid groups say funding shortfalls are forcing them to cut back the very programs meant to prevent starvation.

Why this mattersNews often treats a ceasefire or peace deal as the end of a story, but this shows how the hardest part — actually feeding and rebuilding a population — can drag on long after the cameras move elsewhere. It's a reminder to ask, about any 'resolved' conflict you hear about, resolved for whom, and in what way.
Read the whole story

When a ceasefire is announced, the assumption most people make is that the worst is over. Guns go quiet, and the story moves off the front page. But the head of the UN World Food Programme (WFP), the agency responsible for getting food to places where people can't get enough of it themselves, is saying that in Gaza, a year after a US-brokered ceasefire paused the fighting between Israel and Hamas, roughly 1.4 million people still don't have reliable access to enough food. 'Absolutely brutal' is how she described it — not a cautious diplomatic phrase, but a direct description of daily life.

The ceasefire itself was supposed to be the turning point. Brokered with heavy US involvement, it came paired with an initiative called the Board of Peace — a US-backed plan meant to oversee reconstruction and a longer-term stabilization of Gaza once the shooting stopped. The logic was straightforward: stop the fighting, then rebuild the systems — hospitals, water supplies, food distribution — that war had destroyed. A year later, the fighting has mostly stopped. The rebuilding has not caught up.

Part of the reason is money, and it's a mundane reason compared to the violence that dominated headlines before the ceasefire. Aid organizations like the WFP depend on donations from governments and institutions, and those donations have fallen short of what's needed. When funding gaps open up, aid groups don't just do a little less of everything — they're forced to make triage decisions, cutting specific programs even as the underlying need stays the same or grows. A ceasefire can stop bombs falling on a warehouse; it can't by itself refill a budget.

How you describe this situation depends partly on who's telling the story, and that's worth noticing rather than ignoring. Al Jazeera, a news outlet funded by the government of Qatar, has covered the ceasefire and the Board of Peace skeptically, emphasizing how little has actually improved for Palestinians despite the political fanfare around the deal. That skepticism isn't automatically wrong — government funding doesn't mean a story is false — but it does mean the coverage is shaped by a particular viewpoint, one that tends to be critical of US and Israeli framing. Reading it alongside reporting from NPR and the Washington Post, which approach the story with different institutional interests and have generally portrayed the ceasefire's humanitarian results more cautiously rather than as an outright failure, gives a fuller picture than either account alone.

The deeper point the story reveals is a distinction that's easy to miss: a ceasefire is an agreement to stop fighting, not a guarantee that civilians' basic needs get met. Those are two different problems with two different solutions — one is political and military, the other is logistical and financial — and solving the first doesn't automatically solve the second. For the people in Gaza living through it, that distinction isn't abstract. It's the difference between a headline calling the war 'over' and a kitchen with nothing in it.

Words worth knowing
  • Ceasefire — An agreement between fighting sides to stop military action, which doesn't necessarily resolve the underlying conflict or its humanitarian consequences.
  • Acute food insecurity — A severe, often sudden lack of reliable access to enough food to stay healthy, distinct from longer-term chronic hunger.
  • Board of Peace — A US-backed initiative set up alongside the Gaza ceasefire to oversee reconstruction and longer-term stabilization in the territory.

  1. If a ceasefire stops the fighting but people are still hungry, has it really succeeded?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes, it succeeded

    • Stopping people from being shot at or bombed is itself a huge win, even if problems remain.
    • Fixing hunger and rebuilding takes much longer than stopping a war, so it's unfair to judge the ceasefire by that alone.
    • Without the ceasefire, things could be even worse — people could be facing bombs and hunger at the same time.

    No, it hasn't fully succeeded

    • If people still can't get enough food, the main point of peace — a better life — hasn't been achieved yet.
    • A ceasefire that doesn't lead to real help can leave people suffering in a different way, just slower.
    • People judging success should look at whether daily life actually improved, not just whether guns went quiet.

    • Governments can bring in more money or resources that aid groups alone cannot.
    • Big decisions affecting a million or more people should involve accountable leaders, not just one organization.

  1. Should wealthy countries be expected to keep funding humanitarian aid at full levels even after a ceasefire is declared and a conflict is seen as politically resolved?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Yes, the obligation continues

    • The human needs that caused the crisis — destroyed infrastructure, lost livelihoods — don't disappear the moment fighting stops, so funding shouldn't either.
    • Cutting aid right after a ceasefire can undermine the very peace it's meant to support, since desperation and instability often follow unmet basic needs.
    • Wealthy nations that helped broker or enabled the conditions of a conflict have some responsibility to help fund the cleanup, not just the headline deal.

    No, funding has to compete with other priorities

    • Governments have limited budgets and many competing crises worldwide; no single situation can claim indefinite funding regardless of other needs.
    • A ceasefire is meant to shift responsibility toward local governance and reconstruction bodies rather than keep outside donors permanently on the hook.
    • Donor fatigue is a real political constraint — taxpayers and legislatures in donor countries have their own limits on what they'll sustain long-term.

  2. Should a ceasefire be judged a success mainly by whether the fighting has stopped, or by whether civilians' living conditions have actually improved?

    Stuck? Two ways to argue it

    Judge it by civilian conditions

    • The whole point of ending a war is usually to reduce human suffering, so a ceasefire that leaves people hungry hasn't achieved its real purpose.
    • Measuring success only by absence of violence lets political leaders claim victory while ignoring ongoing humanitarian collapse.
    • Long-term stability is unlikely if basic needs remain unmet, since unresolved suffering can fuel future conflict.

    Judge it narrowly by whether fighting has stopped

    • Ending active violence is an enormous and difficult achievement on its own, and conflating it with reconstruction sets an unrealistic bar that no ceasefire could meet quickly.
    • Humanitarian recovery depends on many separate actors — aid agencies, donor governments, local institutions — whose failures shouldn't be blamed on the ceasefire agreement itself.
    • If ceasefires are only called 'successful' once full recovery happens, negotiators may lose the incentive to pursue achievable, incremental peace deals at all.
