World Leaders Argued for Two Weeks — Then Walked Away With No Deal

Almost 200 countries met for over two weeks to agree on how to fight climate change. They could not agree on one thing: whether to write the words 'coal, oil, and gas' into their final plan. In the end, countries walked out, and no deal was signed at all.

Why this mattersThe weather you grow up with — hotter summers, bigger storms, less snow some winters — is shaped by decisions like this one. When countries can't agree on fighting climate change together, it can take longer to slow down the warming that affects everyone's future, including yours.
Read the whole story

Every year, nearly 200 countries — almost every country on the map — meet to talk about climate change, the warming of our planet. This year's meeting was called COP31, held in a city named Antalya in Turkey. It lasted more than two weeks, longer than any of these meetings in history.

The big argument was about words. Countries in the European Union, plus small island nations like Tuvalu and the Maldives, wanted the agreement to clearly name coal, oil, and gas as the main cause of global warming. Island nations care deeply because rising seas could one day cover parts of their homes.

Other countries, especially ones that make lots of money selling oil and gas, did not want those exact words written down. When the talks could not settle this, the EU and island nations walked out rather than sign a weaker deal.

No final agreement was reached. This is the first time a meeting like this has broken down completely since 2009 — sixteen years ago, before most kids today were even born.

The World's Biggest Climate Summit Just Fell Apart

After more than two weeks of talks in Antalya, Turkey, the United Nations climate summit known as COP31 collapsed on Sunday without a deal. Nearly 200 countries walked away with no new global agreement on fighting climate change — only the second time that has happened since these summits began in the 1990s. The fight that broke it came down to a single question: should the final document say, in plain words, that burning coal, oil and gas is causing global warming?

Why this mattersThe arguments in Antalya were about more than wording — they were about whether the world keeps building its energy, jobs, and future industries around coal, oil, and gas, or shifts toward alternatives, and how fast. That decision shapes the climate you will be living in as an adult, the kinds of jobs that exist in energy and manufacturing, and whether promises made by governments today translate into anything binding tomorrow.
Read the whole story

Every year, almost every country on Earth sends negotiators to a UN summit called a COP — short for 'Conference of the Parties' — to hash out a shared response to climate change. This year's version, held in the Turkish coastal city of Antalya, ran for more than two weeks, making it the longest COP ever held. It ended the way nobody wanted: with no agreement at all. The last time a COP collapsed this badly was in Copenhagen in 2009, a failure so notorious it is still used as the textbook example of what happens when these talks go wrong.

The breaking point was wording, which sounds small until you understand what it actually controls. The European Union and a group of small island nations — countries like Fiji and the Maldives, which face losing land to rising seas — refused to accept a final text that did not explicitly name fossil fuels as the cause of global warming. Oil- and gas-producing countries, whose economies depend on selling those fuels, pushed back against including that language at all. When neither side would budge, the EU and the island states walked out rather than sign a watered-down version.

Why fight so hard over a sentence? Because the wording of a COP agreement becomes a reference point for years afterward — cited in national laws, used as leverage in court cases, quoted by activists and by oil companies alike. A text that avoids saying fossil fuels caused the crisis gives cover to any government that wants to keep expanding coal, oil, and gas production while still claiming to take climate change seriously. A text that names them directly makes that much harder to do without looking like you are ignoring the agreed cause of the problem.

UN climate summits work by consensus, meaning every single country attending has to agree before anything becomes official — there is no outvoting a reluctant minority. That rule exists to make sure small or poor nations cannot simply be overruled by powerful ones, but it also means a handful of determined holdouts can block a deal entirely, which is effectively what happened here.

The practical result is that there is no updated international plan for moving the world away from fossil fuels heading into 2027, when the next major summit is expected. Earlier commitments made at previous COPs still technically stand, but the push to strengthen them has stalled for at least another year. For a problem that scientists say is getting worse every year it goes unaddressed, a year lost to a stalemate over language is not a neutral outcome — it is a year without new pressure on the countries and companies producing the most emissions.

This is only the second time in the history of these summits that talks have broken down this completely, and it raises an uncomfortable question that will follow the process into its next round: if a single bloc can block any deal it dislikes, and that has now happened twice, is consensus-based diplomacy still capable of handling a crisis that does not wait for everyone to agree?

Words worth knowing

  • Consensus — The rule that every single country attending a UN climate summit must agree before any text becomes official, meaning one determined holdout can block the whole deal.
  • Bloc — A group of countries that negotiate together because they share a common position, such as small island nations facing rising seas.

Computer-Brain Companies Have a Very Wobbly Week on the Stock Market

Last week, a group of American tech stocks called the Nasdaq dropped about 10 percent in just five days. That's the worst week for these stocks since March 2020, when the whole world was shutting down because of COVID-19. The drop came right after a company called Anthropic, which builds AI chatbots, sold pieces of itself to the public for the first time ever, in the biggest sale of its kind in history.

Why this mattersLots of grown-ups have money saved up for the future in things called retirement funds, and a chunk of that money is often invested in tech stocks like these. When stock prices fall a lot, it can mean that saved-up money shrinks too, even for people who never bought a single share themselves. It also matters because AI chatbots and tools are becoming part of schools, jobs, and apps you might already use, so how healthy those companies are can shape what gets built next.
Read the whole story

Imagine a company is like a giant pizza. An 'IPO' is when the company cuts itself into slices and sells those slices to anyone who wants to own a piece. Anthropic did this last week. People paid so much for their slices that the whole pizza was suddenly worth about $1.8 trillion. That is more money than most countries make in a whole year.

At first, people got excited and the price of Anthropic's slices jumped up fast. But then investors, the people who buy and sell these slices, started worrying that AI companies might be worth way more on paper than they really are. So lots of people tried to sell their slices at the same time. That pushed prices down hard, not just for Anthropic but for tech stocks everywhere.

The wobble jumped across the ocean too. Stocks linked to AI companies in China lost a combined $1.7 trillion in value since the end of June. Picture every single toy, book, and video game owned by every kid in a huge country, and then imagine all of that just vanishing in value. That's roughly the size of the number we're talking about.

Nobody agrees on what this means. Some experts think this is the start of an 'AI bubble' popping, where excitement over AI pushed prices too high and now reality is catching up. Others think it's just a scary but temporary dip, since companies are still spending huge amounts of money building AI computers and data centers for the future.

Words worth knowing
  • IPO — IPO stands for 'initial public offering' — it's the very first time a company sells small pieces of itself, called shares, so that anyone can own a tiny bit of it.
  • bubble — In money talk, a 'bubble' is when the price of something shoots up because of excitement rather than its real value, and people worry it could pop and crash back down, like a soap bubble bursting.

Tech Stocks Just Had Their Worst Week Since the Pandemic Crashed Markets

The Nasdaq, the stock exchange where most big tech and AI companies list their shares, fell about 10% in a single week — its steepest drop since markets panicked at the start of COVID-19 in March 2020. The slide came right after Anthropic, an AI company, pulled off the biggest stock market debut in history, only to watch its share price nearly give back all its gains within days. The shakiness spread to China too, where AI-related stocks have lost a combined $1.7 trillion in value since the summer.

Why this mattersIf you have a pension, a parent with a retirement account, or even just a phone made by a company whose stock is tied up in this, swings like this week's eventually touch ordinary people, not just traders. A genuine AI bubble bursting could mean job losses at tech companies and a chillier climate for the AI tools now showing up in schools, workplaces, and apps you already use.
Read the whole story

For the past two years, AI companies have been treated by investors almost like a sure thing: pour money in, and the returns will follow, because AI is going to reshape every industry. That belief pushed stock prices for AI-linked companies to levels that assume years of near-perfect growth. This past week, a lot of investors suddenly asked themselves whether that assumption still holds — and started selling.

The clearest symbol of the mood swing is Anthropic, the company behind the Claude AI chatbot. It had just completed the largest initial public offering (IPO) ever recorded — an IPO is when a private company sells shares to the public for the first time, turning itself into a company anyone can buy stock in. Anthropic raised more than $81 billion this way, and briefly carried a total value of around $1.8 trillion, overtaking SpaceX as the most valuable company to go public. Its stock jumped 17% on day one. But by the end of the week it had slumped more than 8%, landing back close to where it started — a sign that the initial excitement had outrun what buyers were actually willing to pay once they thought it over.

That wobble didn't stay contained to one company. The Nasdaq, which is the exchange where most major tech and AI firms are listed, dropped roughly 10% over the week — a fall of a size not seen since the early days of the pandemic, when the entire global economy seemed to be shutting down at once. This time there's no virus, no lockdown, no obvious external shock. The drop was investors themselves getting nervous about something they'd built: years of enormous spending on AI infrastructure — the data centres, computer chips, and power supplies needed to run AI systems — on a bet that the profits to match will eventually arrive.

The nervousness crossed the Pacific. Three major Chinese stock indexes focused on technology have lost a combined $1.7 trillion in value since the end of June, according to data reported by Bloomberg. That is a reminder that this isn't just an American story: investors worldwide had piled into AI-related stocks on both sides of the US-China rivalry, and both sides are now feeling the same chill.

Where analysts split is on what this actually means. Some think this is the start of the AI bubble finally deflating — a correction where prices fall back toward what the underlying businesses are actually worth, the same way the dot-com boom of the late 1990s eventually gave way to a crash once investors realised many internet companies weren't profitable and might never be. Others argue this is just a sharp, temporary wobble inside a boom that still has years to run, pointing out that the heavy spending on AI infrastructure is real and ongoing, not speculative hype divorced from anything being built. Nobody disagrees that a huge amount of money is riding on AI living up to its promise — the disagreement is about timing and whether the promise is overstated.

Words worth knowing

  • IPO (initial public offering) — The first time a company sells shares to the public, turning from a private business into one that anyone can buy a piece of on the stock market.
  • Valuation — The total worth investors place on a company, calculated by multiplying its share price by the number of shares that exist.

A Student Who Studies Brains Gets Stopped at the Airport

Eva Yi Xie studies how brains work at Princeton University. She had just given a talk at a science conference and was flying home when immigration officers stopped her at the airport. Now she is locked in a detention centre, even though she has been waiting ten years for the government to decide if she can stay in the US.

Why this mattersImagine waiting ten years for someone to answer a very important question about your life — and then getting stopped and locked up before you even get the answer. Eva's case shows how messy and slow some rules can be, and how that slowness can hurt real people who are just trying to study, work, and build a life.

Eva Xie came to the United States when she was 14 years old. Her family asked for 'asylum,' which means asking a country for protection because it isn't safe to go home. That was ten years ago, and the government still hasn't given a final answer.

While she waited, Eva grew up, went to university, and became a PhD student studying neuroscience, which is the science of how brains work. She was flying back from a conference where scientists share new discoveries when officers from ICE, a government agency that enforces immigration rules, stopped her at Reagan Airport near Washington, D.C.

A Princeton PhD Student Was Detained at the Airport After Flying Home From a Conference — Here's Why Her Case Is Making News

Eva Yi Xie, a Princeton University neuroscience PhD student who came to the U.S. from China at age 14, was detained by immigration agents the moment she landed back in Washington, D.C., after presenting her research at an academic conference. She has had an asylum application — a request for legal protection because she fears returning to her home country — pending for a decade. The government says she was in the country illegally; her lawyer says she had every right to be there while her case was reviewed.

Why this mattersIf you know anyone studying at a university — a cousin, a family friend, a future version of yourself — this is the system they'd be depending on if they came from another country: one where a decade-long wait for a decision can end with a sudden detention at an airport. It's also a reminder that laws can be read two different ways by the people meant to enforce them and the people meant to follow them, and that the gap between those readings can cost someone their freedom while it gets sorted out.
Read the whole story

Eva Yi Xie had just flown back from an academic conference, the kind of trip PhD students take constantly to present their research to other scientists. Instead of heading home, she was stopped at Reagan National Airport in Washington, D.C., by officers from Immigration and Customs Enforcement, usually called ICE — the federal agency that finds and detains people the government believes are in the country without legal permission. She is now being held at the Caroline Detention Facility, an immigration jail in Bowling Green, Virginia.

Xie's situation turns on a single disputed fact: whether she had the right to be in the U.S. at all. She arrived with her family as a teenager and applied for asylum, a legal process that lets someone stay in the country while the government decides whether they qualify for protection from danger in their home country. Her case has been sitting unresolved for ten years — not an unusual wait, since the U.S. asylum system has a backlog of well over a million cases. Her attorney says that during all that time, an asylum application gave her lawful permission to live, work, and study in the country, including traveling to conferences and coming home again.

The Department of Homeland Security, which oversees ICE, tells a different story. A spokesperson said Xie was in the U.S. illegally and that she was picked up as part of a 'targeted immigration enforcement operation' — language the government uses for planned actions against specific people, rather than random checks. That phrase matters: it suggests officials already had her name and address before she landed, not that she was swept up by chance. Neither side has published the paperwork that would settle exactly what her legal status was at the moment of arrest.

The case has become a flashpoint for a larger argument already underway in the United States: how aggressively immigration law should be enforced against people connected to universities, including international students and researchers who form a large share of graduate programs in fields like neuroscience. Some of this debate is about enforcement tactics — arresting someone returning from a work conference, rather than at a routine check-in, reads to critics as deliberately disruptive. Supporters of stricter enforcement counter that a pending application is not the same as a guaranteed right to stay, and that the government is entitled to act once it decides someone's status has lapsed or was never valid.

The story has also traveled widely in Chinese-language and Hong Kong media, where it's being read alongside other recent cases involving Chinese nationals in the U.S. — a pattern that commentators there link to rising political tension between Washington and Beijing over trade, technology, and security. American outlets have focused more narrowly on what it means for university life, where a growing number of foreign-born researchers now wonder whether leaving the country for work, even briefly, carries a new kind of risk.

Words worth knowing
  • Asylum — Legal protection a country can grant to someone who shows they would be in danger if sent back to their home country.
  • ICE (Immigration and Customs Enforcement) — The U.S. federal agency responsible for finding, arresting, and deporting people believed to be in the country without legal permission.
  • Detention facility — A jail-like building where people are held by immigration authorities while their legal status or case is being decided.
