China says it is talking with the United States and Russia about a meeting between their top leaders. Trump, Xi and Putin Might All Sit Down Together — China Confirms Talks for a First-Ever Summit

China's government has confirmed, for the first time, that it is talking with the United States and Russia about a meeting between all three of their leaders. If it happens, Xi Jinping, Donald Trump and Vladimir Putin would gather in Shenzhen around November 18-19, on the edges of a bigger economic summit. No leader has ever had all three of them in the same room at once, and the main topic looming over any such meeting would be the war in Ukraine.

Why this mattersA meeting like this would be about more than photographs — it could shape whether the Ukraine war moves toward some kind of settlement, and it would say something about whether the world's major decisions increasingly get made by three men in a room rather than through alliances, treaties, or international bodies. Even if it never happens, the fact that it's being discussed shows how the US, China and Russia are each trying to define their own power relative to the other two.
Read the whole story

On November 6, a spokesperson for China's foreign ministry, Mao Ning, told reporters that Beijing 'is in communication with relevant parties regarding a meeting between the three heads of state.' That's a carefully worded sentence — it confirms talks are happening, but not that anything is agreed. Diplomats often speak this way on purpose: it lets them acknowledge reality without promising an outcome they can't guarantee.

The idea isn't brand new. Russian officials had said back in October that Moscow first floated the proposal. What changed on November 6 is that China itself — the country that would actually host the meeting — said publicly that talks are real. That matters because China had stayed quiet on the idea for weeks, and its confirmation is the first time anyone outside Russia has backed up the story.

The setting under discussion is APEC, short for Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation — an annual summit where leaders from around twenty Pacific Rim economies, including the US and China, meet to talk trade and economic policy. Big political meetings often get arranged 'on the sidelines' of events like this, because it lets leaders meet without the enormous formal buildup of a dedicated summit, and without as much obligation to produce a joint statement.

Why would these three leaders want to be photographed together at all? Each has very different reasons. Putin's forces have been fighting in Ukraine for over three and a half years, and a meeting involving both Trump and Xi could be a path toward pressure on Kyiv, international legitimacy for Moscow, or both. Trump has repeatedly said he wants to end the war quickly, and a face-to-face with both other men would let him claim he's driving toward that. For Xi, hosting the meeting on Chinese soil would be a visible reminder that China, not just the US, has the convening power to bring the world's biggest players to one table.

Nothing is locked in. China's statement only confirms that discussions are happening — not a date, an agenda, or even certainty that it will take place at all. Summits like this can fall apart over details as basic as who sits where, what gets said publicly about Ukraine, and whether any of the three leaders decides the political cost of attending outweighs the benefit.

Words worth knowing
  • APEC (Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation) — An annual summit of around twenty economies around the Pacific Ocean, including the US and China, originally focused on trade and economic cooperation.
  • On the sidelines — A diplomatic phrase meaning a separate, often more private meeting held alongside a bigger official event, rather than as its own formal summit.
  • Foreign ministry spokesperson — An official who speaks publicly on behalf of a country's government about its relations with other countries, often using carefully chosen wording.

America Added 165,000 Jobs in Just One Month

On Friday, the U.S. government announced that the country added 165,000 new jobs in October. That's way more than experts expected. It's a sign that even with all the talk about a slowing economy, companies are still hiring.

Why this mattersWhen more people have jobs, more families have money to spend on things like groceries, sneakers, and birthday presents. The decision the Federal Reserve makes in December could change how much it costs your family, or a family you know, to borrow money for a car or a home.

Every month, a government office called the Bureau of Labor Statistics counts how many new jobs got created across the whole country. In October, they counted 165,000 new jobs. That's about as many people as could fill three large sports stadiums, all getting hired in a single month.

Many of these jobs were office jobs, hospital jobs, and factory jobs. A chunk of them came from building and running giant computer warehouses called data centers, which power artificial intelligence, or AI. AI is computer software that can do things like answer questions or recognize pictures, almost like a very fast, very literal helper.

The unemployment rate, which tells you how many people who want jobs can't find one, dropped slightly to 4.1 percent. Picture 100 people all looking for work. About 4 of them still haven't found a job. A few months ago, that number was a touch higher.

People who study money closely are paying extra attention to this report. That's because a group called the Federal Reserve meets in December to decide whether to change interest rates, which affect how expensive it is to borrow money for things like a car or a house.

U.S. Job Market Keeps Surprising Economists — This Time With Help From AI

The U.S. government reported on Friday that employers added 165,000 jobs in October, far more than economists had predicted. The unemployment rate dipped slightly to 4.1 percent. The numbers now sit at the center of a debate over what the Federal Reserve, the institution that sets U.S. interest rates, should do next.

Why this mattersInterest rate decisions sound distant, but they shape what a car loan, a credit card balance, or a future mortgage actually costs a family. A strong jobs report also hints at where new careers are opening up — right now, that includes the people who build and run the physical infrastructure behind AI, not just the programmers who design it.
Read the whole story

Every month, a government agency called the Bureau of Labor Statistics surveys businesses and households across the country and publishes a report on how many jobs were added or lost. Economists study this number closely because it's one of the clearest signs of how the broader economy is doing — not a poll or an estimate, but a count drawn from actual payroll records. For October, the number came in at 165,000 new jobs, well above what most forecasters expected going in.

The unemployment rate — the share of people actively looking for work who can't find any — ticked down to 4.1 percent. That's a historically low number. For comparison, during the worst of the 2008 financial crisis, unemployment climbed above 10 percent, meaning roughly 1 in 10 job-seekers couldn't find work. A rate near 4 percent generally means most people who want a job can get one.

What stood out in this report was where the jobs came from. Along with the usual gains in health care, there was a notable jump in white-collar work and manufacturing, and some of those new jobs were directly tied to the construction and operation of AI data centers — the massive warehouses full of computer servers that power chatbots, image generators, and other AI tools. Building one of these facilities requires electricians, engineers, and construction crews; running one requires technicians to keep the servers cool and online. That means the AI boom is now visibly showing up in ordinary employment numbers, not just in tech company stock prices.

This report matters beyond the headline number because of what it signals for the Federal Reserve, the U.S. central bank, which meets in December to decide whether to raise, lower, or hold steady the interest rate that affects borrowing costs across the entire economy — mortgages, car loans, credit cards, and business loans. The Fed has two competing goals: keep prices from rising too fast (inflation) and keep the job market healthy. When the job market looks this strong, some officials worry that cutting interest rates now could pour fuel on an economy that doesn't need the help, pushing prices up further. Others argue a strong jobs report doesn't rule out a rate cut, since inflation and employment don't always move in lockstep.

There's no dispute about the raw numbers themselves — they come straight from a government agency with no obvious reason to shade them one way or another. The disagreement is entirely about interpretation: does a hot jobs report, built partly on AI construction spending, mean the economy is broadly healthy, or does it mean growth is unusually dependent on one fast-moving industry that could cool off just as quickly as it heated up?

Words worth knowing
  • Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) — The U.S. government agency that collects and publishes official data on jobs, unemployment, and prices.
  • Unemployment rate — The percentage of people actively looking for work who have not found a job.
  • Federal Reserve — The U.S. central bank, which sets interest rates to try to balance economic growth against rising prices.

Tech Companies Lose a Giant Pile of Money in One Week

This week, the value of famous tech companies like Nvidia, Microsoft, and Tesla dropped suddenly and sharply. Together, they lost about $3 trillion. That is such a huge number that if you counted to 3 trillion out loud, one number every second, it would take you about 95,000 years. The drop happened right after an election changed who controls part of the U.S. government, and people are still arguing about why it really happened.

Why this mattersLots of grown-ups have money saved for the future in things connected to the stock market, including retirement savings that pay for life after work. When tech companies lose value quickly, those savings can shrink too, even for families who never bought a share themselves. It also shows how political decisions, like who wins an election, can ripple out and affect jobs and money far beyond Washington.
Read the whole story

Companies like Nvidia and Microsoft make computer chips and software that power artificial intelligence, or AI. AI is technology that can learn, answer questions, or even drive a car. People can buy tiny pieces of these companies, called shares, hoping the company grows and their piece becomes worth more.

This week, something made people nervous, and many tried to sell their shares at once. Nvidia dropped 9.3 percent. AMD dropped 17.3 percent. That is like having a jar of 100 marbles and losing 17 of them in just a few days. Companies like Tesla and Micron also lost big chunks of their value.

The nervousness started after an election on Tuesday. A different political party took control of part of the government. Some people who study the stock market think new leaders might make stricter rules for AI and tech companies, which could slow down how fast they grow and earn money.

By Friday, some of the prices bounced back up a little, though not all the way. Experts do not agree on what really caused the drop. Some think it is really about new rules coming. Others think AI companies had grown so valuable, so fast, that a drop was bound to happen eventually, rules or no rules.

AI Stocks Lose $3 Trillion in a Week as Investors Get Nervous About Washington

The biggest names in artificial intelligence just had one of their roughest weeks in years. Nvidia, AMD, Microsoft, and other tech giants watched billions of dollars evaporate from their stock prices after a US election result that left investors guessing about what comes next. By the end of the week, the damage added up to roughly $3 trillion — gone, at least on paper.

Why this mattersIf you have a retirement account, a college savings fund, or even a part-time job at a company that uses AI tools, swings like this ripple outward — pension funds, index funds, and everyday savings accounts often hold these same stocks. It's also a reminder that the AI boom shaping your phone, your schoolwork, and your future job market isn't just a technology story — it's tangled up with politics and the choices voters make.
Read the whole story

On Tuesday, voters delivered a sweep for Democrats in the US midterm elections, the vote held partway through a president's term that decides who controls Congress. The results meant Democrats would likely hold more power to shape laws — including laws about how AI companies operate. Within days, some of the most valuable tech companies in the world saw their stock prices fall sharply: Nvidia dropped 9.3 percent, AMD fell a steep 17.3 percent, Microsoft slipped 3.6 percent, Micron plunged 17.5 percent, Broadcom lost 10.4 percent, and even Tesla and SpaceX — companies not purely about AI chips, but closely tied to the tech boom — fell 10.8 and 9.1 percent respectively.

To understand why an election result would hit stock prices this hard, it helps to know what these companies have in common: they are all central to the AI boom, either by building the chips that power AI systems (Nvidia, AMD, Micron, Broadcom) or by investing heavily in AI themselves (Microsoft, Tesla). Investors had bet enormous sums that this boom would keep growing largely unchecked. A Congress more willing to regulate — meaning pass laws restricting how companies can operate, what data they can use, or how much power they can hold — introduces uncertainty. Markets generally dislike uncertainty, because it makes it harder to predict a company's future profits.

The selling intensified on Thursday, November 5, before Friday brought a partial recovery: the S&P 500 and Nasdaq, two major indexes that track large groups of US stocks, both bounced back somewhat, even though the Dow Jones — an older, narrower index — kept falling. Still, taken together, it marked the fourth straight week that the US stock market overall had lost value, the longest such stretch in some time.

Analysts — people whose job is to study markets and explain what's driving them — are split on how to read this. Some argue the midterm results are a real turning point: a Democrat-controlled Congress could push through rules limiting how AI companies collect data, concentrate power, or avoid taxes, and investors are simply pricing in that new risk. Others argue the political story is mostly an excuse. In this view, AI stock prices had climbed so high, so fast, over the past two years that a sharp pullback was overdue regardless of who won the election — and the midterms just gave nervous investors a convenient reason to start selling.

Nobody disputes that the numbers are real: trillions of dollars in paper wealth disappeared in days. What's contested is the meaning. A stock price isn't a fixed value stamped on a company — it's simply what buyers are willing to pay right now, and that can swing on sentiment as much as on facts. Whether this week marks the start of a longer AI slowdown or just a nervous blip in an otherwise booming industry is something only time, and the next few months of earnings reports and policy decisions, will answer.

Words worth knowing
  • Selloff — A period when many investors sell a stock or group of stocks at once, pushing the price down quickly.
  • Market value (market cap) — The total worth of a company's shares if you multiply the share price by the number of shares that exist — it rises and falls as the stock price changes.
  • Regulation — Rules, usually made by government, that restrict or guide how a company or industry is allowed to operate.
